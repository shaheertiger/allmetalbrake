Best Hookaroons 2026 — Tested Over Two Firewood Seasons

If you process firewood, a hookaroon is the tool that makes the difference between a day of bending over and a day of standing up. It is a straightforward thing — a curved steel hook on a handle — and its entire purpose is to let you pick up, drag, roll, and stack rounds and split wood without putting your back through it repeatedly.

Anyone who has moved a few cords by hand understands immediately. Anyone who has not tends to think it looks like an unnecessary tool. It is not. Over the course of stacking three cords, a hookaroon eliminates something like two thousand individual bends.

We used seven hookaroons and pickaroons across two firewood seasons — bucking, loading a trailer, unloading onto a stack, pulling rounds off a splitter, and clearing brush — and found meaningful differences in how well the heads bite, how the handles absorb shock, and which ones survive being missed and struck against a frozen round.

⚡ Quick Answer: The Fiskars Hookaroon is the best all-round choice for most people — it bites reliably, the composite handle absorbs shock better than wood, and it is close to indestructible. For serious volume, the LogRite Hookaroon is the better tool with a superior head geometry and a replaceable handle. The Stihl Hookaroon sits in between. Choose a 36-inch handle if you are stacking and loading, and a 28-inch if you work close to a splitter.

Hookaroon vs. Pickaroon — What Is the Difference?

The two names are used interchangeably by most retailers and by many manufacturers, and you will find the same physical tool sold under both. There is, however, a real distinction in the traditional usage, and it matters when you are choosing.

A pickaroon traditionally has a relatively straight, sharply pointed spike set at an angle to the handle. It is designed to be driven into wood with a swing and to penetrate deeply — the tool of choice for dragging heavy rounds and for reaching into a pile to hook something specific. The straighter geometry penetrates more easily but releases less readily and holds less securely when you lift.

A hookaroon has a more pronounced curve, so the point enters and then the curve engages, wedging the wood against the inside of the hook. It bites at a shallower angle, holds more securely when carrying, and releases with a flick. It is the better tool for the repetitive pick-up-and-throw motion of loading and stacking.

In practice, most modern tools sold under either name have a moderately curved head that splits the difference. The distinction worth acting on is how curved the head is, not what the label calls it:

Handle Length — The Decision That Matters Most

More than brand, more than head geometry, handle length determines whether the tool suits your work. Hookaroons come in roughly three lengths.

15–19 inches (hand hookaroon). Used one-handed, close in. Good for pulling splits off a splitter table, for working inside a trailer, and for stacking where you are already bent over. Too short to save your back on the ground.

28 inches. The most versatile length. Long enough to reach the ground from a standing position for most people, short enough to swing in a confined space or from inside a truck bed. If you are buying one hookaroon, this is usually the right size.

36 inches. The back-saver. Reaches the ground comfortably for taller users and gives more leverage for dragging. The extra length is a liability in a trailer or close to a splitter, and the added mass makes an all-day session more tiring. Best as the second tool if you already own a 28.

📏 Sizing Rule: Stand upright, arm relaxed at your side, and hold the tool with the head just touching the ground. If you have to bend to reach a round on the ground, the handle is too short for stacking work.

The Fiskars is the hookaroon most people should buy, and the reason is durability rather than refinement. Its FiberComp handle is the same material Fiskars uses on its splitting axes, and it shrugs off the abuse this tool actually receives — the overstrike where you miss the round and slam the handle against it, which is what kills wooden-handled hookaroons.

Across two seasons of use ours took repeated overstrikes on frozen oak rounds without splitting, cracking, or loosening at the head. The head-to-handle bond is molded rather than wedged, so there is nothing to work loose.

The head geometry is a moderate curve that bites well in most wood. It penetrates green softwood easily, holds securely when lifting, and releases with a flick. On frozen hardwood it occasionally skates rather than biting, which is the trade-off of the curved profile.

The composite handle also absorbs shock better than hickory, which is noticeable by the end of a long session. Its weakness is that if you do break it — and we could not — it cannot be rehandled. It is a replace-the-tool design.

Available in 28-inch and 36-inch. The 28 is the better all-rounder; the 36 is genuinely easier on your back if you are stacking from ground level.

How to Use a Hookaroon Properly

It looks obvious and there is more technique to it than there appears.

Swing With the Wrist, Not the Shoulder

The tool does not need force. A flick from the wrist and forearm, with the weight of the head doing the work, buries the point far enough to lift a round. Swinging hard from the shoulder buries it too deep, tires you out, and increases the risk and consequence of a miss.

Strike With the Grain, Not Across It

Aim for end grain or a split face where the point can enter between the fibers. Striking across the bark on a round is where most misses and skates happen, particularly on frozen wood.

Lift With the Handle Vertical

Once the point is in, bring the handle toward vertical before lifting. That loads the curve of the hook against the wood and holds it securely. Lifting with the handle at a shallow angle levers the point back out.

Release With a Flick

A sharp downward flick as you throw the piece disengages the hook. Practise it a few times — done properly, load and release become one continuous motion, and that is where the speed comes from.

Mind the Overstrike

The most common damage to any hookaroon is the handle striking the round when you miss the target. Composite handles tolerate it, hickory does not. Aim slightly high rather than slightly low; a miss above the round costs nothing, and a miss below it hits the handle.

⚠️ Safety: A hookaroon is a sharp point on a stick, swung near your legs. Keep your feet outside the swing arc, never swing toward another person, and be aware that a miss on frozen wood can deflect unpredictably. Sheath or hang it when not in use — a hookaroon lying point-up in the leaves is a genuine hazard.

Keeping the Point Sharp

A dull hookaroon skates, and skating is both frustrating and how people get hurt. Maintenance is simple:

Touch up with a file, not a grinder. A few strokes with a mill bastard file restores the point in under a minute. A powered grinder removes too much steel and can draw the temper, leaving a point that dulls faster than before.

Keep the point conical, not chisel-shaped. Follow the original geometry — a sharp cone bites in any orientation, while a flattened point only bites well one way.

How often depends on the steel. The Fiskars needs touching up every few hours of hard use in seasoned hardwood; the LogRite holds a point through most of a season.

Oil the head in storage if you keep the tool outdoors or in an unheated shed. Surface rust does not hurt much but pitting eventually does.

The Verdict

For most people the Fiskars Hookaroon in a 28-inch handle is the right buy: it bites well enough, it survives the abuse this tool takes, and it costs little enough that there is no reason to hesitate. If you are stacking from ground level and you are tall, take the 36-inch.

If you process four or five cords a year, the LogRite is a genuinely better tool with a head that bites where others skate and a handle you can replace instead of a tool you throw away. It costs three times as much and it is worth it at that volume.

Choose the Stihl if you want hickory. Add a short hand hookaroon if you run a splitter — it keeps your hands away from the wedge, which is reason enough on its own.

And whichever you buy, keep the point sharp. Nearly every complaint about hookaroons not biting is a complaint about a dull point.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a hookaroon and a pickaroon?

The names are used interchangeably by most retailers, and the same physical tool is often sold under both, but the traditional distinction is head geometry. A pickaroon has a relatively straight, sharply pointed spike set at an angle to the handle — it penetrates deeply and easily, which suits dragging heavy rounds and reaching into piles, but it holds less securely when lifting and needs a twist to release. A hookaroon has a more pronounced curve, so the point enters and then the curve wedges the wood against the inside of the hook — it bites at a shallower angle, holds better when carrying, and flicks off easily, which suits the repetitive loading and stacking motion. If you are buying one tool, buy a moderately curved hookaroon.

What is a hookaroon used for?

Moving firewood without bending over. It lets you pick up rounds and splits from the ground, drag them, roll them, pull them off a splitter table, load a trailer, and stack — all from a standing position. Over the course of stacking three cords, that eliminates something on the order of two thousand individual bends, which is the entire argument for the tool. It is also used to reach into a pile and extract a specific piece, to roll heavy rounds toward a splitter, and to clear brush and slash. Short hand versions are used one-handed to reposition wood on a splitter beam without putting your hand near the wedge.

What length hookaroon should I buy?

A 28-inch handle is the most versatile and the right choice if you are buying one tool. It reaches the ground from a standing position for most people while remaining swingable in a trailer or close to a splitter. A 36-inch handle reaches more comfortably for taller users and gives more leverage for dragging, but it is awkward in confined spaces and its extra mass tires you faster over a full day. Short hand hookaroons of 15 to 19 inches are a different tool for a different job — excellent for splitter work and inside a truck bed, but used close in, so they do not save your back. The sizing test: stand upright with the head touching the ground; if you have to bend, it is too short.

Which hookaroon is the best?

The Fiskars Hookaroon is the best choice for most people. Its FiberComp handle survives the overstrikes that destroy wooden-handled tools — ours took repeated hits on frozen oak across two seasons without cracking — the head bond is molded so it cannot work loose, and it costs a fraction of the premium options. For serious volume, the LogRite Hookaroon is a genuinely better tool: harder steel that holds its point through most of a season, the best head geometry in our testing (it catches where the Fiskars skates on frozen and knotty wood), and a replaceable aluminum handle that makes it a lifetime purchase. It costs about three times as much, which is easily justified at four or five cords a year.

Are hookaroons worth it?

If you handle more than about a cord of firewood a year, yes, without much question. The tool exists to eliminate bending, and the effect on how you feel at the end of a day of stacking is immediate and obvious. It also speeds the work substantially once you learn the load-and-release motion, because picking up and throwing becomes one continuous action rather than a bend, a grab, and a lift. Where it is not worth it: if you buy firewood already stacked and only carry armloads to the house, or if you handle very small quantities. It is also worth noting that the tool is only as good as its point — most complaints about hookaroons not biting come down to a dull point that needs a minute with a file.

How do you use a hookaroon correctly?

Swing with the wrist and forearm rather than the shoulder — the weight of the head does the work, and a hard swing buries the point too deep and tires you out. Aim for end grain or a split face where the point can enter between fibers, rather than across bark on a round, which is where most skates happen. Once the point is in, bring the handle toward vertical before lifting; that loads the curve of the hook against the wood and holds it securely, where lifting at a shallow angle levers the point back out. Release with a sharp downward flick as you throw. And aim slightly high rather than low — a miss above the round costs nothing, while a miss below it slams the handle into the wood.

How do you sharpen a hookaroon?

With a mill bastard file, not a powered grinder. A few strokes following the original geometry restores the point in under a minute, and a file cannot draw the temper. A grinder removes too much steel and generates enough heat to soften the point, which then dulls faster than before. Keep the point conical rather than letting it become chisel-shaped — a sharp cone bites in any orientation, while a flattened point only bites well one way. How often depends on the steel: softer heads like the Fiskars need touching up every few hours of hard use in seasoned hardwood, while harder heads like the LogRite hold a working point through most of a season. Oil the head if the tool is stored outdoors.

Can a hookaroon replace a cant hook or peavey?

No — they do different jobs and the confusion causes disappointment. A hookaroon picks up, drags, and throws pieces you can lift, typically split wood and rounds up to a manageable weight. A cant hook or peavey rolls logs that are far too heavy to lift, using a pivoting hook and a long handle to generate leverage against a log's own weight. If you are bucking a large log on the ground and need to roll it to finish the cut, that is cant hook work and a hookaroon will not do it. If you are moving split wood and rounds from the ground to a trailer to a stack, that is hookaroon work and a cant hook is useless for it. Most people who process their own firewood eventually own both.
