A power station for camping is a different purchase from one for the house. At home the question is how long you can keep a refrigerator alive until the grid comes back. At a campsite there is no grid coming back. The battery you leave the driveway with, plus whatever you can put back from the car and the sun, is all the electricity you will have until you get home. That makes camping energy a budgeting exercise: what you spend each day has to be matched by what you carry or what you can earn.

The good news is that campsite loads are small. A 12 V compressor cooler, LED lights, phones, a headlamp, a camera and a speaker rarely add up to more than a few hundred watt-hours a day. The bad news is that the few big items people bring — a CPAP with a heated humidifier, an electric kettle, an e-bike charger, a drone fleet — can each wipe out a small battery in one go. This guide shows how to add those numbers up, which outputs waste energy and which save it, how much you can realistically refill from a car socket or a folding panel, and then names six widely sold units from EcoFlow, Jackery, Anker SOLIX and Bluetti that fit different trips, from a two-night tent weekend to a week of boondocking in an RV. If you are shopping for outage backup as well, our main portable power station guide covers the full range, including the large wheeled home units.

⛺ The short answer: For tent camping and day trips, a roughly 300 Wh LFP unit such as the EcoFlow River 3 Plus covers lights, phones, cameras and a CPAP on DC, and with a 100–200 W panel it can keep a small 12 V cooler going too. For car camping with a cooler for several days, or overlanding, step up to a 1 kWh class unit — the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 is the lightest, the EcoFlow Delta 3 Plus the most expandable. For RVs and vans, a 2 kWh unit like the Jackery Explorer 2000 v2 runs a CPAP, fans, a TV and a coffee maker without a generator.

Build a Campsite Load Budget

Before choosing a size, write down everything that will draw power on a typical day, how much power it draws, and for how long. The camping version of this list is different from the home version: no refrigerators or sump pumps, but a lot of small rechargeable batteries and one or two loads that run all day and all night.

Three items dominate most budgets. The cooler is the big one, because it runs around the clock; on a hot day in the sun with the lid opening every half hour it can approach the top of that range, and in a shaded spot at night it may barely run. The CPAP is next, and the heated humidifier is the deciding factor: with it switched on, nightly use can climb to several times the dry figure, so most campers switch it off or use a heated-humidifier-free travel setting. The third is anything you are tempted to bring from home that heats water or air. A kettle, a hair dryer and a space heater can each use more energy in fifteen minutes than your phones use in a week.

From List to Battery Size

Add up the daily figures, then adjust for how each device is powered. Loads on the AC outlets lose roughly 15% to the inverter, so use the familiar rule — usable energy ≈ capacity in Wh × 0.85 — and divide by the load to estimate runtime. Loads on DC outputs lose much less. Multiply the daily total by the number of days you will go without recharging, then subtract whatever you can reliably put back from driving and solar. What is left is the capacity to buy, plus a margin of 20% or so for cloudy days and the odd extra charge.

💡 A quick sanity check: if your daily budget is under 200 Wh, a 300 Wh unit lasts a weekend with no recharging. If it is 300–700 Wh because of a cooler, a 300 Wh unit needs daily solar or driving to keep up, and a 1 kWh unit gives you one to three days of slack. If it is over 1,000 Wh, you are in RV territory and should look at 2 kWh with solar.

DC vs. AC: The Free 10–15%

Every power station stores energy as direct current. To feed a household outlet, the inverter has to convert that DC into 120 V alternating current, and that conversion costs energy as heat. Then, for many devices, a charger brick converts the AC straight back to low-voltage DC to charge a battery — losing energy a second time. Running devices from the DC outputs skips both steps.

DeviceWasteful pathEfficient path
12 V coolerCooler's AC adapter in an AC outletCooler's 12 V plug in the car socket output
CPAPStandard AC power brickManufacturer's 12 V DC power cord
LaptopLaptop brick in an AC outletUSB-C cable direct (if the laptop charges over USB-C)
Phones, headlamps, camerasWall chargers in AC outletsUSB-A or USB-C ports
Drone batteriesAC charging hubMaker's car charger in the 12 V socket

The saving is typically 10–15% of whatever those devices use, which on a cooler-heavy trip can be an extra half day of runtime. There is a second, less obvious saving: the inverter draws power just by being switched on, even with nothing plugged in. Over a 24-hour day that idle draw adds up to a meaningful slice of a 300 Wh battery. The habit worth building is simple — run everything you can from DC and keep the AC button off unless something actually needs it.

For CPAP users this matters twice. A CPAP on its DC cord without the humidifier is one of the most efficient overnight loads you can run, and many CPAP makers sell the cord as an accessory. Check your machine's manual for the correct adapter and voltage before relying on it, and never assume a medical device will tolerate a power interruption — test your setup at home for a full night before you depend on it in the backcountry.

Recharging on the Road and Trail

On a multi-day trip, the charging plan matters more than the capacity. There are three realistic sources away from hookups: the car while you drive, solar panels at camp, and campground hookups or a gas generator when available.

Car Charging While Driving

Every power station on this list can charge from a vehicle's 12 V accessory socket. The catch is the socket itself: most are fused at 10–15 amps, which limits charging to roughly 100–120 W. That is useful but slow. An hour of driving puts back around 100 Wh — enough to recover a day of phone and light use, or a few hours of cooler runtime. A 300 Wh unit refills in about three hours of driving; a 1 kWh unit needs closer to ten.

That makes the 12 V socket a top-up, not a primary source, for anything above about 500 Wh. Overlanders who drive daily and run a fridge full-time increasingly use a dedicated alternator charger wired to the starter battery, which several brands, EcoFlow among them, now sell. These deliver several hundred watts while the engine runs and can refill a 1 kWh unit in a couple of hours of driving. They need proper installation with correct cable sizes and fusing, and they should only charge while the engine is running so the starter battery is never drained. Whatever you use, do not run the power station off the car's socket with the engine off overnight — that is how you end up with a dead starter battery at a trailhead.

Solar on the Trail

Folding panels are the camper's free fuel. The honest numbers: a portable panel delivers about 60–75% of its rated wattage when well aimed at midday, and a good summer day provides around four to five peak sun hours. Here is what that means for common panel sizes:

Campsites are rarely ideal solar sites. Tree cover can cut output by half or more, and forest camping in the shade is exactly where people want to pitch a tent in summer. Place the panel in the sun on a long extension lead and leave the power station in the shade — batteries prefer cool, and panels prefer light. Re-aim it two or three times a day to follow the sun, and keep it clear of dust and pollen. In spring and fall the sun is lower and days are shorter, so budget toward the bottom of each range. Hookups and Generators

If a site has power, use it — a wall charger refills these units in roughly one to two hours. Where generators are allowed, a small inverter generator can refill a large power station in the window the campground permits, and the battery covers quiet hours. Our generator guide explains how to size one if that is part of your plan; just remember that gasoline generators must stay outdoors, well away from tents and RV windows, because of carbon monoxide.

Weight per Watt-Hour

A power station's weight scales with its battery, and LFP cells — though safer and longer-lived than the older NMC chemistry — store less energy per pound. That is why even the lightest 1 kWh units weigh more than 20 pounds. But weight does not scale perfectly, and the differences in the table above have real consequences.

Small units carry a weight penalty for their case, inverter, screen and ports, which is why the 286–288 Wh units sit around 29–32 Wh per pound while the Explorer 2000 v2 reaches about 51. Put another way, two River 3 Plus units would weigh about 20 pounds for 572 Wh, while one Explorer 1000 v2 gives you nearly twice that energy in a slightly heavier single box. If your daily budget clearly calls for 1 kWh, buy one 1 kWh unit rather than stacking small ones.

The calculation flips when you have to carry it. A walk-in tent site, a beach a quarter mile from the lot, or a canoe portage changes the value of every pound. At those sites a 10-pound unit plus a 6- to 10-pound folding panel beats a 24-pound battery, because the panel keeps producing every sunny day while the battery only empties. For truly ultralight trips — backpacking, bikepacking, paddling — skip power stations altogether and carry a pocket power bank with a small solar charger. Rules for flying are also strict: lithium batteries above 160 Wh are generally not allowed on passenger aircraft at all, so every unit here travels by road.

Quiet Hours and Generator Bans

One of the best reasons to choose a battery over a portable generator for camping is that the campground may not let you run the generator. Many public campgrounds set generator hours — often a block in the morning and another in the evening — and enforce quiet hours overnight. Some loops, tent-only areas and many backcountry or walk-in sites ban generators entirely. Federal parks commonly reference a noise limit of about 60 decibels measured at 50 feet, which even many quiet inverter generators struggle to meet under load. Rules vary widely by park, so check the specific campground's regulations before you go.

A power station sidesteps all of this. Apart from a cooling fan under heavy load, it is silent, produces no exhaust, and can sit inside a tent vestibule or RV without any carbon monoxide risk. You can run a CPAP all night in a generator-free loop, make coffee at 6 a.m. without waking the neighbors, and keep lights on long after generator hours end. That is also why a growing number of RV owners use a battery overnight and run a generator — where allowed — only during the permitted daytime window to recharge it.

Cold Nights and Hot Cars

Cold. Lithium batteries should not be charged below freezing, and most power stations block charging at around 32°F (0°C) to protect the cells. Discharging in the cold is allowed within the manufacturer's range, but usable capacity falls as temperature drops, so a unit that lasts two days in July may last noticeably less on a frosty October trip. In shoulder season, keep the power station inside the tent or the vehicle cabin overnight, off the cold ground, and insulate it with a jacket or sleeping bag stuff sack — with the vents clear. If a unit has been sitting in a freezing truck bed, let it warm up before plugging in a solar panel. In cold weather you will also be tempted by electric blankets and heaters; a 12 V heated blanket at modest settings is manageable on a 1 kWh unit, but a 1,500 W space heater will drain even the Explorer 2000 v2 in little more than an hour.

Heat. Summer is the bigger risk for most campers. A closed car parked in the sun gets far hotter than the outside air, easily beyond what any battery maker allows. Many units list an upper operating limit somewhere around 104–113°F (40–45°C); check your manual. High heat shortens battery life and can trigger shutdowns just when the cooler needs power most. Keep the station in shade, out of the trunk on hot days, and never charge it in direct sun — run a long cable to the panel instead. Coolers work harder in heat too, which is the other reason a summer trip needs a bigger budget than a spring one.

Rain and dust. None of these units is waterproof. Keep them off the ground, under a vestibule or awning, and away from condensation dripping off tent walls. A dry bag or plastic bin with the lid propped for ventilation works in a downpour. Dust is the enemy of cooling fans in desert camping; wipe the vents and keep the unit off sandy ground.

Three Worked Trip Examples

These examples use the daily figures from the load table and the runtime rule above. Your own numbers will differ — the point is the method.

1. The Weekend Tent Trip (Two Nights, Two People)

Version A — no electric cooler: two phones charged daily (40 Wh), string lights for four hours at 10 W (40 Wh), headlamps and a camera (30 Wh), a Bluetooth speaker (20 Wh). That is about 130 Wh a day, almost all on USB, or roughly 260 Wh for the weekend. A River 3 Plus or SOLIX C300X does it with no recharging and a little left over for the drive home.

Version B — with a 12 V cooler: add a cooler at around 400 Wh a day on the 12 V socket, and the daily total becomes about 530 Wh, or over 1,000 Wh for the weekend. Now a 300 Wh unit lasts barely half a day on its own. Three ways to make it work: a 1 kWh unit such as the Explorer 1000 v2 covers the full weekend with no charging at all; a River 3 Plus plus a 200 W panel in good sun nearly keeps pace day to day; or pre-chill the cooler at home and use block ice to cut its run time. For most car campers who bring a cooler, the 1 kWh unit is the stress-free choice.

2. The Week-Long Overland Trip (Seven Days, Two People)

The daily budget: a 12 V fridge running full time (about 500 Wh), lights (50 Wh), phones and a GPS unit (80 Wh), a laptop for photos and maps (60 Wh), and three drone batteries charged from a car charger (about 175 Wh with losses, depending on the packs). Call it roughly 865 Wh a day, or about 6 kWh over the week — far more than any portable unit carries.

So the trip runs on recharging. Three hours of daily driving on the 12 V socket returns about 300 Wh. A 200 W folding panel at camp adds roughly 500–700 Wh on a clear day. Together that is around 800–1,000 Wh a day — roughly break-even with consumption. A 1 kWh battery is then the buffer, not the fuel tank: it covers one cloudy day or a rest day without driving. That is why the Delta 3 Plus fits so well. Start with the base unit, add an extra battery for more cloudy-day insurance, or fit an alternator charger to make driving days fully replenish the battery. The Explorer 2000 v2 works too, with a two-day buffer instead of one.

3. RV Boondocking (Four Nights, No Hookups)

A couple in a small travel trailer, with the fridge running on propane: CPAP on DC for eight hours (320 Wh), a roof vent fan at 30 W for eight hours (240 Wh), LED lights (100 Wh), a TV or laptop for three hours at 60 W on AC (about 210 Wh after inverter losses), a coffee maker for ten minutes at 1,000 W (about 200 Wh with losses), phones and small devices (60 Wh). The total is about 1,130 Wh a day.

An Explorer 2000 v2 provides about 1,700 Wh of usable AC energy at the 0.85 rule (somewhat more for the DC loads), so it covers about a day and a half alone. With 400 W of portable solar returning around 1,000–1,400 Wh on a sunny day, the system roughly breaks even and the four nights work, with the trailer's own house battery handling the water pump and furnace fan. If the forecast is cloudy, cut the coffee maker in favor of a stovetop percolator and the budget drops by almost a fifth. What this setup will not do is run the rooftop air conditioner, which draws 1,300–2,000 W while running — that remains a job for shore power or a generator.

RV and Van Use

A portable power station can do a lot in an RV or camper van, but it helps to understand where it fits alongside the vehicle's own electrical system.

As a standalone power source. The simplest approach: put the station on the floor or a shelf and plug devices directly into it. This works in any rig, needs no wiring, and moves with you to the picnic table or the house. Most van-lifers and small-trailer owners start here, and many never need more.

Feeding the RV's shore-power inlet. Some larger stations include an RV-style 30 A outlet, and adapters exist for plugging the RV's shore cord into a power station. This powers the RV's 120 V outlets, but the rig's converter will also try to charge the house battery from the station, wasting energy, and anything big on the AC circuits — the air conditioner, the electric water heater element, an electric fridge mode — can overload the inverter instantly. If you go this route, switch off those loads and the converter at the breaker before connecting.

Ventilation and mounting. Secure the unit so it cannot slide or tip under braking, keep its vents clear of bedding and gear, and keep it out of the hottest corner of the vehicle. LFP chemistry is among the most stable lithium types, but any large battery deserves a secured, ventilated spot away from where you sleep with your head against it.

Size honestly. A 1 kWh unit is fine for a van with a 12 V fridge, lights and devices. Once a CPAP, a TV and a coffee maker are added, a 2 kWh unit and a few hundred watts of solar become the realistic minimum. Rooftop air conditioning, electric heat and electric cooking at length are beyond any portable station on this list. If you want those, a built-in lithium house battery with a large inverter, or a quiet generator, is the right tool.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size portable power station do I need for camping?

Size it from a daily load budget rather than a guess. Add up what each device uses per day in watt-hours, multiply by the days you will go without recharging, and subtract what you can reliably put back from driving and solar. As a rough guide, phones, lights and cameras alone need 100–200 Wh a day, which a 300 Wh unit covers for a weekend. Adding a 12 V compressor cooler raises that to roughly 400–800 Wh a day, which points to a 1 kWh unit or a small unit plus a 200 W panel. RV trips with a CPAP, fans and a coffee maker typically need about 1,000–1,300 Wh a day and suit a 2 kWh unit with solar.

How long will a power station run a 12 V camping cooler?

A 12 V compressor cooler draws roughly 40–60 W while its compressor runs, but it cycles on and off, so daily use is typically around 300–700 Wh depending on its size, the air temperature, sun exposure and how often the lid is opened. Run from the 12 V socket, which skips most inverter losses, a 286–288 Wh unit lasts somewhere between half a day and a day, a 1 kWh unit roughly two to three days, and a 2 kWh unit four days or more. Keeping the cooler shaded, pre-chilling it at home and loading it full all reduce how often the compressor runs.

Can I run a CPAP from a power station while camping?

Yes, and it is one of the most common reasons campers buy one. A CPAP without its heated humidifier typically draws 30–60 W, or about 250–500 Wh over an eight-hour night. Using the manufacturer's 12 V DC cord instead of the AC power brick avoids inverter losses and can stretch runtime by 10–15%. A 300 Wh class unit usually covers one night, and often more on DC; a 1 kWh unit covers several. Turning on the heated humidifier can multiply consumption several times over. Confirm the correct adapter with your CPAP maker, and test the full setup at home for a night before relying on it at a remote campsite.

Is a power station better than a portable generator for camping?

For most campers, yes. A battery power station is silent, produces no exhaust, can be used inside a tent or RV, needs no fuel and is allowed in campgrounds and loops that ban generators or restrict them to set hours. That makes it ideal for overnight loads like a CPAP or a cooler. A gas inverter generator still wins when you need large amounts of energy — running a rooftop RV air conditioner, or staying off-grid for weeks without reliable sun. Many RV owners combine the two, running the generator only during permitted daytime hours to recharge the battery for the quiet night.

Can I charge a power station from my car while driving?

Yes. Every unit in this guide accepts charging from a vehicle's 12 V accessory socket. Because most sockets are fused at 10–15 amps, charging is limited to roughly 100–120 W, so an hour of driving returns about 100 Wh. That refills a 300 Wh unit in around three hours, but a 1 kWh unit needs roughly ten. For faster charging, several brands sell dedicated alternator chargers that connect to the starter battery and deliver several hundred watts while the engine runs. Avoid charging from the socket with the engine off for long periods, since it can drain the starter battery and leave you stranded.

How much solar do I need for camping?

A portable panel produces roughly 60–75% of its rated wattage in good midday sun, and a clear day provides about four to five peak sun hours. That means a 100 W panel returns about 250–350 Wh a day, a 200 W panel about 500–700 Wh, and 400 W around 1,000–1,400 Wh. For devices and lights, 100 W is plenty. To keep a 12 V cooler running indefinitely alongside devices, plan on 200 W. For overlanding fridges plus laptops and drones, or for RV use, 400 W or more. Shade, low winter sun and clouds can cut these figures dramatically, so keep a battery buffer for poor days.

Can I use a power station in cold weather?

You can use one, but with care. Lithium batteries should not be charged below freezing, and most power stations block charging at around 32°F (0°C) to protect the cells. Discharging in the cold is permitted within the manufacturer's stated range, but available capacity drops as the temperature falls, so expect shorter runtimes on frosty nights. Keep the unit inside the tent or vehicle cabin overnight, raised off cold ground and wrapped loosely with its vents clear. Let a cold unit warm up before connecting a solar panel or charger. Avoid electric space heaters, which drain even large units in an hour or two.

Can a camping power station charge an e-bike?

Usually, as long as the e-bike charger's wattage is within the station's AC output and the battery is big enough. Many e-bike packs store roughly 400–750 Wh, and charging through the AC inverter and the bike's charger brick loses energy at both steps. A 286–288 Wh unit can add only a partial charge. A 1 kWh unit can typically fill one mid-size e-bike pack, but that may use most of its capacity, leaving little for the cooler and devices. If e-bikes are part of the plan, a 2 kWh unit with generous solar is more realistic, or charge the bikes at a campground hookup when you can.
