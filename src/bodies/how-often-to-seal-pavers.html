How Often Should You Seal Pavers? The Real Reseal Interval

Every three to five years is the usual answer, but the sealer chemistry, the sun and what happens on the surface move that number by years in either direction. Here is how to judge your own patio — and why sealing too often causes more trouble than sealing too late.

Most paver sealers need renewing every three to five years, but that average hides a wide range. A glossy solvent-based acrylic on a sun-baked driveway may be tired after two years; a penetrating silane/siloxane on a shaded patio can keep repelling water for five to ten. The sealer chemistry sets the baseline, and exposure — sun, traffic, pool water, winter freeze-thaw — decides where in that range your pavers land.

The better answer than any calendar is the surface itself. When water stops beading and soaks in, the colour has gone flat, and joint sand is washing out, the sealer has done its job and it is time. If none of those things is happening, resealing early does not add protection — it adds build-up, and build-up is where most sealer problems start.

⚡ Typical reseal intervals: Wet-look solvent acrylic every 2–3 years. Water-based acrylic or acrylic-urethane film every 3–5 years. Penetrating silane/siloxane (natural look) every 5–7 years, sometimes longer. Pool decks, full-sun driveways and hard-winter climates sit at the short end; shaded, lightly used patios at the long end. Reseal when water stops beading — not because a date arrived.

Reseal Interval by Sealer Type

Paver sealers fall into two families, and they wear out in completely different ways. Film-forming sealers — acrylics and acrylic-urethane hybrids, in solvent-based and water-based versions — leave a thin coating on top of the paver. That film produces the colour enhancement and sheen people buy them for, and it is also what wears: UV breaks it down, tyres and foot traffic abrade it, and once it thins the protection fades with it.

Penetrating sealers — silanes, siloxanes and blends of the two — soak into the pores of the concrete and react to form a water-repellent lining inside the material rather than on it. There is little or no film to wear off, so traffic and sunlight affect them far less, and their life is measured in the slow loss of repellency at the very surface. The trade-off is that they do almost nothing for colour; they are the natural-look option. Why Wet-Look Sealers Need Resealing Sooner

A high-gloss solvent acrylic is the thinnest-skinned, most UV-exposed coating in the category. The sheen that makes it attractive in year one is the first thing to go, and because the look is the whole point, people notice the decline earlier than they would with a matte product. The film itself may still be offering some water resistance at two years, but it no longer looks wet, and that is usually what prompts the reseal. If the dramatic look is what you want, accept a two-to-three-year cycle as part of the deal; the options in our guide to wet-look paver sealers all work on roughly that schedule.

Why Water-Based Acrylics Last a Little Longer

Water-based acrylics usually go on as a thinner, lower-gloss film. They do not darken the paver as much, but they are generally more breathable, less prone to trapping moisture, and age more gracefully — a satin finish that slowly gets more matte is far less noticeable than a gloss finish turning patchy. Most land in the three-to-four-year range on an ordinary patio. The water-based paver sealers we cover are where most homeowners should start for that reason.

Why Penetrating Sealers Go the Longest

With no meaningful film on top, there is nothing for tyres to scuff or sunlight to chalk. A good silane/siloxane keeps repelling water until the outermost fraction of a millimetre of paver surface wears or weathers away, which on a patio takes many years. The catch is that the protection is invisible, so you cannot judge it by looking — only by the water test described below. Products in our natural-look paver sealer roundup are mostly in this family.

What Shortens or Stretches the Interval

Two identical pavers sealed with the same product on the same day can need resealing years apart. These are the factors that decide it, roughly in order of how much they matter.

1. Sun Exposure

Ultraviolet light is the main enemy of film-forming sealers. It breaks down the acrylic resin, which turns chalky and dull, then thins and eventually flakes. A patio in full afternoon sun in Arizona, Texas or Florida will wear through an acrylic noticeably faster than the same patio under trees in Oregon. As a rule of thumb, knock a year off the average interval for full southern or western exposure, and add one for areas that are mostly shaded.

2. Vehicle Traffic

Driveways wear sealers far faster than patios. Turning tyres scrub the surface, hot tyres can soften some acrylics in summer, and the wheel tracks wear long before the edges of the drive. It is normal for a driveway to need a reseal a year or more before a patio sealed with the same product — and it is common to see the tyre lanes clearly matte while the rest of the drive still beads water.

3. Pool Water and Chemicals

Pool decks are a severe environment. Splash-out is constant, chlorinated water is mildly aggressive to some film sealers, and salt-water pools are worse: salt crystallises in the pores as water evaporates, and that crystallisation can spall unprotected concrete and travertine over time. A sealed pool deck typically needs attention every two to three years whatever the sealer, and the water test is worth doing every spring. Slip resistance matters here too, which is why a deck should use a low-sheen product, ideally with an anti-slip additive — see our guide to the best paver sealers for pool decks.

4. Freeze-Thaw Cycles and De-Icing Salt

In cold climates, water that soaks into a paver and then freezes expands with real force. A sealer that has lost its repellency lets more water in, so a failing sealer and a hard winter compound each other. Rock salt adds chloride damage on top. In a climate with dozens of freeze-thaw cycles a year, check your pavers every autumn before the first frost and reseal on the shorter side of the range — and if you use de-icer, calcium magnesium acetate or a sand-and-grit mix is kinder to both sealer and paver than sodium chloride.

5. Paver Material

Standard concrete pavers are the baseline for every interval in this guide. Clay brick pavers are much denser and absorb less; many never need sealing at all, and those that are sealed rarely need it more than every five years. Natural stone varies: travertine and sandstone are porous and benefit from a penetrating sealer renewed every three to five years, while dense granite or slate may only need it at much longer intervals. Whether your particular pavers need sealing in the first place is a separate question, covered in do pavers need to be sealed?

Signs Your Pavers Are Due

The calendar gives you a reminder to check; the surface tells you whether to act. These are the signs, from most to least reliable.

  • Water no longer beads. On a well-sealed paver, water sits in round drops or sheets off. When it darkens the surface immediately and soaks in within a minute, the sealer has lost its repellency. This is the single best test and the only one that works for penetrating sealers.
  • Uneven darkening after rain. If some areas darken and stay dark long after others have dried, the sealer is wearing patchily — usually in traffic lanes, downspout areas and full-sun sections first.
  • Colour fade and a chalky surface. Film sealers that enhance colour slowly lose that effect. When the pavers look as pale as unsealed ones, or a fingertip rubbed across them picks up a faint white powder, the film is breaking down.
  • Loss of sheen, or patchy sheen. A gloss or satin finish that is dull in the walkways and still shiny at the edges is wearing through. The edges do not need more sealer; the walkways do, which is why a light, even reseal coat is better than a heavy one.
  • Joint sand washing out. Sealers help bind the top of the sand in the joints. When sand starts scattering onto the surface after rain or sweeping, or joints are visibly lower than the paver edges, the binding is gone. Re-sand before resealing.
  • Weeds and ant hills in the joints. A sealed, well-filled joint resists both. Seeds germinating in the gaps or ants piling sand on the surface mean the joints are open again.
  • Stains that sink in. On a sealed surface, a spill of oil, wine or barbecue grease can usually be wiped up if caught quickly. When the same spill leaves a dark mark within minutes, the surface is absorbing again.
  • Flaking or peeling film. This is a sign of failure rather than ordinary wear, and it means the surface needs remedial work — sometimes stripping — rather than a straightforward recoat.
💡 Check, don't count. Put a reminder in your calendar every spring and do a five-minute water test on four spots: a main walkway, a sunny section, a shaded edge and, if it is a driveway, a tyre lane. If all four still bead water, you are not due — whatever year it is.

The Water Test, Step by Step

This takes a few minutes and settles the question better than any rule of thumb.

1. Pick dry pavers. Do it on a dry day, at least 24 hours after rain, so the pavers start out dry all the way through. Test in several places, because sealers rarely wear evenly.

2. Pour or sprinkle water. A cupful splashed onto each test spot is plenty. Do not use a pressure washer or a hose on jet; you want to see how the surface treats standing water.

3. Watch for one to five minutes. Water that beads up and sits on the surface, with the paver underneath staying its normal colour, means the sealer is doing its job. Water that spreads flat but still sits there means repellency is fading. Water that darkens the paver and disappears into it within a minute or two means it is time to reseal.

4. Write it down. Note which spots failed. If only the tyre lanes or the walkway from the back door are absorbing, a spot reseal of the worn areas — feathered into the rest — may be enough with some water-based and penetrating products. Film sealers usually look better with an even overall coat.

When to Seal New Pavers

New concrete pavers should not be sealed straight away. Concrete releases soluble salts as it cures, and these migrate to the surface as a white powdery bloom called efflorescence. Seal over it and the salts are trapped under the film, where they show as a cloudy white haze you cannot wash off.

The standard advice is to wait at least 60 to 90 days after installation, and many installers prefer to let a full season of weather pass. If efflorescence appears, clean it off with a dedicated efflorescence cleaner, let the pavers dry thoroughly, and then seal. Some manufacturers of penetrating sealers allow earlier application because their products breathe; read the label for the specific product.

Joint sand comes first. If the pavers were laid with polymeric sand, it needs to be fully cured — typically several days to a few weeks depending on the product and weather — before sealing over it. Our guide to using polymeric sand covers the cure, and polymeric sand pros and cons explains why it pairs well with a sealer in the first place.

The Problem With Sealing Too Often

It is tempting to think a coat every year is extra insurance. With film-forming sealers it is the reverse: each new coat adds to the thickness of what is already there, and thick acrylic films cause most of the problems people blame on sealer.

Build-Up

Acrylic sealers are designed to be applied as thin films. Stack enough coats and the film gets thick enough to behave differently — it becomes brittle, cracks with the slight movement of the pavers, and starts to peel in sheets. Once that happens, the fix is chemical stripping and starting over, which is far more work than any reseal.

White Haze and Blushing

Pavers are never perfectly dry. Moisture moves up from the base below, and a thin, breathable sealer lets it escape. A thick film traps it, and trapped moisture turns the sealer milky white — known as blushing. Hazing is also common when a sealer is applied too heavily in one go, in high humidity, or over damp pavers. With solvent-based acrylics, the haze can often be fixed by re-dissolving the film with the appropriate solvent (typically xylene) so the moisture can escape; with water-based products, the fix is usually stripping the affected area.

Slippery Surfaces

A thick, glossy film fills in the texture of the paver surface. Dry, that looks great; wet, it can be slippery underfoot — a genuine hazard on steps, around a pool and on sloped walkways. Every extra coat makes it worse. If you want a glossy finish in a wet area, an anti-slip grit additive mixed into the final coat is worth the small cost.

Penetrating sealers are more forgiving here, because there is no film to build. But once the pores are full, extra product simply sits on the surface and can leave a sticky or blotchy residue. Over-applying penetrating sealer wastes money rather than causing structural trouble.

How a Reseal Differs From the First Seal

A reseal is usually less work than the first application, but it has a couple of rules of its own. Our full guide on how to seal pavers covers the process step by step; these are the points that apply specifically to resealing.

Use a compatible product. The safest reseal uses the same product, or at least the same chemistry, as the original. Solvent-based acrylic over solvent-based acrylic bonds well, because the new solvent softens and fuses with the old film. Water-based over water-based generally works. Switching between them is where trouble starts: water-based sealer over an intact solvent-based film often fails to bond, and solvent-based sealer over a water-based film can soften and wrinkle it. If you do not know what is there, test a small hidden area first, or ask the sealer manufacturer whether a primer or stripping is required.

Clean thoroughly, but carefully. Sweep, then wash with a paver cleaner suited to the dirt — degreaser for oil, an efflorescence cleaner for white bloom, a biological cleaner for moss and algae. If you pressure wash, keep the nozzle at a low angle and a reasonable distance; a jet aimed straight at the joints blasts sand out and can erode the paver surface.

Re-sand joints before sealing. Any washed-out joints should be refilled and, if using polymeric sand, allowed to cure before the sealer goes down. Sealing over half-empty joints just locks in the problem.

Let everything dry. Pavers that look dry on top can still hold water underneath, especially after washing. Most products want at least 24 hours of dry weather after cleaning — longer in cool or humid conditions — and none should go on with rain forecast in the next 24 hours.

Apply less than you think. A reseal is a maintenance coat, not a first coat. One thin, even application is usually enough, concentrated where the wear is. The coverage rate on the label for a reseal is often higher (more square feet per gallon) than for a first application, which also brings the cost down. Maintenance Between Seals

A sealer's lifespan depends as much on how the surface is kept between coats as on the product itself. None of this is time-consuming, and it is what moves a patio from the short end of the range to the long end.

  • Sweep regularly. Grit underfoot acts like sandpaper on a film sealer. A weekly sweep in heavy-use areas, and after storms, does more for sealer life than anything else.
  • Rinse, then wash once or twice a year. A garden hose and a soft brush with a pH-neutral cleaner takes off pollen, dirt and early algae. Avoid harsh acids and strong alkaline cleaners, which attack both sealer and concrete.
  • Deal with spills quickly. Oil, grease, wine and leaf tannins stain fastest when the sealer is ageing. Blot spills, then clean with a suitable degreaser; the sooner, the better.
  • Top up joint sand. Check joints each spring. Sweep in fresh sand wherever they have dropped below the chamfer of the paver, so the edges are supported and weeds have nowhere to root.
  • Be careful with de-icers and snow removal. Use a plastic-edged shovel or a snow blower with skid shoes set high, and choose a gentler de-icer over rock salt where possible.
  • Avoid aggressive pressure washing. Use the lowest pressure that does the job, a fan tip rather than a turbo nozzle, and keep the wand moving. Strip-cleaning a sealed patio to "freshen it up" is the fastest way to wear a sealer away early.

Late spring and early autumn are the best windows for resealing in most of the US. Most sealers need air and surface temperatures above roughly 50°F during application and for the first day of cure, and few should be applied in direct midday sun on a hot day, when they can skin over before levelling out.

The Short Version

Plan on resealing every three to five years for most water-based and hybrid film sealers, every two to three years for a glossy wet-look finish, and every five years or more for a penetrating natural-look sealer. Move toward the short end for full sun, driveways, pool decks and hard winters; toward the long end for shaded, lightly used patios.

Do not reseal by the calendar alone. A spring water test on a few spots tells you more than any rule, and resealing a surface that still beads water is how build-up, haze and slippery finishes happen.

When it is time, clean thoroughly, top up the joint sand, let everything dry, and put down one thin coat of a compatible product. In between, sweep, wash gently and catch spills early — the cheapest way to stretch the interval is to look after the coat you already have.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should pavers be sealed?

Typically every three to five years, depending on the sealer and the conditions. Glossy solvent-based wet-look acrylics usually need resealing every two to three years because the UV-exposed film and the sheen wear fastest. Water-based acrylics and acrylic-urethane hybrids tend to last three to five years. Penetrating silane/siloxane sealers, which soak in rather than forming a film, often last five to seven years and sometimes longer on a shaded patio. Full sun, vehicle traffic, pool water and freeze-thaw winters all push toward the shorter end. The most reliable way to decide is a water test: if water still beads on the surface, you are not due yet.

How do I know if my pavers need resealing?

Splash a little water on several dry pavers and watch for a minute or two. If it beads up and the paver stays its normal colour, the sealer is working. If the water darkens the surface and soaks in quickly, it is time to reseal. Other signs include colour fading back to an unsealed look, a dull or patchy sheen on film sealers, joint sand washing out after rain, weeds or ant hills appearing in joints, and spills leaving stains that used to wipe off. Test in a main walkway, a sunny area and any tyre lanes, because sealers wear unevenly and those areas fail first.

Can you seal pavers too often?

Yes, and with film-forming acrylics it causes real problems. Each coat adds to the thickness of the film. A thick film becomes brittle and peels, traps moisture rising from below so it turns milky white (blushing), and fills in the paver texture so the surface becomes slippery when wet. A new coat applied over sealer that is still intact also bonds poorly and tends to peel early. Fixing heavy build-up usually means chemically stripping the sealer and starting over. Penetrating sealers are more forgiving because they do not form a film, but excess product still sits on the surface and can leave a sticky or blotchy residue.

How long should I wait to seal new pavers?

Wait at least 60 to 90 days after installation, and many installers recommend a full season. New concrete pavers release soluble salts as they cure, which appear on the surface as white efflorescence. Sealing too soon traps those salts under the film as a cloudy haze that cannot be washed off. If efflorescence appears, clean it off with an efflorescence cleaner, let the pavers dry completely, then seal. If the joints were filled with polymeric sand, make sure it has fully cured before sealing over it. Some breathable penetrating sealers allow earlier application, so check the product label.

How long does paver sealer last on a driveway?

Usually less time than on a patio. Turning tyres scrub film sealers, hot tyres can soften some acrylics in summer, and the wheel tracks take most of the wear. Expect roughly two to four years from a water-based acrylic or hybrid on a typical driveway, as little as one and a half to two years from a glossy solvent acrylic in full sun, and three to five years from a penetrating silane/siloxane. It is common for the tyre lanes to stop beading water while the rest of the driveway still looks fine, so test those first and consider a light maintenance coat concentrated on the worn areas.

How often should you seal pavers around a pool?

Every two to three years is a reasonable expectation for most pool decks, whatever the sealer. Constant splash-out, chlorinated water and especially salt-water pools are hard on both the sealer and the pavers, since salt crystallising in the pores can damage concrete and porous stone such as travertine over time. Check pool decks with a water test every spring. Use a low-sheen or penetrating sealer rather than a high gloss, and add an anti-slip additive to any film sealer, because a glossy wet deck is a genuine slip hazard.

Can I switch to a different sealer when I reseal?

Sometimes, but it is the riskiest part of resealing. Solvent-based acrylic bonds well over solvent-based acrylic, and water-based over water-based usually works. Water-based sealer over an intact solvent-based film often fails to bond, and solvent-based products over water-based films can soften and wrinkle them. A penetrating sealer cannot soak into pavers that still carry an acrylic film. If you want to change chemistry, the reliable route is to let the old sealer wear off fully or strip it, then apply the new product. If you are not sure what is on the pavers, test a small hidden area first and check the new product's label for compatibility guidance.

What happens if you never reseal pavers?

Nothing dramatic at first. Concrete pavers are durable and many unsealed patios last for decades. What you lose is the extra protection: colour fades faster under UV, oil and food stains soak in rather than wiping up, joint sand washes out more readily so weeds and ants move in, and in cold climates more water gets into the pavers to freeze. Once a sealer wears off, the pavers simply behave like unsealed pavers again. If you decide not to reseal, keep up with sweeping, cleaning and re-sanding the joints every year or two, and deal with spills promptly.
