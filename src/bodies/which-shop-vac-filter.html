Which Shop Vac Filter Do You Need? Match the Media to the Mess

The filter that came in the box is built for sawdust and leaves. Drywall dust, concrete grinding, fireplace ash and water each need something different — and the wrong one either clogs in minutes or blows the finest dust straight back into the room.

The right shop vac filter depends on the size of the particles you are picking up, not on the vac. A standard pleated cartridge handles sawdust, leaves, gravel and general garage debris. A fine-dust cartridge is for drywall sanding, fireplace ash and other powders. A HEPA cartridge — paired with a dust bag or a cyclone separator — is the minimum for concrete, masonry and anything else that contains crystalline silica. For water, the paper cartridge comes off and a foam sleeve goes on.

Most people use one filter for everything, which is why so many shop vacs lose suction ten minutes into a drywall job or leave a haze hanging over a ground slab. The fix is cheap: two or three cartridges that share one fit, a pack of bags, and knowing which goes on when.

⚡ The short answer: Sawdust, leaves and general cleanup — standard cartridge, ideally with a bag. Drywall dust and cold ash — fine-dust or HEPA cartridge plus a bag. Concrete grinding, masonry cutting or old epoxy removal — HEPA cartridge, a cyclone separator in front, and a respirator. Water — remove the paper cartridge and fit the foam sleeve. Buy filters by your vac's model number, not by tank size alone, and replace any filter that is torn or will not recover suction after cleaning.

The Six Filter Types

Most mid-size and large wet/dry vacs accept several of these on the same filter cage. Knowing what each is for is most of the decision.

Standard cartridges have open media and lots of pleat area, so they hold airflow through a tank of chips — but fine powder partly passes through and partly packs into the pleats. Fine-dust cartridges use denser media: far less blow-through, at the cost of loading sooner. HEPA cartridges are rated at 99.97% capture at 0.3 microns, the hardest size to filter. That rating applies to the media; on a consumer vac some air bypasses a worn lid seal or loose gasket, so treat HEPA on a shop vac as a large improvement rather than a sealed HEPA system. HEPA media also loads very fast on bulk dust, which is why it needs a bag or separator in front.

The foam sleeve is for water only — it keeps spray off the motor and filters almost nothing dry. Bags catch dust at the inlet before it reaches the cartridge, the single most effective accessory for fine dust, and turn emptying into tying off a bag rather than tipping a cloud into a bin. Disc filters are used on some compact vacs instead of a cartridge; they come in grades too and are not interchangeable with cartridges.

Drywall Dust

Drywall dust is the job that finds out a standard filter fastest. Joint compound sands into an extremely fine powder, and most of it is small enough to pass through open media or wedge itself into the pleats. A standard cartridge typically blinds within minutes: suction drops, the motor note rises, and the dust you are trying to pick up stays on the floor or comes out the exhaust as a fine haze.

The setup that works is a fine-dust or HEPA cartridge with a dust bag in the tank. The bag catches the bulk of the powder, so the cartridge only sees what escapes the bag, and it stays clean enough to hold suction for a whole room. For a whole basement of drywall, add a cyclone separator in front, or use a dust extractor with automatic filter cleaning; our shop vac buying guide covers where the line between a shop vac and an extractor falls.

Do not sweep drywall dust into a pile first — sweeping lofts the finest fraction. Vacuum it directly, and tie the bag off inside the tank before lifting it out, outdoors.

Concrete and Epoxy Grinding Dust

This is the one where the filter choice is about your lungs rather than your suction. Grinding, cutting or scarifying concrete releases respirable crystalline silica, and so does removing an old coating with a grinder. The particles that reach deep into the lungs are invisible, and long-term exposure causes silicosis, which is irreversible. OSHA's exposure limit is 50 micrograms per cubic metre averaged over an eight-hour day — a small fraction of what an unshrouded grinder produces in a closed garage.

The minimum shop vac setup for grinding a garage slab is:

  • A HEPA cartridge correctly seated, with the lid and gasket in good condition.
  • A cyclone separator between the grinder shroud and the vac, so the bulk of the dust drops into a bucket and the HEPA filter sees only what the cyclone misses.
  • A fitted respirator — at least an N95, and a half-mask with P100 cartridges is better — worn while grinding, vacuuming and emptying the separator.
  • A dust shroud on the grinder itself. The vac can only collect what the shroud captures.

Without the separator a HEPA cartridge loads quickly, airflow at the shroud collapses and dust escapes at the wheel. OSHA's construction benchmark for handheld surface grinders is 25 CFM per inch of wheel diameter — 175 CFM for a 7-inch cup wheel — more than a typical shop vac sustains through a hose. For a whole floor with a rented walk-behind grinder, rent the matching extractor; the shop vac setup is for edges and small areas.

The same applies if you are weighing preparation methods for a coating. Grinding produces this dust; acid etching produces a wet slurry instead, which changes the vacuum setup entirely — see our comparison of acid etching vs grinding a garage floor. And the floor needs to be properly clean before any coat goes down: residual dust is a common reason an epoxy garage floor coating fails to bond. The vac, separator and filters belong in the same budget line as the grinder, as our epoxy garage floor cost guide lays out.

💡 Empty the separator like it matters. The fine dust in a separator bucket is the same silica you were trying to capture. Wear the respirator, empty it outdoors, mist the contents lightly with water first or pour slowly into a heavy bag, and never blow out a loaded HEPA filter with compressed air in an enclosed space.

Fireplace and Stove Ashes

Ash is fine, light and abrasive, so the filter side is straightforward: a fine-dust or HEPA cartridge with a bag. A standard cartridge passes a visible plume of ash out of the exhaust and coats the room it was supposed to clean.

The real issue is heat. Buried embers stay hot far longer than they look — commonly a day or more, and fire services often advise several days. A live ember drawn into a tank of fine dust, a paper filter and moving air can smoulder and flare after the vac is put away. The rules:

  • Only vacuum ash that is completely cold. If you are not sure, it is not cold.
  • Shovel first. Move the bulk of the ash into a metal can with a tight-fitting metal lid, stored outdoors on a non-combustible surface away from the house, and use the vac only for the last fine layer.
  • Never mix ash with sawdust. Empty the vac of wood dust before an ash job and empty it again afterwards.
  • Consider a dedicated ash vacuum if you clean a stove weekly — they have metal canisters and hoses for exactly this reason.

Wet Pickup

For water, the paper cartridge comes off. Paper media not designed for water goes soggy, tears, and is ruined, and any dust embedded in it turns to paste. Some cartridges are labelled for both wet and dry use; if yours is, it can stay on, but it must dry completely before dry work again. If the label does not say, assume it is dry-only.

Fit the foam sleeve over the filter cage instead. It keeps spray and wet debris away from the motor and lets water through to the tank. Most vacs have a float in the cage that rises and seals the inlet when the tank is full; when suction suddenly drops and the motor note rises during wet pickup, the tank is full, not clogged. Empty it and carry on.

Remove any dust bag too — bags are dry-only. Afterwards, rinse the sleeve, wipe the tank and leave everything open to dry before refitting paper; a damp tank and filter go mouldy quickly in a closed vac.

If you are vacuuming water out of the basement more than once a season, the vac is treating a symptom. A recurring seep needs the walls, grading or drainage looked at — start with our guide on how to fix a damp basement, and for persistent humidity a basement dehumidifier does more than any vac will.

Sawdust and Shop Debris

Ordinary sawdust from a circular saw, mitre saw or table saw is mostly coarse, and a standard cartridge handles it well. Here airflow matters more than fine filtration, and the standard media's open structure is an advantage: it holds suction through a full tank of chips.

Two exceptions. Sanding dust from an orbital or belt sander is far finer than saw chips, closer to drywall dust than to sawdust, and benefits from a fine-dust cartridge and a bag. And planer or jointer chips fill a tank very fast without challenging the filter much — a separator bucket in front means you empty a bucket rather than unclamping the vac lid every few minutes.

Wood dust is a respiratory hazard in its own right, and MDF sanding dust is especially fine, so for regular woodworking a fine-dust cartridge is a sensible default.

Matching a Filter to Your Vac

Filters fit by the dimensions of the filter cage, the way the filter is retained — a lid, a threaded knob or a clip — and the gasket where it seals against the motor head. Tank size alone is not a reliable guide, because brands use one cartridge across several tank sizes and different cartridges within the same size. The reliable route is the model number on the vac's label, usually on the motor head or the back of the tank, checked against the manual or the filter packaging.

Within a brand family the standard, fine-dust and HEPA cartridges are usually the same size, so once you know your fit you can keep several grades and swap by job.

Ridgid

Most Ridgid wet/dry vacs from about 5 gallons up, including the 12- and 16-gallon NXT models, share one cartridge size. The VF4000 is the standard pleated filter, the VF5000 the fine-dust version, and the VF6000 the HEPA-media version. Because they interchange, you can run a VF4000 for cleanup and swap to a VF6000 for grinding in under a minute. Smaller portable Ridgid vacs use different filters, and dust bags are sized to the tank — check the manual for those.

Shop-Vac

The 90304 is Shop-Vac's standard cartridge for most of its 5-gallon-and-larger wet/dry vacs, and the 90585 is the high-efficiency cartridge of the same fit for finer dust. Bags are sold by letter type according to tank size. Compact and wall-mount Shop-Vac units use their own cartridges or disc filters, which vary by model.

Craftsman

Craftsman is where buying by tank size goes wrong most often. Vacs sold under the name have changed manufacturer and design over the years, and filters for older Sears-era models do not necessarily fit current CMXEVBE-series ones. Match the model number on the vac to the manual's filter chart rather than buying the cartridge that looks right on the shelf.

Aftermarket Filters

Third-party cartridges are often cheaper and many are fine. Check that the listing names your model, not just a tank size, and that the gasket seats firmly — a loose filter lets dust bypass the media entirely. Treat "HEPA-type" wording as a description, not a rating.

Bags and Cyclone Separators

The filter is the last line of defence; the more dust stopped before it, the longer it holds suction and lasts. Bags are the cheap way, and on drywall or ash they transform how a shop vac behaves.

Cyclone separators sit between the hose and the vac, usually on a 5-gallon bucket. Air spins inside a cone, throwing dust against the wall and down into the bucket, so the vac sees a small fraction of the load. Cone-style cyclones such as the Dust Deputy are the choice for fine dust; baffle lids catch chips well but pass more powder. The costs are a little suction and a second container to empty. Use a rigid bucket — a thin one can collapse if the hose blocks.

On fine dust, from worst to best: standard cartridge alone; fine-dust alone; fine-dust plus bag; HEPA plus bag; HEPA plus cyclone. On concrete only the last is adequate.

A loaded filter is the most common reason a shop vac "loses suction". Before assuming the motor is going, check for these:

Most wet/dry vac motors are cooled by the air they move, so running for long periods on a blinded filter makes the motor run hotter. Clearing the filter is cheaper than replacing the vac.

Clean It or Replace It?

Most cartridges can be cleaned several times. The test: if suction comes back after cleaning, keep it; if not, or the media is damaged, replace it.

How to Clean a Cartridge

  • Tap it out first, outdoors and masked, knocking it gently inside a bin or large bag. Often that is all it needs.
  • Brush lightly along the pleats, not across them.
  • Compressed air with care: low pressure, held back, blowing from the clean inside outward. Close-range high pressure tears pleats — and never do this with silica or mould-laden filters indoors.
  • Wash only if labelled washable. Rinse inside-out with a gentle stream, never a pressure washer, and dry completely — typically 24 hours or more. A damp paper filter clogs almost instantly on dry dust.

When to Replace

  • Any tear, hole or split pleat, however small. Hold the filter up to a light to check.
  • A damaged or hardened gasket that no longer seals.
  • Suction that does not recover after a proper clean — the media is blinded deep in the pleats.
  • After silica, lead-paint debris or mould, where cleaning would put the dust back in the air. Bag it sealed.
  • Any sign of mould, or after contaminated water with the filter left on.

Life varies enormously: a standard cartridge on occasional sweeps can last years, while a HEPA cartridge grinding without a separator can be finished in an afternoon. Keep a spare of each grade you use, swap when one loads mid-job, and clean the other later, outside, without hurrying.

The Short Version

Keep a standard cartridge on for sawdust, leaves and everyday garage cleanup. Switch to a fine-dust cartridge with a bag for drywall, sanding dust and cold ash. Use a HEPA cartridge with a cyclone separator and a respirator for concrete, masonry and coating removal. Take the paper off and fit the foam sleeve for water.

Buy filters by the model number on your vac, not by tank size, and favour a brand family where all three grades share one fit. If you are choosing a vac with this in mind, the ease of buying filters is a real reason to pick one model over another — something our best shop vac for garage guide weighs for each pick.

And when suction fades, look at the filter before the motor.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which shop vac filter do I need?

It depends on the dust. A standard pleated cartridge handles sawdust, leaves and general debris. A fine-dust cartridge with a bag suits drywall, sanding dust and cold ash. Concrete grinding or masonry cutting needs a HEPA cartridge, a cyclone separator and a respirator, because of crystalline silica. For water, remove the paper cartridge and fit a foam sleeve. Buy by your vac's model number, not tank size.

Do I need a HEPA filter for drywall dust?

Not necessarily, but you need more than a standard filter, which blinds within minutes on joint compound and passes the finest dust into the room. A fine-dust cartridge with a bag handles most drywall cleanup. HEPA with a bag is worth it for large jobs or anyone sensitive to dust, and for a whole basement add a cyclone separator or use a dust extractor.

What filter should I use for concrete grinding dust?

A HEPA cartridge, with a cone-style cyclone separator between the grinder shroud and the vac, and an N95 or better a P100 respirator. Grinding releases respirable crystalline silica, with an OSHA limit of 50 µg/m³ over a working day. Without the separator a HEPA cartridge loads within minutes and dust escapes at the wheel. For a whole slab, rent the grinder's matching extractor.

Can I vacuum fireplace ashes with a shop vac?

Only when the ash is completely cold, with a fine-dust or HEPA filter and a bag. Buried embers can stay hot for a day or more and can smoulder in a tank of dust and paper. Shovel the bulk into a lidded metal can kept outside, vacuum only the residue, and never into a tank holding sawdust. For weekly stove cleaning, a dedicated ash vacuum is safer.

Do you need to remove the filter to vacuum water?

Remove a paper cartridge unless it is labelled for wet and dry use, take out any dust bag, and fit the foam sleeve. Paper not made for water goes soggy and tears. When suction suddenly drops and the motor note rises during wet pickup, the float has shut off a full tank. Dry the tank, sleeve and filter fully before going back to dry dust.

How do I know if my shop vac filter is clogged?

Suction fading during a job, a rising motor note with weak airflow at the hose, and dust from the exhaust are the usual signs. A puff of dust at start-up means dust has reached the clean side — usually a tear or poor seal. A hot smell means airflow is restricted enough to overheat the motor. If cleaning does not restore suction, check the hose and float, then replace the filter.

Can you wash a shop vac filter?

Only if the label says washable. Tap the dust out outdoors first, rinse gently from the inside outward — never with a pressure washer — and let it dry completely, typically 24 hours or more, before use. A damp paper filter clogs almost instantly on dry dust and can go mouldy. Filters used on silica, lead paint or mould should be bagged and replaced, not cleaned.

Are shop vac filters universal?

No. They fit by cage size, retainer and gasket, and differ across and within tank sizes. Within a brand the grades often share one fit: most Ridgid vacs from about 5 gallons up take the VF4000, VF5000 and VF6000, and most larger Shop-Vac models take the 90304 and 90585. Craftsman varies by generation, so match your model number to the manual.
