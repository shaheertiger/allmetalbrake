Concrete Floor Paint vs Epoxy: Which Should You Use?

Floor paint is cheap, breathable and easy to touch up. Two-part epoxy is many times thicker and far tougher, but it traps moisture and punishes poor prep. The right choice depends less on the product than on the floor under it and what drives across it.

Use floor paint for concrete floors that see foot traffic, sit on a slab that might be damp, or get direct sun: basements, covered porches, patios and light-duty garages. Use two-part epoxy where cars park, chemicals spill and rolling loads grind at the surface, and only on a slab that is dry and has been mechanically prepared. The "one-part epoxy" paints sold beside both are acrylic paints with some epoxy added. They behave like paint, not like epoxy, and you should price and plan them that way.

Most of the difference comes down to thickness and chemistry. Two coats of good porch and floor paint leave about 3 mils of film, and it stays slightly soft and lets water vapour through. A high-solids epoxy coat can leave 10 mils or more, and it cures into a hard, cross-linked plastic that nothing passes through. That one fact explains why epoxy lasts longer under tyres, why it delaminates on damp basements, and why paint is so much easier to live with on the floors that don't need armour.

⚡ The short answer: Acrylic floor paint costs $0.15–$0.50 per sq ft in materials and lasts 2–4 years. It is breathable, UV-stable and recoats in an afternoon. A DIY high-solids epoxy with a topcoat costs $600–$1,400 for a two-car garage and lasts 10–20 years, but it needs a ground, dry slab and stays indoors. Pick paint for basements, porches and patios. Pick epoxy for a garage you park in, and only after the floor passes a moisture test.

What Is Actually in Each Can

Shelf labels blur these products together, so it helps to sort them by chemistry rather than by the word on the front.

Acrylic Latex Porch & Floor Paint

This is a water-based acrylic or modified-acrylic paint, formulated harder and more scrub-resistant than wall paint. It dries as the water evaporates and the acrylic particles fuse into a film. Nothing cross-links chemically, so the film stays thermoplastic: it softens slightly when hot and can be re-wetted by some solvents. Typical coverage is 300–400 square feet per gallon on smooth, sealed concrete and noticeably less on a porous first coat. Each coat dries to roughly 1.5 mils, so two coats give about 3 mils. It cleans up with water, rolls like wall paint, and is sold in hundreds of colours, including the exterior grades sold as porch paint (see our best porch paint guide).

One-Part "Epoxy" or Epoxy-Acrylic Floor Paint

Labels say "1-Part Epoxy," "epoxy-fortified" or "epoxy acrylic." These are acrylic latex paints with an epoxy ester or epoxy-modified resin blended in to improve hardness, adhesion and chemical resistance. You open the can and roll it on. There is no hardener and no pot life, because there is no true two-component cure. Film build is similar to porch paint, around 1.5–2 mils per coat. These are a real step up from basic floor paint on abrasion and oil resistance, and they are a long way short of a two-part epoxy. Think of them as premium paint for concrete floors, not as an epoxy floor.

Two-Part Epoxy

True epoxy comes as two components, a resin and a hardener (usually an amine), that react chemically once mixed. The result is a thermoset, a cross-linked plastic that won't soften with heat or redissolve. Once mixed, the clock starts: pot life is typically 30 minutes to two hours depending on the product and temperature. Two-part epoxies come in three broad grades:

  • Water-based kits (the big-box garage kits). These are roughly 40–60% solids and leave a thin film, often 2–4 mils, with more chemical resistance than paint but not much more thickness.
  • Mid-grade (50–70% solids). These are solvent- or water-borne and build a moderate film.
  • High-solids and 100% solids. Nearly everything you apply stays on the floor, building 8–20 mils per coat. This is what professional floor coatings are built on.
💡 How to tell what you're holding: If the can has no separate hardener, it isn't a two-part epoxy, whatever the label says. If it's a two-part kit, check the coverage rate. A kit that claims 500 sq ft per gallon is laying down a film a fraction as thick as one rated at 150 sq ft per gallon.

Film Thickness: The Number That Decides Lifespan

Every floor coating wears, so lifespan depends mainly on how much material sits between the concrete and the traffic. That's measured in mils (thousandths of an inch).

A gallon of liquid spread over 1,604 square feet makes a wet film 1 mil thick. A 100% solids product rolled at 160 square feet per gallon therefore leaves about 10 mils, and all of it stays because nothing evaporates. A paint that is roughly 35–45% solids by volume, rolled at 350 square feet per gallon, goes on at about 4.5 wet mils and dries to under 2. Two coats of paint come to about 3 mils. One coat of 100% solids epoxy is three times that, and a typical professional system with primer, base and topcoat builds 15–30 mils.

This is why the cheap water-based epoxy kits disappoint. They are genuinely epoxy, with better chemical resistance than paint, but at 2–4 mils they wear through traffic lanes on roughly the same timetable as a good paint. You pay for the epoxy label without the thickness that makes epoxy last. If you want epoxy performance, buy a kit by its coverage rate. Adhesion and Hot-Tire Pickup

How Each One Grips the Slab

Neither paint nor epoxy sticks to smooth, dense concrete well. Both need an open, slightly textured surface to key into. The difference is what happens when the bond is stressed. Paint is thin and flexible, so a weak bond usually shows up as small scuffs and worn patches. Epoxy is thick, rigid and shrinks slightly as it cures. A weak bond under epoxy fails in sheets, often taking a skin of concrete with it, and any coating peels where the slab underneath was contaminated, dusty or damp. Our guide to why epoxy garage floors peel covers those failure patterns in detail.

Hot-Tire Pickup

This is the failure that rules out floor paint for most working garages. Tyres heat up on the drive home, often to 140°F or more after highway driving or in summer. A hot tyre presses into the coating and softens it, and the tyre rubber's plasticisers migrate into the film. When the car pulls out, the tyre lifts the coating with it. You'll see tyre-shaped bare patches exactly where the wheels rest.

Acrylic floor paint is thermoplastic, so it's the most vulnerable. Even the garage-rated latex paints mostly help by being harder, not by changing the chemistry. One-part epoxy-acrylics resist better but still lift under hot, heavy tyres, particularly in the first month while the film is still hardening. A properly cured two-part epoxy is a thermoset and doesn't soften. Lifting on epoxy usually means the bond to the concrete was weak, the film was very thin, or the car went on before full cure. Topcoating epoxy with polyaspartic or polyurethane improves resistance further. That's why the epoxy vs polyaspartic comparison matters if you park a car daily.

Curing time compounds this. Most acrylic floor paints allow foot traffic after a day but recommend waiting 72 hours to a week before vehicles, and they keep hardening for about 30 days. Driving on a two-week-old painted floor in July is close to a guaranteed failure.

Which One for Which Floor

Basement Floors

Usually paint. Basement slabs are the most likely to carry moisture, many older ones have no vapour barrier underneath, and the traffic is light: feet, laundry baskets and the occasional dragged box. A breathable acrylic or one-part epoxy-acrylic handles that well and lets any vapour escape instead of trapping it under the film. Two-part epoxy works in a basement only after the slab passes a moisture test, or with a moisture-mitigating primer underneath. Even then, a failure across a finished basement is expensive. Our best basement floor paint guide covers the products made for this job.

Garages

Usually epoxy, if you park in it. Hot tyres, road salt, dripping oil and brake fluid are exactly what a thick, cross-linked film is built for. Paint is reasonable in a garage that's really storage, a gym or a workspace where the cars stay outside, or as a short-term cosmetic fix before a sale. For a daily-parking garage, the minimum that lasts is a high-solids epoxy, ideally with a polyaspartic or polyurethane topcoat. If the slab fails a moisture test, consider tiles before either.

Covered Porches

Paint. Porches see some UV even under a roof, swing through wide temperature ranges, and often sit on slabs with no barrier beneath. Exterior porch and floor paint is made for that, and it touches up in minutes when furniture scuffs it. Epoxy would amber and chalk at the edges where the sun reaches, and it would trap moisture.

Patios and Uncovered Concrete

Paint, or better, stain plus sealer. Full sun, standing water and freeze-thaw cycles are hard on any film. Exterior porch paint survives with recoating every couple of years. Standard epoxy shouldn't go outdoors at all. A penetrating concrete stain under a breathable sealer is often the better answer because there is no film to peel. See how to stain a concrete patio for that route.

Workshops

Epoxy, or the slab left bare and sealed. Workshops combine rolling tool chests, dropped steel, solvents and spilled finishes, and paint loses to all of them. A high-solids epoxy with a broadcast of flake or grit resists solvents and hides the inevitable marks. One caveat: very heavy point loads and dropped tools can chip a rigid epoxy, while a painted floor just scuffs. If the shop does heavy fabrication, a densified and sealed bare slab may outlast both.

Moisture: Breathable vs Sealed

Concrete is porous, and most slabs on grade move water vapour upward from the soil continuously. How a coating handles that vapour matters more than anything else on a basement floor.

Acrylic paint is breathable. Its film is microporous, so vapour passes through at a modest rate without building pressure underneath. That's why paint tolerates slabs that would destroy epoxy. It isn't waterproof, though. Liquid water from hydrostatic pressure or a high water table will still lift it, leave white efflorescence under it, or simply wear it away faster.

Two-part epoxy is effectively a vapour barrier. Vapour reaching the underside of the film has nowhere to go. It condenses, pressure builds and the bond fails, usually as blisters first and then as peeling sheets. Water-based epoxies are slightly more vapour-tolerant during application, but once cured they still block vapour far more than paint does. Most epoxy manufacturers specify a maximum moisture emission rate (commonly around 3 lbs per 1,000 sq ft per 24 hours by calcium chloride test) or an in-slab relative humidity of roughly 75–80%. Slabs above that need a moisture-mitigating epoxy primer, which adds $1–$3 per square foot.

The free first check is the plastic-sheet test. Tape a two-foot square of plastic tightly to the bare slab with all four edges sealed and leave it for 24–48 hours. Condensation or a darkened patch underneath means vapour is coming through. If it does, sort out the water before choosing a coating. Our guides on hydrostatic pressure in basements and how to fix a damp basement walk through the causes and fixes. A damp slab is the single most common reason a basement epoxy job fails, and it's one of the main disadvantages of epoxy flooring.

Sunlight and UV

Aromatic epoxy resins, which is nearly all of them, are not UV-stable. Sunlight turns them amber, then chalky, within months to a few years, and tinted epoxy shifts colour unevenly wherever sun falls through a door or window. That's acceptable deep in a garage and obvious in a sunlit doorway strip. The fix is a UV-stable topcoat of aliphatic polyurethane or polyaspartic over the epoxy. On any floor that gets real sun, the topcoat is essential, not an optional upgrade.

Acrylic floor paints, especially the exterior porch and floor grades, are UV-stable. They fade gradually rather than yellowing, which is why paint remains the default for porches, steps and patios. One-part epoxy-acrylics sit in between. Many are labelled for interior and exterior use, but check the can before using one where it will see direct sun.

Slip Resistance and Anti-Slip Additives

Every smooth floor coating is slippery when wet, and glossy epoxy is the worst offender. A high-gloss epoxy garage floor with melted snow on it is genuinely hazardous, and painted porch steps after rain aren't much better. Both are easy to fix.

  • Polymer grit additives (fine polypropylene or polyethylene beads) stir into paint or clear topcoats and leave a subtle texture. They suit porches, basement stairs and paint generally. Follow the additive's label for the mix rate, which is usually a few ounces per gallon. Keep stirring as you roll, because the beads settle.
  • Aluminium oxide grit is harder and more aggressive. It's broadcast into or mixed into epoxy and polyaspartic topcoats and is the standard choice for garages, ramps and workshops.
  • Flake broadcast into epoxy adds texture as well as hiding dirt. A full broadcast with a clear topcoat is noticeably less slippery than solid-colour gloss. See our guide to epoxy flake flooring.

Texture makes a floor harder to mop, so concentrate grit at doorways, ramps and steps. Satin finishes are also less slippery than high gloss.

Surface Prep for Each

Preparation decides whether either coating lasts, and the two need different amounts of it.

Prep for Floor Paint

  1. Check for an existing sealer. Sprinkle water on the slab. If it beads instead of darkening the concrete within a minute, a sealer or old coating is present, and paint won't bond until it's removed.
  2. Degrease and clean. Use a concrete degreaser on oil spots, then scrub and rinse.
  3. Open the surface. Use acid or a citric etcher for bare, previously untreated concrete, or a light grind or sanding screen for dense, smooth or troweled slabs. Rinse until the water runs neutral.
  4. Dry and repair. Fill cracks, and let the slab dry fully (at least 24–48 hours after rinsing).
  5. Prime if the label asks. Use a concrete primer on porous or chalky slabs. Then apply two thin coats, not one heavy one.

Prep for Two-Part Epoxy

  1. Moisture test first. Do it before buying anything.
  2. Degrease and remove old coatings completely. Epoxy over paint fails, because it bonds to the paint and the paint lets go of the concrete.
  3. Diamond grind to a concrete surface profile of roughly CSP 2–3, about the texture of medium sandpaper. Grinder rental runs $80–$150 a day.
  4. Repair cracks and spalls with an epoxy or polyurea filler, and grind the patches flush.
  5. Vacuum thoroughly. Grinding dust left on the surface is a bond breaker.

Acid etching is cheaper and is what most budget kits assume. It's fine for paint and unreliable for epoxy, particularly on dense or previously sealed slabs. Our acid etching vs grinding comparison explains when each one works.

Recoating and Repairs

Paint is forgiving. Most acrylic floor paints can be recoated after about four hours. Years later, a worn traffic lane needs only cleaning, a light scuff and a fresh coat, and the repair blends in. You can repaint over sound paint indefinitely, though eventually the build-up chips and wants stripping.

Epoxy is less forgiving. Each two-part product has a recoat window, often between a few hours and roughly 24 hours. Coats applied within it bond chemically. Coats applied after it need the surface sanded or screened first, or the new layer can peel from the old. Spot repairs on chipped epoxy are possible with a matching repair kit, but they nearly always show, especially on solid colours. On a flaked floor they're far less obvious. Refreshing a worn epoxy floor usually means a full-floor scuff and a new topcoat, which is a weekend job rather than an afternoon.

Switching later is one-directional. You can paint over epoxy after scuffing it with a suitable bonding primer, though there's rarely a reason to. You can't epoxy over paint. The paint has to come off completely, which means grinding, so a painted garage floor that you later want to epoxy adds a full day of grinding to the job.

Which Should You Use?

Choose acrylic or one-part epoxy-acrylic floor paint if: the floor is a basement, porch, patio, laundry room or storage garage; the slab is damp or untested; the area gets sun; you want to change colour easily or touch up without fuss; or the budget is under a few hundred dollars. Spend the extra on the one-part epoxy-acrylic where there's light vehicle traffic or occasional spills.

Choose two-part epoxy if: you park cars on the floor daily, work with oils and solvents, or want a floor that still looks finished in ten years, and the slab passes a moisture test and you're prepared to grind it. Buy by solids content, add a polyaspartic or polyurethane topcoat, and give it the full five to seven days before driving on it.

Choose neither if the slab is actively wet or cracking from movement. Fix the water first, or use tiles, which float over a problem slab without bonding to it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is epoxy better than paint for concrete floors?

For a dry garage or workshop with vehicle traffic, yes. Two-part epoxy builds 8–20 mils per coat against roughly 1.5 mils for a coat of paint, cures into a cross-linked film that resists hot tyres and chemicals, and lasts 8–15 years against 2–4. For basements, porches and patios, paint is often the better choice. It's breathable, so it tolerates slab moisture that would blister epoxy. It's UV-stable where epoxy ambers. And it costs a fraction as much to apply and to touch up. "Better" depends on the floor more than on the product.

Is one-part epoxy floor paint real epoxy?

Not in the sense people usually mean. One-part "epoxy" or "epoxy-fortified" floor paints are acrylic latex paints with an epoxy-modified resin blended in for hardness and adhesion. They have no separate hardener and no chemical cross-linking cure, and they build about the same film thickness as porch paint, around 1.5–2 mils per coat. They're a worthwhile step up from basic floor paint on scuff and oil resistance and typically last 3–5 years rather than 2–4. They don't perform like a two-part epoxy, especially under hot tyres.

Can you put epoxy over painted concrete?

No, not reliably. Epoxy shrinks slightly as it cures and grips hard, and it will bond to the paint well enough to pull the paint off the concrete. The result is a sheet failure that takes both layers with it. The paint has to come off completely first, which in practice means diamond grinding the floor back to bare, sound concrete. Going the other way works: paint can go over scuffed, sound epoxy with a suitable bonding primer.

What is the best paint for concrete floors in a basement?

A breathable acrylic or one-part epoxy-acrylic porch and floor paint labelled for concrete, applied over a cleaned, etched and fully dry slab. Breathability matters most because many basement slabs move water vapour, and an impermeable coating traps it and blisters. Run the plastic-sheet test first, and fix any liquid water before painting, because even breathable paint won't survive hydrostatic pressure.

Why does garage floor paint peel under car tires?

Hot-tire pickup. Tyres get hot after driving, the heat softens a thermoplastic acrylic film, and plasticisers from the rubber migrate into it. When the car pulls away, the tyre lifts the coating. It's worst in summer, on new paint that hasn't reached full cure (around 30 days), and on floors that weren't properly etched or ground. Garage-rated and one-part epoxy-acrylic paints resist it somewhat better. Two-part epoxy with a polyaspartic or polyurethane topcoat is the real fix.

Can I use epoxy on an outdoor patio or porch?

Standard epoxy isn't suitable for sunlit outdoor concrete. Aromatic epoxy resins yellow and chalk under UV, outdoor slabs often carry moisture that an impermeable film traps, and freeze-thaw cycles stress a rigid coating. Use exterior porch and floor paint, a penetrating stain with a breathable sealer, or a UV-stable polyaspartic system if you need a tough outdoor coating. Epoxy belongs indoors or under a UV-stable topcoat.

How long does concrete floor paint last compared to epoxy?

Acrylic floor paint typically lasts 2–4 years and one-part epoxy-acrylic 3–5, with traffic lanes and doorways wearing first. A big-box water-based epoxy kit lasts 2–4 years because its film is thin. Mid-grade epoxy lasts 5–8 years, high-solids epoxy 8–15, and a professional epoxy system with flake and a polyaspartic topcoat 10–20. Preparation matters as much as product: any of these fails early over a contaminated, unprepared or damp slab.
