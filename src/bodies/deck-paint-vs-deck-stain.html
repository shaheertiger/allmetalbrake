Deck Paint vs Deck Stain: Which Should You Use?

Paint sits on top of the wood as a film; stain soaks into it. That one difference decides how long each lasts on a deck floor, how it fails, and how much work the next refresh will be.

For the walking surface of an open, uncovered deck, stain is the better choice for most people. It soaks into the wood rather than sitting on top of it, so when it wears out it simply fades, and the next coat goes on after a wash. Deck paint forms a film, and on horizontal boards that take rain, sun and foot traffic, moisture works its way underneath and the film peels — usually within two to four years, and usually in the busiest paths first. Fixing it means scraping and sanding, not just recoating.

Paint earns its place on vertical surfaces. Railings, balusters, posts, fascia and skirting shed water instead of holding it, take no foot traffic, and can carry a painted finish for many years. It also makes sense on covered porches, where the floor is out of the rain. That is why the best-looking, lowest-maintenance answer for many decks is both: stain underfoot, paint on everything that stands up.

⚡ The short answer: On an open deck floor, use stain — semi-transparent for wood in decent shape, solid for tired boards. Expect 2–5 years between recoats with no stripping. Use paint on railings, posts, balusters and skirting, where it can last 5–10 years, and on covered porch floors. Avoid painting an exposed deck floor unless you accept scraping and spot-repairing every few years. And once a deck is painted, going back to stain means stripping or sanding every board to bare wood.

Film vs Penetration: The Difference That Decides Everything

Deck stain is mostly pigment and binder in a thin vehicle designed to soak into the top layer of wood fibers. Semi-transparent stains carry modest pigment and let the grain show; solid stains carry much more and behave a little like thin paint. The defining trait of a true penetrating stain is that once it has dried, there is almost nothing left sitting on the surface. The color and water repellency live inside the wood.

Deck and porch paint is built the other way. It has a heavier binder and higher solids, and it is designed to form a continuous skin over the wood — a few thousandths of an inch thick after two coats. That skin is what gives paint its uniform color, its opacity and its scrub resistance. It is also its weakness on a deck, because a film is only as good as its bond to what is underneath.

Wood is never still. It absorbs water and swells, dries and shrinks, every day and every season. A penetrating stain moves with the fibers because it is part of them. A film has to stretch and flex over the top, and where water gets behind it — through end grain, cracks, fastener holes, the gaps between boards, or up from the underside — the bond weakens and the film lifts. That is the mechanism behind almost every peeling deck, whether the coating on it is paint or a heavily applied solid stain.

Solid stain sits between the two. It forms a thinner film than paint and is formulated to stay a little more flexible, but it still films, and it still peels on a deck floor when it fails. If your real choice is between the two kinds of stain rather than stain and paint, our comparison of solid vs semi-transparent deck stain goes through that decision in detail.

Lifespan: Why Paint Lasts on Rails but Peels on Boards

The same can of paint can last eight years on a baluster and three years on the floor two feet below it. The difference is orientation, and it is the most important thing to understand before choosing.

Vertical surfaces shed water. Rain hits a baluster or a post and runs off within seconds. The wood stays relatively dry, its moisture content swings less, and the film is under little stress. Nothing walks on it, so there is no abrasion. Exterior paint on vertical wood behaves much as it does on siding, and a good acrylic over a proper primer routinely lasts five to ten years there. On railings, paint often outlasts any stain, because a thick film blocks UV better than a thin penetrating coat.

Horizontal surfaces hold water. A deck board catches rain on its top face, lets it pool in cupped boards and sit along fasteners, and wicks it into the end grain at every cut. The underside of a deck is almost never coated and often sits over damp soil with poor airflow. Moisture moves through the board and tries to exit through the top face, and when that face is covered by a film, the vapor pushes against the back of it. Add direct overhead sun — the harshest UV exposure any surface on a house gets — and constant foot traffic grinding grit into the finish, and the film is under attack from above and below.

The result on a painted deck floor is a predictable pattern. The first signs appear within two to four years: hairline cracks along board ends, small blisters, then flakes lifting near fasteners and in the walkway from the door to the steps. The areas under furniture or along the shaded edges may still look good, which makes the deck look patchy rather than evenly worn. Covered porches are the exception — out of the rain, a painted porch floor can last five years or more, which is why porch and floor paint is a well-established category while painting open decks remains contentious.

Stain on the same deck floor wears out on a similar timeline — roughly two to three years for semi-transparent and three to five for solid — but it does so by fading and eroding from the top, evenly enough that one cleaning and one coat restores it. Calendar lifespan is closer than people expect. What differs is what the end of that lifespan looks like and costs.

💡 The rail cap is a floor. The flat top of a handrail is a horizontal surface: it holds water, takes direct sun and gets leaned on. It is often the first place paint fails on an otherwise sound painted railing. If you paint the balusters and posts, consider staining the rail cap to match the floor — it ties the look together and removes the weakest painted surface.

Maintenance and Recoating

Maintenance is where the decision is really made, because you will repeat it many times over the life of the deck.

Refreshing a Stained Deck

When a semi-transparent stain fades and water stops beading, the routine is simple: clear the deck, wash it with a deck cleaner (an oxygen-bleach or sodium-percarbonate cleaner handles most graying and mildew), rinse, let it dry for a day or two, and apply one coat of the same stain. On a typical 300-square-foot deck that is a cleaning day plus a staining afternoon. No scraping, no priming, no patches.

Solid stain is a step more work. Where it has only worn thin, a cleaning and a fresh coat will do. Where it has started to peel, the loose areas must be scraped and feathered first — the same job as paint, on a smaller scale.

Refreshing a Painted Deck

Paint cannot be refreshed by recoating alone, because a new coat bonds only to the old one, and the old one is lifting. The process is: scrape every loose or blistered area back to a firmly bonded edge, sand those edges so they do not telegraph through the new coat, wash and let the deck dry, spot-prime the bare wood, then repaint — usually the whole floor, since patches rarely match in sheen or color once they weather. A careful job on a 300-square-foot deck is a long weekend, and it tends to come around every two to four years on an exposed floor.

Over several cycles, each repaint thickens the areas that held while peeled areas restart from bare wood, and eventually many owners strip the whole deck. If your deck is already at that stage, our guide to why deck finishes peel covers the causes and the repair sequence.

Slipperiness Underfoot

A painted deck is noticeably more slippery when wet than a stained one. Paint fills the grain and smooths over the fine texture of the board surface, and most deck and porch paints dry to a satin or low-gloss finish. Add rain, dew, frost or wet leaves and the surface can become genuinely hazardous, especially on stairs.

Stain leaves the wood's own texture in place. Even a solid stain, which hides the grain color, leaves most of the surface texture, so a stained deck keeps its grip far better when wet.

If you do paint a deck floor or steps, use an anti-slip additive — a fine polymer or silica grit stirred into the final coat — or choose a paint with grit already in it. On steps, a few strips of exterior anti-slip tape on the tread nosings are a sensible addition either way.

Color, Coverage and Heat

Color range. Paint wins outright. Any exterior color a paint store can tint is available, including whites, grays, blues and greens that stain lines rarely offer. Semi-transparent stains are mostly wood tones — cedar, redwood, chestnut, gray — because the wood's own color shows through and mixes with the pigment. Solid stains offer a wider palette, much closer to paint, but still with fewer bright and very light options.

Coverage per gallon. On smooth, previously sealed or painted wood, deck paint typically covers 250–400 square feet per gallon per coat. Stain varies widely with the wood: 150–300 square feet per gallon on new or smooth boards, sometimes as little as 100 on rough, thirsty, weathered wood. Paint generally needs a primer on bare wood and two finish coats; semi-transparent stain often needs one coat, or two wet-on-wet.

Heat. Dark finishes of either type get very hot in direct summer sun — hot enough to be uncomfortable barefoot. Light paint colors stay noticeably cooler than dark stains, which is one genuine advantage of paint on a sunny deck. If you stain, a mid-tone rather than a dark brown helps.

Hiding Old or Damaged Wood

This is the most common reason people consider painting a deck: the boards are gray, stained, patched with newer lumber, or a mix of species, and a semi-transparent stain would highlight every flaw. Paint does hide all of that. It also hides tannin streaks, old stain remnants and rust marks from fasteners, especially over a stain-blocking primer.

But the decks that most need hiding are often the worst candidates for paint. Weathered, checked, splintering boards absorb water readily and move a lot, which is exactly the condition that makes a film fail early. Paint covers the color of a crack, but not the crack itself; it bridges hairline checks for a season, then splits along them. It does nothing for soft or rotting wood, which has to be replaced regardless of finish.

Before reaching for paint, consider solid stain. It covers color differences and gray weathering almost as well, keeps more texture for grip, and when it fails it is thinner and easier to scrape. For many tired decks, solid stain on the floor and paint on the railings is the better compromise. If the boards are truly beyond help — deep splits, widespread splinters, cupping — replacing the worst boards, or flipping them to expose the fresh underside, is often a better investment than any coating.

Deck Resurfacers: The Third Option

Resurfacers, often sold as "deck restore" coatings, are very thick, textured acrylic coatings marketed for decks that are too far gone for stain. They go on at far heavier film builds than paint, sometimes with a primer and two coats, and they claim to fill cracks up to roughly a quarter inch, lock down splinters and leave a textured, slip-resistant surface. Coverage is low — often only 50–100 square feet per gallon per coat — so material costs run well above paint or stain.

They are worth knowing about mainly for their track record. In the mid-2010s, some of the best-known resurfacer lines from major manufacturers drew large numbers of customer complaints and class-action lawsuits alleging peeling, bubbling and delamination, and at least one of those cases ended in a settlement with refunds for buyers. Formulations and application instructions have changed since, and some owners do report good results, particularly on well-prepared decks in drier climates. But the underlying physics has not changed: a very thick film on a horizontal board still has to cope with moisture coming up from below.

The other cost is permanence. A resurfacer is extremely difficult to remove. When it fails, it often fails in sheets, and stripping it back to bare wood is a heavier job than stripping paint. In practice, a resurfaced deck usually stays resurfaced, or gets new boards. Treat a resurfacer as a last step before replacement, not as a maintenance product, and only consider it if the boards are structurally sound and you accept that you are committing to it.

Cost Compared

Per gallon, deck stain and deck paint cost roughly the same: about $35–$70 for a good-quality product of either type. The differences come from how many gallons and coats the job needs, whether a primer is required, and above all the cost of maintenance over the following decade.

Switching Between Them

From Stain to Paint: Straightforward

Going from a penetrating stain to paint is easy. Once the old stain has weathered and the wood absorbs water again — sprinkle some on; if it soaks in rather than beading, it is ready — clean the deck, let it dry thoroughly, prime, and paint. Freshly applied oil-based stain should be left to weather for a season before painting, because the oils can interfere with adhesion. A bonding or stain-blocking primer is cheap insurance either way. Going from solid stain to paint works similarly, provided the stain is sound; scrape any loose areas first.

From Paint to Stain: A Major Job

Going the other way is hard. A penetrating stain needs bare wood to soak into, and any paint left on the boards will block it, leaving blotches and a patchy color. That means removing essentially all of the paint from every board surface you want stained.

There are two routes. Chemical stripping with a paint stripper, followed by power washing and scraping, works but often takes more than one application, and the gaps between boards and the edges around fasteners are tedious. Standard deck stain strippers, which are designed for stain, frequently struggle with full paint films; our guide on how to strip deck stain explains which products handle which coatings. Sanding with a floor or belt sander removes paint from board faces quickly but leaves paint in the gaps and on board edges, and it takes off some wood along with it. Most real jobs use both. Budget at least a full weekend for a modest deck, and expect some boards to be easier to replace than to clean.

The practical middle ground, if a painted deck is peeling and you want to move away from paint, is to go to a solid stain rather than a semi-transparent. A solid acrylic stain can usually go over sound, well-scraped paint after cleaning and light sanding, much like a repaint, without needing bare wood everywhere. It does not get you back to a penetrating finish, but it gives a more textured, less slippery surface and a thinner film than more paint would. Check the product label for its guidance on applying over existing coatings.

💡 The one-way door. Stain can become paint at any time. Paint becomes stain only with stripping and sanding. If you are undecided, start with stain — you keep both options open.

The Best Combination: Stain the Floor, Paint the Rails

Many of the best-looking, longest-lasting decks use both finishes, each where it works best. The floor boards, stair treads and rail caps get a semi-transparent or solid stain. The balusters, posts, stair stringers, fascia boards and skirting get exterior paint — often white or a color matching the house trim.

It looks deliberate and classic, the way traditional porches have been finished for generations. It puts the peeling-prone film only on surfaces that shed water, so the paint lasts its full potential life. And it keeps the walking surface on the easy-maintenance cycle: when the floor needs attention every few years, you wash and restain it without touching the painted railings.

A few practical points make the combination work:

  • Work top down. Paint the railings and posts first, with the floor covered by drop cloths, then stain the floor after the paint has dried. Drips of stain on a painted post are easier to wipe than paint drips on bare deck boards are to remove.
  • Mask the joint. Use painter's tape where posts meet the floor so the stain line is clean.
  • Stain the rail cap. As noted above, the top of the handrail behaves like a floor board. Staining it to match the deck avoids the most failure-prone painted surface.
  • Prime the paint surfaces. Bare or pressure-treated wood that will be painted needs a quality exterior primer first, especially if it bleeds tannin.

For new pressure-treated lumber, both finishes need the wood to dry first — often several weeks to a few months after installation, depending on how wet it was delivered. The water-drop test applies to both. Our picks for stain for pressure-treated wood cover the products that tolerate it best.

The Short Version

Stain the floor. On any open, uncovered deck surface you walk on, a penetrating stain is the better finish. It wears evenly, stays grippy when wet, and is refreshed with a wash and one coat. Use semi-transparent if you like the wood, solid if the boards need hiding. Our best deck stain roundup covers the strongest options in each category.

Paint what stands up. Railings, balusters, posts, fascia and skirting are where paint performs well, giving a crisp color for five to ten years. A painted railing above a stained floor is the classic combination for good reason.

Paint a floor only when it is covered, or when you are happy to scrape and touch up every few years. If you do, choose a purpose-made deck or porch paint, prime bare wood, and add anti-slip grit. Our best deck paint guide compares the products built for that job.

Treat resurfacers as a last resort, not a maintenance product, and remember the one-way door: stain can always become paint later, but paint becomes stain only by stripping it all off.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it better to paint or stain a deck?

For the floor of an open, uncovered deck, stain is better for most people. It soaks into the wood rather than forming a film, so it fades and wears evenly instead of peeling, and refreshing it takes a wash and one coat. Paint forms a film that tends to peel on horizontal boards within two to four years, because moisture gets underneath from rain, end grain and the damp underside of the deck, and fixing it means scraping and sanding. Paint is the better choice for railings, posts, balusters and skirting, which shed water, and for covered porch floors that stay out of the rain. Many decks look and last best with both: stain on the floor, paint on the rails.

Does deck paint last longer than stain?

On vertical surfaces, yes — exterior paint on railings and posts commonly lasts five to ten years. On an open deck floor, not really: paint there typically starts peeling in traffic areas within two to four years, against two to three for semi-transparent stain and three to five for solid. The bigger difference is how each ends: stain fades evenly and is recoated after a cleaning, while paint peels unevenly and must be scraped, sanded and primed first.

Can you stain over a painted deck?

Not with a semi-transparent or other penetrating stain. Those need bare wood to soak into, and leftover paint leaves a blotchy result, so you would have to strip or sand off essentially all of it — a weekend or more on a modest deck. The practical alternative is a solid acrylic stain, which can usually go over sound, well-scraped paint after cleaning and light sanding. Check the label for its guidance over existing coatings.

Can you paint over deck stain?

Yes, and it is much easier than going the other way. Once the old stain has weathered and the wood absorbs water again — a few drops should soak in rather than bead — clean the deck, let it dry thoroughly, apply an exterior primer, and paint. If an oil-based stain was applied recently, let it weather for a season first, because fresh oils can interfere with paint adhesion. Over a solid stain, scrape any loose areas, clean and lightly sand, and prime where wood is exposed. Use a bonding or stain-blocking primer if you are unsure what is on the deck.

Why does paint peel on deck floors but not on railings?

Because horizontal boards hold water and vertical surfaces shed it. A deck board catches rain, lets it pool around fasteners and soaks it up through cut ends, while the uncoated underside over damp ground pushes moisture up against the back of the paint film. Direct sun and foot traffic attack it from above. Railings and posts drain within seconds and take no traffic. The flat top of a handrail is the exception — it behaves like a floor and is often the first painted surface to fail.

Are deck resurfacers like "deck restore" coatings worth it?

Only as a last resort before replacing boards. Resurfacers are very thick textured coatings that claim to fill cracks and lock down splinters, but some of the best-known products drew class-action lawsuits over peeling and delamination in the mid-2010s, at least one of which was settled. Formulas have changed since, but a thick film on horizontal boards still faces moisture from below, and resurfacers are very hard to remove. Price out replacing the worst boards and staining before committing to one.

Is it cheaper to paint or stain a deck?

The first job costs about the same per gallon — roughly $35–$70 for a quality product of either type — but paint usually needs a primer and two coats, so materials for a 300-square-foot deck floor run around $200–$400 for paint against $100–$300 for stain. Professional labor is similar at about $2–$5 per square foot, with paint toward the higher end. The real difference is maintenance: a stain refresh is a wash and one coat, while repainting a peeling deck means scraping, sanding and priming first. Over ten years on an open deck, stain is almost always cheaper.
