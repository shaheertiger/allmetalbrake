Paver Sand vs. Play Sand — What Actually Goes Under Pavers

They sit on the same pallet, cost within a couple of dollars of each other, and look the same through the plastic. Using the wrong one is the most common reason a paver patio sinks within two seasons.

Walk into a home center and you will find at least five bagged sands sitting on the same pallet rack: play sand, all-purpose sand, paver sand, paver base, polymeric sand, and mason sand. They look nearly identical through the plastic. They cost within a few dollars of each other. And using the wrong one under a patio is the single most common reason a paver installation sinks, rocks, and washes out within two seasons.

The confusion between paver sand and play sand is the most consequential of these mix-ups, because play sand is the cheapest bag on the shelf and it is the worst possible choice for the job. This guide explains what each sand actually is, why the difference matters mechanically, how much you need, and exactly which product belongs in each layer of a paver installation.

⚡ Quick Answer: Never use play sand under or between pavers. Play sand is fine-grained, rounded, and washed specifically so it is soft and dust-free for children — the same properties make it compress unevenly, hold water, and refuse to lock together under load. Paver sand (coarse, sharp-edged, angular concrete sand) is what you bed pavers in. Polymeric sand is what you sweep into the joints afterward. Play sand belongs in a sandbox and nowhere else on a hardscape job.

What Is Paver Sand?

Paver sand — sold as bedding sand, leveling sand, or in bulk as concrete sand or ASTM C33 sand — is a coarse, washed, angular sand with a controlled range of particle sizes. The particles are irregular and sharp-edged because they come from crushed rock or from natural deposits that have not been rounded by long water transport.

That angularity is the entire point. When you compact angular particles, they interlock — each grain wedges against its neighbors and the layer becomes a stable, load-bearing mass that will not shift. Round particles cannot do this. They roll past one another under load, which is precisely why a bag of marbles behaves nothing like a bag of gravel.

The second defining property is drainage. Paver sand is graded so that water passes through it quickly rather than being held in the layer. Water trapped under pavers is what causes frost heave in cold climates: the water freezes, expands roughly 9%, lifts the pavers, and leaves them sitting proud and uneven when it thaws. A properly graded bedding sand drains before it can freeze in place.

You will also see this material sold as Quikrete paver sand, patio paver sand, or simply paver leveling sand in bagged form at the big-box stores. Those are all the same category of product. In bulk from a landscape supply yard, ask for concrete sand or C33 sand — it is the identical material at roughly a fifth of the bagged price.

What Is Play Sand — and Why It Fails Under Pavers

Play sand is manufactured to a completely different specification, and every step of that specification works against hardscape performance.

It is fine-grained, typically screened to a narrow band of small particle sizes so it feels soft and pours smoothly. It is washed and often chemically treated to remove silt, clay, and crystalline silica dust, because children breathe it. Most importantly, the source material is usually a natural river or beach deposit, which means the grains have been tumbled smooth over geological time. They are rounded.

Put that under a paver and three things go wrong:

  • It will not lock. Rounded grains of uniform size cannot interlock. Compact it and it densifies briefly, then re-mobilizes the first time a vehicle or a heavy furniture leg loads it. This is what produces the rocking, sinking pavers that show up in year two.
  • It holds water. The fine, uniform grading means small pore spaces and slow drainage. In a freeze-thaw climate, that trapped water is a heave engine. In any climate, saturated bedding sand loses its bearing capacity entirely.
  • It migrates. Fine sand travels. It washes down through the base course, out through the edges, and up through the joints. The bed thins unevenly and the surface goes wavy.

The same reasoning rules out play sand for the joints between pavers. Joint sand needs to wedge tight and stay put; fine round sand washes out with the first hard rain, opening the joints to weeds and ants and letting the pavers lose their interlock with each other.

Paver Base vs. Paver Sand — Two Different Layers

This is the second common confusion, and it matters just as much. Paver base and paver sand are not alternatives. They are two distinct layers in the same installation, and you need both.

Paver Base (Class II Road Base, Crusher Run, 3/4-inch Minus)

The base is the structural layer. It is a crushed aggregate containing stone from roughly 3/4-inch down to fine dust, and that full range of sizes is deliberate: the fines fill the voids between the larger stones so the compacted layer becomes nearly solid. This is the layer that actually carries load and distributes it into the subgrade. It goes down in lifts of 2–3 inches and each lift is compacted with a plate compactor before the next is added.

Depth: 4 inches of compacted base for pedestrian patios and walkways. 6–8 inches for a driveway that will carry vehicles. Add 2 inches to those figures in areas with deep frost or soft clay subgrade.

Paver Sand (Bedding Layer)

The bedding sand sits on top of the compacted base and its only job is to give the pavers a uniform surface to seat into, absorbing the small dimensional variations between individual units. It is screeded, not compacted — you drag a screed board across guide rails to strike it perfectly flat, then lay pavers on the undisturbed surface without walking on it.

Depth: 1 inch, and 1 inch only. This is the number people get wrong most often. A thicker bedding layer feels more forgiving during installation and is the direct cause of the most common paver failure mode. Sand deeper than about 1.5 inches will consolidate unevenly under traffic, and every low spot in that consolidation becomes a dip in the finished surface. If your base is uneven, fix the base — do not fix it with extra sand.

🔧 The One-Inch Rule: Screed exactly 1 inch of bedding sand. If you are tempted to add more to level a low spot, you are correcting a base problem in the wrong layer. Pull the sand, add and compact more base, and re-screed.

Joint Sand (Between the Pavers)

After the pavers are laid and the field is compacted, sand is swept into the joints to lock the units against one another. This is a third material again — either a coarse jointing sand or, far more commonly now, polymeric sand, which contains a binder that activates with water and sets into a firm, weed-resistant, ant-resistant joint. For anything other than a very casual installation, polymeric sand is worth the premium.

How Much Sand Do I Need for Pavers?

Bedding sand at a 1-inch depth covers approximately 100 square feet per cubic yard, or roughly 0.5 cubic feet per 6 square feet if you are working in bags. The practical arithmetic:

  • Cubic yards needed = (square feet × 0.0833) ÷ 27 — where 0.0833 is one inch expressed in feet.
  • A 50-lb bag of paver sand yields roughly 0.5 cubic feet.
  • One cubic yard = 27 cubic feet ≈ 54 bags.

What to Use to Fill In Between Pavers

The joints are where most patios visibly fail, so the choice of joint filler deserves real attention. There are four realistic options:

Polymeric Sand

Fine sand blended with a polymer binder. Swept into dry joints, then misted with water in stages, it cures into a firm matrix that resists washout, suppresses weeds, and blocks ants. It is the default recommendation for almost every residential installation. Expect 5–10 years of service before joints need topping up. The critical constraint is that the pavers and joints must be bone dry when you sweep it in, and every trace of it must be blown off the paver faces before you water it — polymeric sand that cures on the surface leaves a haze that is very difficult to remove.

Coarse Jointing Sand

Plain angular sand with no binder. Cheaper, permanently flexible, and forgiving of movement, but it washes out in heavy rain and under pressure washing, and it does nothing to stop weeds or ants. Reasonable for informal paths and for installations where you expect to re-sweep annually.

Stone Dust / Screenings

Common in older installations and still used in some regions. It packs firmly and is inexpensive, but it holds moisture, stains light-colored pavers, and grows weeds enthusiastically. We would not choose it today.

Silica Sand for Pavers

A frequent search, and the answer is nuanced. Silica sand — fine, uniform, high-purity quartz — is a component in many polymeric products and is used in specific applications like artificial turf infill. On its own, in a paver joint, it behaves much like play sand: too uniform, too fine, prone to washing out. Dry silica sand also produces respirable crystalline silica dust, which is a genuine health hazard. Use a purpose-made jointing or polymeric product rather than raw silica.

How to Use Paver Sand Correctly

Step 1 — Excavate

Dig to the depth of your base plus 1 inch of bedding plus the paver thickness. For a typical 2-3/8-inch paver on a 4-inch base, that is roughly 7-1/2 inches of excavation below the finished grade. Slope the excavation away from any structure at 1/8 to 1/4 inch per foot so water sheds.

Step 2 — Compact the Subgrade

Run a plate compactor over the bare soil before any aggregate goes in. Skipping this step means the base compacts into the subgrade later instead of at the start, and the surface drops.

Step 3 — Base in Lifts

Spread the crushed base in 2–3 inch lifts, lightly moistening each one, and compact each lift in two directions before adding the next. A base that has been dumped to full depth and compacted once is not compacted — the bottom half is loose.

Step 4 — Set Screed Rails and Screed the Sand

Lay two lengths of 1-inch outside-diameter conduit or 1-inch square tube on the compacted base as guide rails. Spread bedding sand over them, then pull a straight board across the rails to strike the sand exactly level at 1 inch. Lift the rails out and fill the grooves by hand.

Step 5 — Lay Pavers Without Walking on the Sand

Work from laid pavers outward. Set each unit straight down rather than sliding it into place, which would plow the screeded sand. Do not compact the bedding sand — the pavers themselves will seat into it during final compaction.

Step 6 — Edge Restraint, Then Compact the Field

Install rigid plastic or aluminum edge restraint spiked into the base around the perimeter. Without it, the outer courses migrate outward and the whole field loosens. Then run the plate compactor over the pavers with a protective mat, in two passes at right angles, to seat them into the bedding layer.

Step 7 — Joint Sand and Final Compaction

Sweep joint sand across the surface, working it fully into every joint, then compact again to settle it and top up. If using polymeric sand, follow the product's watering sequence precisely — usually a light mist, a pause, and a second mist — and stop before the joints are saturated.

⚠️ Common Failure: Almost every sunken, rocking paver patio traces back to one of three errors — too little base, too much bedding sand, or no edge restraint. All three are cheap to get right on installation day and expensive to fix afterward.

Keeping Sand Between Pavers in a Patio

Joint sand washing out is the most common maintenance complaint after a patio has been down a few years, and it usually has a cause worth addressing rather than just re-sweeping.

Surface water is running across the patio instead of through it. Check that the slope is genuinely 1/8–1/4 inch per foot and that downspouts are not discharging onto the field. Concentrated flow strips joints faster than anything else.

Pressure washing has been stripping it. A pressure washer aimed into the joints will excavate them in seconds. Clean pavers with the fan tip at a shallow angle, held well back, and never point the stream directly down a joint.

The joints were never properly filled. Sand needs to be worked in with repeated sweeping and compaction, not swept once and watered. Under-filled joints leave the pavers free to move, which grinds the remaining sand and accelerates loss.

The product was wrong. If plain sand keeps washing out, switching to a quality polymeric sand solves it. Removing the old sand first matters — the polymeric product needs a joint depth of at least 1 to 1.5 inches to develop strength.

The Bottom Line

Paver sand and play sand are not interchangeable and never have been. Paver sand is coarse and angular so it interlocks and drains; play sand is fine and rounded so it is soft and clean for children. Substituting one for the other trades a $0 saving for a patio that sinks.

Build the layers correctly and in order: a compacted crushed-aggregate base of 4 inches for foot traffic or 6–8 inches for vehicles, exactly 1 inch of screeded concrete sand as bedding, pavers laid without disturbing that screed, rigid edge restraint around the perimeter, and polymeric sand swept into the joints. Every one of those layers uses a different material, and doing it right the first time costs a few hundred dollars more than doing it wrong — against a rebuild that costs thousands.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use play sand under pavers?

No. Play sand is manufactured to be soft, fine, and dust-free for children, which makes it exactly wrong for hardscape. Its grains are rounded rather than angular, so they cannot interlock under compaction — they re-mobilize under load and the pavers sink and rock. Its fine, uniform grading also drains poorly, so it holds water that causes frost heave in cold climates and destroys bearing capacity everywhere else. And it migrates: fine sand washes down through the base, out at the edges, and up through the joints. Use washed concrete sand (ASTM C33), sold bagged as paver sand or bedding sand. It costs about the same as play sand, so there is no saving in the substitution.

What is the difference between paver base and paver sand?

They are two different layers, not two options. Paver base is crushed aggregate ranging from about 3/4 inch down to dust; the mixed sizes let it compact into a nearly solid structural layer that carries load and spreads it into the subgrade. It goes down first, in 2–3 inch lifts, each compacted before the next. Paver sand is the thin bedding layer that sits on top of the compacted base and gives the pavers a uniform surface to seat into. Base depth is 4 inches for patios and walkways, 6–8 inches for driveways. Bedding sand depth is exactly 1 inch. You need both layers — base alone leaves pavers sitting on an irregular stone surface, and sand alone has nothing structural beneath it.

How much paver sand do I need?

At the correct 1-inch bedding depth, one cubic yard of sand covers roughly 100 square feet. The formula is cubic yards = (square feet × 0.0833) ÷ 27. A 50-lb bag yields about 0.5 cubic feet, so a cubic yard is roughly 54 bags. Practical figures: 100 sq ft needs about 0.31 cu yd (17 bags), 300 sq ft needs about 0.93 cu yd (50 bags), and 600 sq ft needs about 1.85 cu yd (100 bags). Order 10% extra for screeding waste. Above about 150 square feet, buy in bulk — a 300 sq ft patio needs roughly $350 of bagged sand versus $40–$70 delivered loose.

How much sand should go under pavers?

Exactly 1 inch of screeded bedding sand — no more. This is the number DIY installations most often get wrong. A thicker sand layer feels more forgiving while you are laying pavers because it hides irregularities in the base, and that is precisely the problem: it consolidates unevenly under traffic, and every spot that consolidates more becomes a dip in the finished surface. Anything over about 1.5 inches will move. If you find yourself adding sand to level a low area, the base is the problem — pull the sand back, add and compact more base aggregate, and re-screed at 1 inch. The bedding sand is also screeded, never compacted; the pavers seat into it during final plate compaction.

What should I use to fill in between pavers?

Polymeric sand for almost every residential installation. It is fine sand blended with a polymer binder that activates when misted with water and cures into a firm joint that resists washout, weeds, and ants, typically lasting 5–10 years before it needs topping up. The two rules that determine whether it works: the pavers and joints must be completely dry when you sweep it in, and every trace must be blown off the paver faces before watering, because cured polymeric haze on the surface is very hard to remove. Plain coarse jointing sand is a cheaper alternative that stays flexible but washes out in heavy rain and does nothing about weeds. Avoid stone dust, which stains light pavers and grows weeds, and avoid raw silica sand, which washes out like play sand and produces hazardous respirable dust when dry.

Is Quikrete paver sand the same as concrete sand?

Functionally, yes — bagged products marketed as paver sand, patio paver sand, or paver leveling sand are washed coarse sand graded for use as a bedding layer, which is the same category as ASTM C33 concrete sand. The bagged versions are convenient, consistent, and appropriate for small jobs and repairs. The one thing they are not is economical at scale: the identical material costs roughly a fifth as much when ordered in bulk from a landscape supply yard, where you should ask for concrete sand or C33 sand rather than paver sand, since yards use the industry terminology. For anything over about 150 square feet, bulk is the obvious choice.

Can I use regular sand between pavers instead of polymeric?

You can, and it will work in a limited way, but expect to maintain it. Coarse jointing sand with no binder stays flexible and is inexpensive, but it washes out in heavy rain, strips under a pressure washer, and offers no resistance to weeds or ants. If you use it, plan on re-sweeping the joints roughly once a year. What you should not use is fine sand — play sand, mason sand, or raw silica — which washes out far faster and lets the pavers lose interlock with one another. If your joints keep emptying, the fix is to remove the loose sand to a depth of at least an inch and refill with a quality polymeric product.

How long does polymeric sand last between pavers?

Five to ten years is realistic for a correctly installed polymeric joint, and the variation depends almost entirely on installation quality and drainage. Joints filled to full depth (at least 1 to 1.5 inches), swept clean off the paver faces, and watered in the staged sequence the manufacturer specifies will sit at the long end of that range. Joints filled shallowly, or watered in one heavy soak that floods the binder out, will start crumbling within two or three years. Drainage matters as much: if surface water sheets across the patio because the slope is inadequate or a downspout discharges onto it, even a good joint will erode early. Topping up is straightforward — clear the loose material, re-sweep with fresh product, and water it in again.
