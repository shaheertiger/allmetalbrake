How to Waterproof Basement Walls — Inside and Out

Most damp basements are misdiagnosed. Here is how to tell condensation from seepage from a leak, why exterior drainage comes before any coating, and what each product category can actually do.

A wet basement is almost never a wall problem. It is a water-management problem that shows up at the wall, and that distinction decides whether a weekend of work fixes it or whether you spend money twice.

Water reaches a basement wall by three routes, and each needs a different response. Understanding which one you have is the whole job — the products people reach for first, waterproofing paints and sealants, only address one of the three, and applied against the other two they fail visibly within a season.

⚡ Start here: Before buying anything, tape a 2-foot square of plastic sheet tightly to the bare wall and leave it 48 hours. Condensation on the room side means humid air, and a dehumidifier fixes it. Moisture under the plastic, against the wall means water is coming through the masonry, and that needs exterior drainage correction and an interior coating in that order. Getting this test wrong is how people end up painting a wall that was never leaking.

The Three Ways Water Gets In

1. Condensation

Warm humid air meets a cool masonry wall and gives up its moisture on the surface. The wall is wet, but nothing came through it. Basements do this every summer, and it is the single most misdiagnosed cause of a damp basement.

Signs: dampness that is worse in humid weather and better in winter, moisture spread evenly across the wall rather than concentrated at cracks or the floor joint, and a musty smell without visible staining or efflorescence.

Fix: a dehumidifier sized to the space, insulating cold water pipes, venting the dryer outside, and ensuring the space has some air movement. No coating required, and a vapour-impermeable coating will not help.

2. Capillary Seepage Through the Masonry

Water in the surrounding soil is drawn through concrete or block by capillary action — the same effect that pulls water up a paper towel. Concrete and concrete block are porous, and hydrostatic pressure from saturated soil drives water through the pore network.

Signs: damp patches that darken after rain, white efflorescence deposits, paint blistering off in sheets, and moisture concentrated low on the wall or at the wall-floor joint.

Fix: manage the water outside first (grading, gutters, downspout extensions), then a cementitious or elastomeric waterproof coating on the interior to resist what remains.

3. Bulk Water Through a Defect

Water entering through a crack, a failed mortar joint, a pipe penetration, a window well, or over the top of the foundation wall. This is not seepage; it is a leak with a location.

Signs: water appearing at one identifiable point, often during or immediately after heavy rain, sometimes running visibly.

Fix: repair the defect — hydraulic cement for active leaks, polyurethane injection for structural cracks — and correct whatever concentrates water at that spot. No coating fixes a leak.

🔍 Efflorescence Tells You Something: The white powdery bloom on a basement wall is salt left behind as water evaporates out of the masonry. Its presence proves water is moving through the wall, not condensing on it — which rules out condensation as the primary cause and points you at seepage.

Why Exterior Work Comes First

Interior coatings resist water pressure from the wrong side. A waterproof coating applied inside is holding back water that has already saturated the wall, and hydrostatic pressure works to push that coating off the substrate. Manufacturers rate their products for a specific pressure — commonly expressed in feet of water head — and exceeding it delaminates the coating.

Reducing the water reaching the wall lowers that pressure, and most of the effective reduction is cheap surface work rather than excavation:

  • Extend downspouts. A single downspout discharging at the foundation can deliver hundreds of gallons against one spot in a storm. Six feet of extension is the highest-value fix available and costs almost nothing.
  • Correct the grade. Soil should fall away from the wall at roughly 6 inches over the first 10 feet. Settled backfill that slopes back toward the house turns the foundation into a drain.
  • Clear the gutters. An overflowing gutter concentrates roof water exactly where you least want it.
  • Check window wells. They fill and hold water against the wall unless drained and covered.
  • Move irrigation and beds. Sprinkler heads and planting beds against the foundation keep the soil saturated year-round.

In many basements these five items resolve the problem entirely. They are also the prerequisite for any coating to survive.

The honest sequence for most houses: correct surface drainage, repair defects, then coat the interior. Full exterior excavation with a drainage membrane and footing drain is the permanent answer, and it is warranted when a basement floods repeatedly, when the foundation is structurally cracked, or when the interior approach has already failed on a properly prepared wall.

Interior drainage systems — a channel cut at the wall-floor joint feeding a sump — sit between the two. They do not stop water entering; they collect it and pump it out. For a basement with high water table pressure that no coating will hold back, this is often the realistic answer.

The Coating Types, and What Each Is For

Cementitious Waterproof Coatings

Portland cement based, often with acrylic or polymer modifiers, applied by brush at a heavy film build. The coating bonds into the pores of masonry and cures rigid, resisting water pressure from the negative (inside) side. This is the category most consumer basement waterproofing paints fall into, and it is the correct default for concrete block.

Requirements people miss: bare, unpainted, unsealed masonry, a damp substrate at application, two coats brushed rather than rolled to work material into the pores, and a stated coverage rate that is far heavier than ordinary paint. Applied thin, over paint, or on a dry wall, these products fail — and most negative reviews describe exactly one of those three errors.

Elastomeric and Acrylic Coatings

Flexible films that bridge hairline cracks and accommodate slight movement. Better than cementitious products where fine cracking is expected, less able to resist high hydrostatic pressure, and generally applied over a primed or previously coated surface.

Penetrating Silicate Sealers

Sodium or lithium silicate solutions that react with free lime in concrete to form insoluble crystals inside the pore structure. They densify the concrete and reduce capillary transmission without forming a film, which means they cannot peel and do not change appearance.

They handle capillary moisture well and do nothing for cracks or bulk water. Their advantage is permanence — there is no film to fail — and they can be applied where a coating would later need stripping.

Hydraulic Cement

A rapid-setting patching compound that expands slightly as it cures, which is what lets it seal against active water flow. It is the product for a hole or crack that is leaking right now. It is a repair material, not a waterproofing system, and it is brittle — a crack that moves seasonally will reopen alongside it.

Polyurethane and Epoxy Crack Injection

Injected into a poured-concrete crack through the full wall thickness. Polyurethane foams on contact with water and stays flexible, which suits cracks that move; epoxy cures rigid and restores structural strength. This is the correct fix for a leaking crack in a poured wall and it is well within DIY range with a kit.

How to Waterproof a Basement Wall, Step by Step

1. Fix the Outside First

Downspouts, grade, gutters, window wells. Then wait through a heavy rain and see what remains. A surprising number of basements need nothing further.

2. Strip the Wall Back to Bare Masonry

Waterproof coatings bond to masonry, not to paint. Any existing paint has to come off — wire brushing, a paint scraper, or grinding with a dust shroud. If old paint is peeling, it is telling you water is already pushing it off; leaving it under a new coating guarantees the same result.

3. Remove Efflorescence and Contamination

Wire brush the deposits off dry first, then wash. A proprietary efflorescence remover handles what remains. Do not use muriatic acid unless you are prepared to neutralise it thoroughly — residual acid ruins coating adhesion.

4. Repair Cracks and Joints

Hydraulic cement for active leaks and the wall-floor cove joint. Polyurethane injection for cracks in poured walls. Repoint failed mortar joints in block walls and let new mortar cure before coating.

5. Dampen the Wall

Counter-intuitive and important: cementitious waterproofers are applied to a damp surface. A dry, thirsty wall pulls water out of the coating before it cures, leaving it weak and powdery. Mist the wall so it is damp but not running.

6. Brush On Two Heavy Coats

Use a stiff masonry brush and work the first coat into the pores in a circular motion rather than laying it on in strokes. The goal is to fill the pore structure, not to cover the colour. Apply the second coat at right angles to the first once the label's recoat window is reached — usually a few hours.

Watch the coverage rate. These products specify something like 75–100 square feet per gallon per coat, against 350–400 for wall paint. If a gallon is covering an entire wall, the film is far too thin to resist anything.

7. Cure Before Testing

Most cementitious systems need several days to reach full properties, and some specify light misting during cure. Judge the result after a heavy rain, not the next morning.

⚠️ The Most Common Failure: Applying a waterproof coating over old paint, on a dry wall, at paint-like coverage. All three defeat the mechanism the product depends on — bonding into open masonry pores at a heavy film build. A coating that fails this way peels in sheets within a season and takes the old paint with it.

Concrete Block Walls Specifically

Block walls behave differently from poured concrete and deserve their own note. A block wall is a grid of hollow cells, and water entering anywhere can travel through those cavities and emerge some distance from where it got in — which is why a leak in a block basement often appears nowhere near its source.

Two consequences. First, tracing a block-wall leak by looking at where it appears inside is unreliable; look outside at grade, downspouts and the top of the wall instead. Second, the mortar joints are the weakest path, so repointing failed joints matters more on block than on poured concrete.

Cementitious waterproof coatings suit block well because the porous surface gives them something to key into. Cores that are actively carrying water may need drainage rather than coating — a persistent flow from a block cavity is a case for an interior drainage system.

Two product families dominate the consumer shelf. Drylok is the best-known cementitious/latex masonry waterproofer and is a reasonable choice on bare block, applied per its stated coverage; the complaints it attracts almost always describe application over paint or at insufficient film build. Flex Seal and similar rubberised coatings are surface films with no masonry pore penetration and no negative-side hydrostatic rating — they are patching products for above-grade surfaces, and using them on a below-grade basement wall is outside what they are designed to do.

For foundation waterproofing on the exterior, liquid rubber and elastomeric membranes applied to the excavated wall are a legitimate category, and they work because they sit on the positive (water) side where pressure pushes them onto the substrate rather than off it.

The Short Version

Diagnose before you buy. The plastic-sheet test separates condensation from seepage in 48 hours and costs nothing, and condensation — which needs a dehumidifier, not a coating — is the most commonly misdiagnosed cause.

Fix the outside first. Downspout extensions, grading, gutters and window wells resolve a large share of damp basements outright and are the precondition for any interior coating to survive.

Then coat, correctly: bare masonry, damp surface, two heavy brushed coats at the stated coverage. Most reported failures of basement waterproofing paint are one of those three conditions not being met rather than a bad product.

And know when to stop. Repeated flooding, structural cracking, or a genuine high water table are not coating problems — they need drainage, and no amount of product on the inside face will substitute.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my basement has condensation or a leak?

Tape a two-foot square of plastic sheet tightly to the bare wall and leave it 48 hours. Moisture on the room side of the plastic means condensation — humid air giving up moisture on a cool wall — and the fix is a dehumidifier, pipe insulation and venting, not a coating. Moisture underneath, against the masonry, means water is coming through the wall and needs drainage correction plus an interior treatment. Other tells: condensation is worse in humid summer weather and spread evenly across the wall, while seepage darkens after rain, concentrates low on the wall or at the floor joint, and often leaves white efflorescence. Efflorescence is conclusive — it is salt deposited as water evaporates out of the masonry, so it proves water is moving through.

Should I waterproof my basement from the inside or the outside?

Outside is the permanent fix and inside is the practical one, but the sequence matters more than the choice. Exterior excavation with a drainage membrane and footing drain stops water reaching the wall at all, costs $10,000–$30,000, and is warranted for repeated flooding or structural cracking. Interior coatings resist water at the inside face, cost $300–$2,000 DIY, and work well once the water load is reduced. What makes interior work succeed is doing the cheap exterior surface work first: extending downspouts, correcting the grade to fall roughly 6 inches over 10 feet, clearing gutters and draining window wells. Those five items resolve many damp basements outright and are the precondition for any coating to survive hydrostatic pressure.

Why did my basement waterproofing paint peel off?

Almost always one of three application errors rather than a bad product. First, it was applied over existing paint — waterproof coatings bond into open masonry pores, and over paint they can only bond to the paint, which the water then pushes off. Second, the wall was dry; cementitious waterproofers are applied to a damp surface, because a dry wall pulls water out of the coating before it cures and leaves it weak and powdery. Third, it went on too thin — these products specify roughly 75–100 square feet per gallon per coat against 350–400 for wall paint, so if a gallon covered a whole wall the film cannot resist anything. Strip back to bare masonry, dampen it, and brush two heavy coats working the material into the pores.

Does Flex Seal work on basement walls?

It is outside what the product is designed for. Flex Seal and similar rubberised coatings are surface films: they do not penetrate masonry pores and they carry no negative-side hydrostatic pressure rating, which is the specification that matters on a below-grade wall. Water pushing through from the soil side works to lift any film that is only adhered to the surface. They are reasonable for patching above-grade surfaces, gutters and flashing where they are sealing against water running over them rather than pressure pushing through. For a basement wall, use a cementitious masonry waterproofer on bare damp block, hydraulic cement for active leaks, or polyurethane injection for a leaking crack in a poured wall.

What is the best sealant for basement walls?

It depends which problem you have, which is why a single answer misleads. For general seepage through block or concrete, a cementitious masonry waterproofer applied in two heavy brushed coats on bare, damp masonry is the default and the most widely used category. For capillary damp where you do not want a film that could later peel, a penetrating sodium or lithium silicate sealer reacts with free lime in the concrete to densify the pore structure permanently. For an active leak at a crack or the wall-floor cove joint, hydraulic cement is the right material because it expands slightly as it sets and can seal against flowing water. For a leaking crack in a poured wall, polyurethane injection through the full wall thickness is the correct repair.

How do you waterproof concrete block walls?

The same sequence as any basement wall, with two differences that matter. A block wall is a grid of hollow cells, so water entering anywhere can travel through the cavities and emerge some distance away — which means tracing a leak by where it shows inside is unreliable, and you should look outside at grade, downspouts and the top of the wall instead. And the mortar joints are the weakest path, so repointing any failed joints matters more on block than on poured concrete. Otherwise: fix exterior drainage first, strip to bare masonry, remove efflorescence, repoint and patch, dampen the wall, then brush two heavy coats of cementitious waterproofer, working it into the porous surface. Block takes these coatings well because there is plenty for them to key into.

How much does basement waterproofing cost?

It spans two orders of magnitude depending on what the problem actually is. Downspout extensions and regrading run $50–$400 DIY or $500–$2,500 professionally, and often solve the problem alone. Injecting a leaking crack is $60–$150 with a DIY kit or $400–$800 professionally. Coating roughly 800 square feet of wall with a cementitious waterproofer costs $300–$700 in materials, or $2,000–$5,000 installed. An interior drainage system with a sump runs $4,000–$12,000, and full exterior excavation with a membrane and footing drain $10,000–$30,000 or more. The ordering matters more than any figure: cheap drainage work done first is what makes the coating above it last.

Can I waterproof a basement wall that already has paint on it?

Not effectively — the paint has to come off first. Cementitious waterproofers work by bonding into the open pore structure of masonry, and over an existing paint film they can only adhere to that film. Water pushing through from the soil side then lifts the paint and takes your new coating with it, usually within a season. If the existing paint is already blistering or peeling, that is direct evidence water is pushing it off and the same will happen again. Remove it by wire brushing, scraping, or grinding with a dust shroud, then clean off efflorescence, repair cracks, dampen the surface and coat. If full removal is impractical, a penetrating silicate sealer on the exposed bare areas or an interior drainage system are the realistic alternatives.
