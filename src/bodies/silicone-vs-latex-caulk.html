Silicone vs Latex Caulk — Which One Goes Where

Silicone is the better sealant and the worse neighbour: it stays waterproof for decades and nothing, including paint and new caulk, will stick to it afterwards. Two questions settle the choice — will the joint get wet, and will it get painted?

Use 100% silicone where a joint will be wet and never painted — tubs, showers, sinks, glass, tile and metal. Use acrylic latex where a joint will be painted and stays dry — interior trim, baseboards, crown moulding and drywall seams. Getting it backwards causes most caulking complaints: latex in a shower goes soft and mouldy within a year or two, and silicone on painted trim leaves a shiny strip that paint refuses to cover.

The awkward joints are the ones in between: exterior trim that gets rain and paint, window perimeters where wood meets brick, siding butt joints that move with the seasons. For those, neither plain silicone nor plain latex is the best answer. A siliconized acrylic handles painted joints with modest movement, and a hybrid polymer (MS polymer) sealant gives silicone-class flexibility and weather resistance while still accepting paint. And silicone is close to a one-way decision: once it has been on a surface, its residue stops paint and most other caulks bonding there until it is removed completely.

⚡ The quick answer: Wet and unpainted (shower, tub, sink, glass, tile, metal) — 100% silicone. Dry and painted (interior trim, baseboards, drywall gaps) — acrylic latex. Painted and exposed to weather (exterior trim, siding, window casings) — siliconized acrylic for small, stable joints; hybrid polymer for joints that move or face hard weather. Never put silicone anywhere that will be painted, and never caulk over old silicone without removing every trace of it first.

What Is in Each Tube

Acrylic Latex

Acrylic latex — often sold as painter's caulk — is a suspension of acrylic polymer particles, fillers and pigment in water. It cures the way latex paint dries: the water evaporates, the particles pack together and fuse into a film. The dried bead is essentially a thick, flexible layer of acrylic paint — cheap, low-odour, and something paint bonds to readily.

The same mechanism sets its limits: a bead that dries by losing water shrinks, stretches only a little, and can re-wet in standing water.

100% Silicone

Silicone sealant is a silicone polymer — a backbone of alternating silicon and oxygen atoms — that cures by reacting with moisture in the air into a rubber. That backbone is very stable under ultraviolet light and temperature extremes, and the cured rubber barely changes over decades. Most good silicones meet ASTM C920, the standard for elastomeric joint sealants.

There are two common cure types. Acetoxy silicones release acetic acid as they cure, which is the vinegar smell. They cure quickly and bond very well to glass, but the acid can corrode bare metals such as copper and brass, attack mirror backing, and etch or stain some natural stone and masonry. Neutral-cure silicones (alkoxy or oxime) release alcohol or other non-acidic by-products, have little odour, and are the safer choice on metal, mirrors, stone and masonry. Kitchen-and-bath silicones add a mildewcide.

The trait that matters most for this comparison is surface energy. Cured silicone is extremely low-energy and slippery at the molecular level — which is why water beads on it, and why paint and most other sealants cannot bond to it.

Flexibility and Joint Movement

Every joint between two different materials moves. Wood swells with humidity, aluminium and vinyl expand with heat, and a window frame shifts against brick as the sun crosses the wall. Manufacturers rate a caulk's movement capability: the percentage of the joint's width the cured bead can repeatedly stretch and compress without failing.

The difference here is not subtle. Typical acrylic latex is rated at roughly ±5%. On a 1/4-inch joint that is about 1/80 inch — fine for interior trim, nowhere near enough for most exterior joints. Siliconized acrylics roughly double it. Silicone sealants made to ASTM C920 are rated Class 25 at minimum (±25%), and many are Class 35 or Class 50 — five to ten times the movement of painter's caulk.

That is why painter's caulk on exterior trim is so often cracked down the middle by the second winter.

💡 Joint design matters as much as the tube. A sealant's movement rating assumes a properly shaped bead — roughly twice as wide as it is deep, bonded to the two sides of the joint but not the back. In a deep gap, push in foam backer rod first; a bead bonded to three sides of a deep slot cannot stretch, and even silicone tears. Our exterior window caulking guide walks through sizing backer rod and tooling the bead.

Water Resistance and Mould

Silicone is waterproof in the full sense: it does not absorb water or soften when submerged, which is why aquariums are built with it. In a shower it sits wet daily for years without losing its bond to tile, porcelain or acrylic.

Acrylic latex is water-resistant once fully cured, which is different. It sheds rain on painted trim well, but in a joint that is wet for hours every day it can soften, whiten, lose adhesion and wash out. Kitchen-and-bath acrylic with mildewcide is fine for splash zones such as a vanity-to-wall joint, but not for a tub-to-tile seam.

Mould Resistance

No caulk is mould-proof. Mould feeds on soap scum and organic film on the surface, not on the caulk itself. Kitchen-and-bath formulas add a mildewcide that suppresses growth for some years before it depletes.

Silicone has a specific weakness here: it is slightly permeable, and mould that establishes itself on the surface can grow into the rubber. The black spots you cannot scrub out of an old shower bead are inside it, and the fix is replacement. A squeegee and a running extractor fan do more for a bead's life than any formula. If the grout next to the bead is staining too, sealing it slows the same process; our grout sealer guide covers which products suit showers.

Paintability

This is the property that decides most choices, and the answer is binary. 100% silicone cannot be painted. Latex and oil paint alike crawl away from cured silicone into beads and fish-eyes, and whatever does stay on peels off in strips with a fingernail. No primer reliably fixes this.

Acrylic latex and siliconized acrylic paint beautifully, usually within 30 minutes to two hours. In siliconized versions the silicone is an additive, not the binder, so they take paint like any latex caulk.

Be careful with the words "paintable silicone" on the front of a tube. Products sold that way are almost never 100% silicone; they are siliconized acrylics or hybrid polymers. Read the chemistry line on the back: if it says 100% silicone, it will not take paint.

Adhesion: What Each Sticks To

Silicone has outstanding adhesion to non-porous, smooth, inorganic surfaces: glass, glazed ceramic tile, porcelain, enamelled steel tubs, aluminium and most metals. That is its home territory, and on those surfaces it is the best sealant you can buy.

It is a much weaker choice on bare wood, which is porous and moves with moisture, and on painted surfaces, where the bond is only as good as the paint underneath — when the joint moves, silicone often pulls the paint film off with it. It also bonds poorly to polyethylene and polypropylene plastics.

Acrylic latex is the opposite. It grips porous and painted surfaces well — wood, drywall, plaster, primed and painted trim, masonry — because it soaks slightly into them the way paint does. Its bond to glass and glazed tile weakens under constant water. Hybrid polymers are the widest-ranging of the four: they bond to wood, masonry, metal, vinyl, painted surfaces and glass, and many tolerate damp surfaces.

UV, Weather and Lifespan

Silicone is extremely resistant to UV, ozone and temperature cycling. Unpainted silicone on a sunny wall or skylight stays flexible for decades, and premium silicones advertise 20-year to lifetime durability. Indoors in a shower, the limiting factor is mould rather than ageing, and replacement every five to ten years is normal.

Acrylic latex is far less UV-stable on its own. Exterior acrylic caulk left unpainted chalks, hardens and cracks within a few years; under paint it lasts much longer, because the paint film shields it. Treat "35-year" or "50-year" label ratings as a ranking within a brand's line-up, not a forecast. In practice, painter's caulk on an exterior joint that moves often lasts only two to five years; a good siliconized acrylic on a well-built, painted joint can go eight to fifteen.

Application, Cleanup and Cure Times

Working With Each

Acrylic latex tools smoothly with a damp finger, wipes away with a wet rag, and paint hides a slightly uneven bead. Silicone skins within minutes, smears an invisible film onto everything it touches, and cannot be painted to disguise errors. Mask both sides with painter's tape, tool the bead in one pass with a finger dipped in soapy water, and pull the tape before it skins.

Temperature limits differ too. Most acrylic latex caulks need surface and air temperatures of at least 40°F and protection from freezing until dry. Many silicones and hybrids can be applied well below that, which suits winter repairs.

Cleanup

Uncured latex and siliconized acrylic clean up with water. Uncured silicone just smears in water: wipe the bulk off with a dry rag, then clean residue with mineral spirits or isopropyl alcohol. Hybrids generally clean up with mineral spirits while wet.

Dry and Cure Times

Acrylics dry by evaporation: touch-dry in 15–30 minutes, paintable in 30 minutes to two hours, safe from rain after about 24 hours, and fully cured in one to two weeks. Silicone skins in 10–30 minutes and typically needs 24 hours before it gets wet (less for fast-cure formulas), but it cures inward at roughly 1/8 inch per day, so a deep bead stays soft underneath for days. Our full guide to how long caulk takes to dry breaks those milestones down by chemistry, bead depth and weather.

Removal and the Silicone Residue Problem

Acrylic latex is easy to remove. Score along each edge with a utility knife, pull the bead out with a hook tool, and soften stubborn sections with warm water or a heat gun on low.

Silicone is harder in two ways. The first is mechanical: it comes out of glass and tile in rubbery fragments. Cut along both edges with a sharp razor, pull out what you can, then apply a silicone caulk remover gel to what remains and leave it the full time on the label before scraping again with a plastic scraper.

The second problem is the one people miss. Even when every visible scrap is gone, silicone leaves an invisible film of low-surface-energy residue behind — silicone oils and microscopic remnants of rubber. Acrylic caulk, hybrids and paint will not bond properly to it; they bead, fish-eye or peel away weeks later, and even new silicone bonds weakly. It is the usual reason a re-caulked tub seam fails in its first year.

Getting the residue off is a cleaning job, not a scraping job:

  • Scrape and remover first, as above, until nothing is visible or rubbery to the touch.
  • Scrub with a non-scratch pad and mineral spirits or isopropyl alcohol, turning clean rags often so you lift the film rather than spread it.
  • Test with water. If water beads tightly where the old caulk was and sheets out elsewhere, residue remains; clean again.
  • On wood that is going to be painted, sanding through the contaminated surface layer is often more reliable than solvent alone,, then prime. Let everything dry before re-caulking.

The Middle Options: Siliconized Acrylic and Hybrid Polymer

Siliconized Acrylic

Siliconized acrylic is the workhorse for painted joints that see some weather. It keeps everything good about latex — water cleanup, easy tooling, fast paintability, strong adhesion to wood and paint — and roughly doubles the movement capability, with better water resistance.

It is the right choice for interior trim in humid rooms, small and stable exterior trim joints, and painted window and door casings. It falls short on large or high-movement joints — wood to masonry, anything over about 1/4 inch on a sun-facing wall — and in constant water.

Hybrid Polymer (MS Polymer)

Hybrid sealants are the closest thing to a product that does everything. They cure by moisture like silicone, typically carry ASTM C920 movement ratings around ±25–35%, resist UV and weather well, bond to a very wide range of materials including damp surfaces, and — unlike silicone — can usually be painted the same day.

The trade-offs are price, a stiff paste that takes practice to tool neatly, mineral-spirit cleanup, and removal nearly as hard as silicone. Most accept latex paint well, but some caution against certain alkyd paints, so check the data sheet. For painted exterior joints that move, they are usually the best choice — which is why our exterior caulk guide points to hybrids for wood-to-masonry joints and siding.

💡 A useful rule of thumb: if you would have used latex but the joint is outdoors and moves, step up to siliconized acrylic. If you would have used silicone but the joint has to be painted, step across to a hybrid.

Where to Use Each

Bathrooms

100% silicone (kitchen-and-bath, with mildewcide) for the tub-to-tile seam, shower corners and change-of-plane joints in tile, shower trays and basins. Acrylic latex or siliconized acrylic for painted trim, door casings and the joint between a vanity backsplash and a painted wall.

Kitchens

Silicone around sinks, including undermount sinks, and where countertops meet the backsplash. On marble and other natural stone, use a silicone labelled stone-safe or non-staining. Siliconized acrylic for the gaps between painted cabinets and walls, and along painted crown and toe-kick trim.

Windows

Exterior window perimeters are the classic in-between joint. Where a painted wood casing meets painted siding, use siliconized acrylic for a small, stable gap and a hybrid where the gap is larger or the wall faces the sun. Where an aluminium, vinyl or fibreglass frame meets brick or stone and nothing will be painted, neutral-cure silicone or a hybrid is the long-life answer. Many window makers specify the sealant in their instructions, and following that protects the warranty. Our step-by-step window caulking guide covers removal, backer rod and tooling.

Trim and Baseboards

Interior baseboard, crown, casing and chair rail are the one place plain acrylic latex is clearly best: cheap, easy to tool, paintable in under an hour, and the joints barely move. Step up to siliconized acrylic in humid rooms. Never use silicone on trim.

Siding

Painted wood and fibre-cement siding butt joints and trim joints move with temperature and moisture and plain latex usually cracks there within a season or two. Use a high-performance siliconized acrylic or hybrid, and check the siding manufacturer's installation instructions — fibre-cement makers specify the sealant standard they require. Vinyl siding laps and J-channel generally should not be caulked at all, since it must move freely.

Glass, Mirrors and Metal

Silicone, without question, for glass-to-glass, glass-to-metal and metal joints that will not be painted — shower screens, skylights, aquariums (aquarium-safe product only), gutters and flashing. Use neutral-cure silicone on mirrors, which acetoxy cure can damage from the back, and on copper, brass and galvanised metal, which the acid can corrode.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is silicone or latex caulk better?

Neither overall; they suit opposite jobs. 100% silicone is better where a joint is wet and unpainted — showers, tubs, sinks, glass, tile and metal — because it is waterproof, very flexible and UV-stable. Acrylic latex is better where a joint is painted and dry, such as interior trim, because it paints within an hour, cleans up with water and costs far less. For painted joints exposed to weather, siliconized acrylic or a hybrid polymer usually beats both.

Can you paint over silicone caulk?

Not 100% silicone. Paint cannot wet its low-energy surface, so it crawls into beads and fish-eyes and peels off what little sticks, and no primer reliably fixes it. If a joint needs paint, use acrylic latex, siliconized acrylic or a hybrid. Tubes sold as "paintable silicone" are generally siliconized acrylics or hybrids — check the chemistry on the back.

Can I put new caulk over old silicone?

No. Acrylic caulk, hybrids and paint will not bond to silicone or its invisible residue, and even new silicone over old bonds weakly. Cut out the old bead, use a remover gel on what is left, then scrub with mineral spirits or isopropyl alcohol until water no longer beads differently on that strip. Let it dry before re-caulking.

Which caulk is best for a shower or bathtub?

A 100% silicone labelled for kitchen and bath, which contains a mildewcide. It stays waterproof and flexible under daily soaking and bonds well to tile, porcelain, enamel and acrylic. Allow at least 24 hours before showering, longer for a deep bead. Expect to replace it every five to ten years, as mould that grows into silicone cannot be cleaned out.

What is siliconized acrylic caulk?

Acrylic latex caulk with a small amount of silicone added for flexibility, adhesion and water resistance. It is still water-based, cleans up with water and takes paint quickly. Movement ratings of roughly ±10–12.5% are about double basic painter's caulk but well short of silicone, so it suits painted exterior trim with small, stable joints.

Should I use silicone or latex caulk around exterior windows?

Usually neither in its plain form. Where painted wood casing meets painted siding, use siliconized acrylic for a small gap or a hybrid for a larger or sun-facing one. Where an aluminium, vinyl or fibreglass frame meets unpainted brick or stone, use neutral-cure silicone or a hybrid. Check the window maker's installation instructions first.

How do you tell if old caulk is silicone or latex?

Silicone is rubbery, slightly slippery and stretches before it tears; old latex is firmer and crumbles. A painted bead with sound paint is almost certainly acrylic, while a shiny bead with flaking paint is likely silicone, and water beads tightly on silicone. If in doubt, treat it as silicone and clean the residue thoroughly after removal.
