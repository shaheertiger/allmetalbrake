How to Stain a Deck: Step-by-Step DIY Guide

Brushing on the stain is the easy afternoon. Whether the finish lasts one season or four is decided earlier — by the weather you pick, how clean and dry the wood is, and how much stain you leave sitting on top.

To stain a deck, you repair loose fasteners and damaged boards, clean the wood (or strip it if an old film-forming stain is peeling), neutralise it with a brightener, let it dry for two to three days, sand any mill glaze or raised fibres, then apply a penetrating stain in thin coats — railings first, then the floor two or three boards at a time from one end to the other — and keep everything off it until it has cured.

Nearly every stain failure — peeling in the first winter, blotchy colour, a surface that stays sticky — traces back to preparation or application rather than the product in the can. The application is one afternoon; the work that decides how long it lasts happens in the days before.

⚡ The short version: Stain when the air is 50–90°F (ideally 60–80°F), the deck is out of direct midday sun, and no rain is forecast for 24–48 hours. The wood must pass the water-drop test — droplets soak in within a few minutes rather than beading. Clean, brighten, wait 48–72 hours, sand to no finer than 80 grit, then apply thin coats and back-brush everything. Oil stains usually want the second coat wet-on-wet; water-based stains are recoated once the first is dry, inside the label's window. Furniture goes back after 48–72 hours.

When to Stain: Weather and Wood

The Weather Window

Most deck stains list an application range of roughly 50–90°F, and the sweet spot is 60–80°F. Below 50°F, water-based stains may not form properly and oils take days to dry, collecting dust and footprints in the meantime. Above 90°F — or on boards baking in direct sun, which can run 20–30°F hotter than the air — the stain flashes off before it soaks in, leaving lap marks and a thin, uneven film. Our guide to what temperature to stain a deck goes through the edge cases, including overnight lows and dew.

In practice: stain in the morning after the dew has gone or in late-afternoon shade, avoid midday sun on south- and west-facing decks, and make sure no rain is forecast for at least 24 hours with a water-based stain and 48 hours with an oil. Late spring and early autumn are the best seasons in most of the country.

The Water-Drop Test

Wood has to be dry and open enough to absorb stain, and the calendar is a poor guide to whether it is. The test takes two minutes: sprinkle a handful of water on several boards, including a shaded one and one near the house. If the water soaks in within a few minutes and darkens the wood, the wood is ready. If it beads up and sits there, either the wood is still too wet, it has mill glaze, or there is an old sealer or stain on it that needs to come off.

If you own a pin moisture meter, the target is below roughly 15% moisture content, and some stain makers specify 12%. Readings above that mean the stain cannot penetrate, because the cells are already full of water — and whatever sits on the surface will peel.

New Pressure-Treated Lumber

Standard pressure-treated lumber leaves the treatment plant saturated with water-borne preservative, and it can feel heavy and damp even at the lumberyard. It needs time to dry out before it will accept stain — anywhere from a few weeks to several months, depending on how wet it was, your climate and how much sun the deck gets. Do not stain on a schedule; run the water-drop test every few weeks and stain when it passes. Lumber stamped KDAT (kiln-dried after treatment) has already been dried and can usually be stained much sooner. If you are choosing a product for new boards, see the best deck stain for pressure-treated wood.

Cedar and redwood are not treated and are usually dry within a few weeks, but often need mill glaze dealt with first (Step 5).

The applicators matter more than people expect; the right one is the difference between an even finish and a streaky one.

Beyond applicators, the shopping list is:

  • Deck cleaner (oxygen-based for weathered wood) or stripper if old film stain is peeling — see the best deck cleaner
  • Wood brightener (oxalic or citric acid based)
  • A stiff synthetic scrub brush on a pole, and a garden hose with a spray nozzle or a pressure washer you can turn down
  • 60–80 grit sandpaper or sanding screens, a pole sander, and a random-orbit sander for rails
  • Hammer, nail set, and exterior deck screws one size longer than the nails they replace
  • Plastic sheeting and painter's tape to protect siding, door thresholds and plants
  • A paint tray or bucket for the pad, a stir stick, and a spare empty bucket for boxing cans
  • Chemical-resistant gloves, safety glasses, and old clothes you will throw away
  • A metal container with a lid for used rags (Step 9)

For how much stain to buy, coverage on the label is typically 150–300 square feet per gallon per coat, but weathered or rough-sawn wood drinks far more than smooth new boards. Measure the floor, add roughly 30% for railings and stairs, and divide by the low end of the label's coverage figure. Step 1: Repairs

Do repairs first, because sanding, cleaning and staining all go better on a sound surface, and any new board needs to go through the same cleaning cycle as the rest.

Fasteners. Walk the deck slowly and look for nail heads standing proud. Drive popped nails back down with a nail set so the heads sit just below the surface. Nails that pop repeatedly have lost their grip; pull them and replace them with exterior-rated deck screws slightly longer than the nail, driven into fresh wood beside the old hole. Countersink screw heads just below the surface so they do not catch the stain pad or a bare foot.

Boards. Probe suspect areas — around posts, at board ends, near downspouts and planters — with a screwdriver. If it sinks in easily, that wood is rotten and the board needs replacing. Replace boards that are split along their length, badly cupped, or soft. Use the same species and treatment as the existing deck. New wood will not match the colour of the old after cleaning; a semi-transparent stain narrows the difference, and it evens out further over a season.

Step 2: Clean or Strip

Stain over dirt, mildew and grey weathered fibres bonds to them — and comes off with them. Which product you need depends on what is already on the deck.

Cleaning: Bare, Weathered or Faded Penetrating Stain

If the deck is bare and grey, or its last transparent or semi-transparent stain has simply faded, you need a cleaner — normally an oxygen-based one (sodium percarbonate), which lifts dirt, grey fibres and mildew without harming the wood. Avoid chlorine bleach, which whitens mildew without reliably removing it and degrades surface fibres. Wet the deck, apply the cleaner, keep it wet for the label's dwell time (typically 10–15 minutes), scrub along the grain, and rinse thoroughly, working in sections so nothing dries on.

Stripping: Peeling or Film-Forming Stain

If the old finish is a solid or semi-solid stain, or anything peeling, a cleaner will not remove it — and new stain over a failing film fails with it. That calls for a caustic stripper, usually sodium hydroxide-based, scrubbed and rinsed away. The full process is in our guide to how to strip deck stain. A useful rule: if you can scratch the old finish off with a fingernail, or water beads on boards that look clean, plan to strip.

Pressure Washer or Garden Hose?

A pressure washer speeds up rinsing, but it is also how a lot of decks get damaged. On softwoods like pine, cedar and redwood, keep it to roughly 500–1,200 psi, use a 25° or 40° fan tip, hold it a foot from the surface, and move it along the grain without stopping. Too much pressure or too close a tip gouges the soft earlywood and raises a carpet of fuzz that then has to be sanded off. A stiff brush and a garden hose with a strong nozzle do the job more slowly but with no risk; let the chemistry do the work rather than the water.

Step 3: Brighten

Brightener is the step people skip, and it is the difference between a deck that looks freshly stained and one that just looks darker. It is an acid, usually oxalic or citric, applied to the wet wood straight after cleaning or stripping. It neutralises the alkaline cleaner or stripper, which can otherwise interfere with how stain penetrates and cures; it removes tannin stains and the grey-black iron marks around nails; and it restores the wood's natural colour, which matters most on cedar and redwood.

Spray it on the still-wet deck, leave it 10–15 minutes, scrub lightly and rinse. On cedar the change can be startling, and that brightened colour is the base your stain sits on — worth keeping in mind when reading our best stain for cedar decks guide.

💡 Protect what is around the deck. Wet down plants, lawn and shrubs before you apply cleaner, stripper or brightener, and rinse them again afterwards. Cover them with plastic only briefly — under a sheet on a hot day they will cook. Keep pets and children away until everything has been rinsed.

Step 4: Let It Dry

After the final rinse, the wood is saturated, and it needs to dry out before it will take stain. Allow at least 48 hours of dry weather, and 72 hours if it is cool, humid or the deck is in shade. If it rains in the meantime, the clock restarts.

Do not trust appearance. Boards dry on the surface within hours and look ready the same afternoon, while the inside is still wet. Before you open a can, repeat the water-drop test on several boards — especially those in shade and those close to the house, which dry last. If you have a moisture meter, check for readings below about 15%. Staining wet wood is the single most common cause of a new finish peeling within months, and it is entirely avoidable with a little patience.

Step 5: Sand Mill Glaze and Fuzz

Not every deck needs sanding, but two situations do. Mill glaze is a smooth, compressed layer left on new planed lumber by the planer knives. It resists absorption and shows up as beading in the water-drop test on new, dry, unfinished boards. Cleaning and brightening often remove it; where they do not, a light sanding opens the surface. Fuzz is the raised fibres left by stripping or pressure washing, particularly on cedar and pine. It soaks up stain unevenly and breaks off underfoot, taking the stain with it.

Sand once the deck is completely dry, with 60–80 grit and no finer. A deck is not furniture: finer grits polish and close the surface, reducing how much stain it can absorb. A pole sander or a floor buffer with a sanding screen handles the boards; a random-orbit sander handles the rails. Then vacuum or blow the dust out of the gaps, or the brush will drag it into the stain.

Step 6: Choose and Test the Stain

Product choice is covered in our comparison of the best deck stains, plus the cedar and pressure-treated guides linked above. Three points affect how you apply it. Opacity: semi-transparent penetrating stains wear away gradually rather than peeling and are the best balance for most deck floors; solid stains behave more like paint and eventually peel in traffic lanes. Base: oils soak in deeply and forgive lap marks; water-based stains dry fast and need a dry window between coats (Step 8). Compatibility: a penetrating stain cannot soak through an existing film, so over an old solid stain you either strip or stay with a film-forming product.

Test the colour on an inconspicuous board and let it dry fully — the can and the card are never what you get on your wood. Then box the stain: mix all the cans together in one large bucket so batch differences do not show up as a colour shift halfway across the deck. Stir again regularly as you work; pigment settles.

Step 7: Apply the First Coat

Prepare the Area

Mask the siding where the deck meets the house, door thresholds and any stone you want kept clean, and lay plastic on the ground under the railings. Clear the deck completely and plan a route that ends at the stairs so you do not stain yourself into a corner.

Order of Work

Railings first, then the floor. Drips from the railings land on the floor below; if the floor is not yet stained, they disappear under the floor coat instead of drying as dark spots on finished boards. Work top down — cap rail, balusters, posts, bottom rail — and brush all four faces of each baluster. With a pump sprayer, mist a few balusters at a time and brush them out immediately.

Then the boards, two or three at a time, end to end. A lap mark is the darker stripe where wet stain overlaps stain that has started to dry. Stain a patch in the middle of the deck and every edge of it becomes a potential lap mark. Stain two or three boards along their entire length instead, then move to the next two or three, and the only edges are the gaps between boards, which hide any overlap. Keep a wet edge and never stop halfway down a run.

Stairs last — treads, risers, stringers and stair ends — as you work your way off the deck.

Technique

Load the pad or brush, let the excess drip off, and apply in long strokes along the grain. The goal is a coat the wood absorbs, not one that sits on top. Use a brush to push stain into board edges and gaps.

Seal the end grain. Board ends absorb water like a bundle of straws, which is why decks crack and rot from the ends first. Give every exposed end a generous brushed-in coat.

Back-brush everything. Whether the stain went on by roller, sprayer or pad, go over it with a brush while it is wet. That works stain into the grain, evens out puddles and catches missed spots; unbrushed sprayed or rolled stain sits on the surface and wears unevenly.

Wipe off what does not soak in. Many oil stains say to wipe off anything still on the surface after 20–30 minutes. Pooled stain dries tacky, collects dirt and peels — especially on dense or new wood.

💡 Follow the shade. Start on the shaded side of the deck and move with it. Even on a 75°F day a sunlit board can pass 100°F, and stain on a hot board dries before you can keep a wet edge.

Step 8: The Second Coat

Whether you need a second coat, and when to apply it, depends on the base of the stain — and getting this wrong is a common reason for a sticky or peeling finish.

Oil-Based Penetrating Stains: Wet-on-Wet

Many oil-based semi-transparent stains call for a wet-on-wet second coat: you apply the second coat to a section while the first is still wet, typically within 20–60 minutes, before it has started to dry. The two coats soak in together. If you wait until the first coat has dried, the wood is already filled, the second coat cannot penetrate, and it sits on top as a sticky film that later peels. Some oil stains on dense or new wood are intended as one coat only. Read the label and do exactly what it says — this varies by product more than anything else in the process.

In practice, wet-on-wet means working in manageable sections: first coat on a run of boards, move on to the next run, then come back and apply the second coat to the first run before it dries.

Water-Based Stains: The Recoat Window

Water-based stains are recoated after the first coat is dry to the touch, commonly 2–4 hours in good conditions. Many also have an upper limit — a recoat window — after which the second coat bonds less well. Apply the second coat in the same order and the same end-to-end runs as the first. The full timelines for different stain types are in our guide to deck stain drying time.

Step 9: Cleanup and Rag Safety

Water-based stains clean up from brushes and tools with warm, soapy water. Oil-based stains need mineral spirits or the thinner named on the label, followed by soap and water. Clean brushes immediately; stain left in the bristles overnight usually ruins them. Pour leftover stain back into a can, seal it, and label it with the colour and the date — you will want it for touch-ups.

⚠️ Oily rags can catch fire on their own. Rags, pads and paper towels soaked with oil-based stain — especially products containing linseed or other drying oils — generate heat as the oil cures. Bundled in a pile, in a bin or in a closed bag, that heat cannot escape and can build until the rags ignite, sometimes hours later. Spread used rags flat outdoors to dry fully, away from the house, or put them in a metal container filled with water and sealed with a lid. Dispose of them according to your local hazardous-waste rules. Never leave them in a heap in the garage.

Remove masking tape before the stain cures fully, so it does not pull the finish with it. Take the plastic off plants as soon as you are done, and rinse off any drips on concrete or stone immediately.

Step 10: Drying and Cure

"Dry" and "cured" are different things, and treating the first as the second is how furniture feet and planters leave permanent marks in a new finish.

  • Light foot traffic (socks or clean shoes): typically 24–48 hours, longer for oils in cool weather.
  • Furniture and grills: 48–72 hours. Lift furniture into place rather than dragging it.
  • Rugs, planters and anything that traps moisture: wait 1–2 weeks, until the finish has fully cured.
  • Full cure: around a week for many water-based stains, two weeks or more for oils and solid stains.
  • Washing the deck: avoid cleaners or pressure washing for at least two weeks.

Keep pets off until the stain is at least dry to the touch, and longer if they will scratch. If rain arrives before the first day is out, do not panic; a water-based stain that was dry to the touch is usually fine, and oils may show some spotting that a light maintenance coat later corrects.

Common Mistakes

Almost every failed stain job comes down to one of these, and most are covered by the steps above:

  • Staining wet wood. The most common and most expensive mistake; it shows up as peeling within months. Run the water-drop test before every application.
  • Applying too much. Stain the wood cannot absorb dries as a tacky surface film that holds dirt and peels. Thin coats, back-brushed, excess wiped off.
  • Working in patches instead of full-length runs, which creates lap marks.
  • Staining in direct sun, which means lap marks, poor penetration and a short life.
  • Skipping the brightener, leaving a high-pH, darkened surface that makes the finish look muddy.
  • Too much pressure from the washer, gouging softwood and raising fuzz.
  • Sanding finer than 80 grit, which closes the surface and reduces absorption.
  • Penetrating stain over an old film. It cannot soak through, so it fails. Strip first or stay with the same type of product.
  • Not boxing the stain, so a batch difference shows up halfway across the deck.
  • Forgetting end grain and board edges, which is where water gets in and rot starts.
  • Leaving oily rags in a pile — a fire risk that takes thirty seconds to avoid.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to stain a deck?

Plan four to five days, with about two days of actual work on a typical 300-square-foot deck. Repairs, cleaning and brightening take most of the first day. The wood then needs 48–72 hours of dry weather. Sanding and staining take another day, the application itself three to six hours. Allow 24–48 hours before foot traffic and 48–72 hours before furniture. Most of the elapsed time is waiting for the wood to dry, and that waiting is what keeps the finish from peeling.

Do I have to clean my deck before staining?

Yes. Stain bonds to whatever is on the surface, so stain over dirt, mildew and grey weathered fibres comes off with them, usually within a year. An oxygen-based deck cleaner removes all of that without damaging the wood. If the old finish is solid or peeling, you need a stripper instead. Follow either with a brightener, then let the deck dry for at least 48 hours.

Should I sand my deck before staining?

Only where it is needed: new boards with mill glaze, which makes water bead up, or fuzzy fibres raised by stripping or pressure washing. Use 60–80 grit and go no finer, because finer sanding closes the surface and reduces absorption. Sand only once the deck is fully dry, and vacuum the dust out of the gaps. A sound weathered deck that has been cleaned and brightened often needs no sanding at all.

Can I use a roller to stain a deck?

Yes, but always back-brush. A 3/8" to 1/2" nap roller is fast on rough boards and with solid stains, but it lays stain on too thickly, where it can dry tacky and peel. Brushing each run while it is still wet works the stain into the grain. You will still need a brush for edges, gaps and cut ends. For most semi-transparent stains, a stain pad on a pole is the better floor tool.

Should I apply one or two coats of deck stain?

Weathered, porous wood usually benefits from two; new, smooth or dense wood may only accept one. Timing matters as much as the number. Many oil-based penetrating stains want the second coat wet-on-wet, before the first dries — once it has dried, the second coat cannot penetrate and sits on top as a sticky film. Water-based stains are recoated once dry to the touch, usually 2–4 hours, within the label's recoat window. If a coat is not soaking in, wipe off the excess.

How long after rain can I stain my deck?

Usually 24–48 hours of dry weather, longer if it is cool, humid or shaded. The surface dries in hours but the inside of the boards takes much longer. Rather than counting hours, sprinkle water on a few boards, including shaded ones: if it soaks in within a few minutes, the wood is ready. You also need a dry forecast afterwards — at least 24 hours for water-based stains and around 48 for oils.

How long should I wait to stain new pressure-treated wood?

Until it passes the water-drop test, which can take anywhere from a few weeks to several months. Standard pressure-treated lumber is saturated with preservative solution when it is delivered, and how fast it dries depends on your climate, sun and airflow. Test every few weeks and stain once water soaks in rather than beading. Lumber stamped KDAT, kiln-dried after treatment, can usually be stained much sooner.

Can I stain over old deck stain?

Often, if the old stain is a penetrating transparent or semi-transparent that has faded rather than peeled and you are using the same type of product: clean, brighten, dry and restain. If the old finish is solid or semi-solid, a penetrating stain cannot soak through it, so strip the deck or use another film-forming product. If the old finish is peeling, it has to come off first. Water beading on clean-looking boards means finish is still blocking absorption.
