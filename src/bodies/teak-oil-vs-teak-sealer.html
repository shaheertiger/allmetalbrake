Teak Oil vs. Teak Sealer — What Teak Actually Needs

Teak survives untreated for fifty years. The real question is whether you want honey-brown or silver-grey, and how much work you will do to keep it. Here is what each product does and where each belongs.

Teak is the one wood that genuinely does not need to be finished. Its natural oil content and unusually high silica content make it resistant to rot, insects, and water in a way no other commercially available timber matches — which is why it has been the default for boat decks and outdoor furniture for two centuries. Left alone, teak weathers to a soft silver-grey and lasts for decades.

So the choice between teak oil and teak sealer is not really about protecting the wood. It is about whether you want to keep the honey-brown color, and if so, how much work you are willing to do to keep it. That reframing answers most of the confusion around the two products.

⚡ Quick Answer: Teak sealer is the better choice for almost everyone. It holds color for 9–12 months against 6–10 weeks for teak oil, it does not feed mildew, and it does not darken over time. Use teak oil only if you want the deep, rich, freshly-oiled look and are prepared to reapply every month or two — and never on a boat deck, where it makes the surface slippery and traps dirt.

What Is Teak Oil?

The first thing to understand is that teak oil does not come from teak, and there is no standard formulation. It is a category name, not a specification. Most products sold as teak oil are a blend of a drying oil — usually linseed or tung — thinned with mineral spirits or naphtha, sometimes with varnish resins, UV absorbers, and a fungicide added.

Because there is no standard, two bottles labeled teak oil can behave very differently. A tung-oil-heavy formulation cures harder and lasts longer; a heavily thinned linseed formulation penetrates fast and evaporates fast. Read the technical data sheet rather than the front label if longevity matters to you.

What the oil does is straightforward: it penetrates the surface fibers, and while it is in there it refracts light in a way that makes the wood look wet and rich. That is the entire appeal. It restores the deep honey-brown of new teak on wood that has started to grey, and it does it instantly and dramatically.

The Problem With Oiling Teak

Three problems, in fact, and they compound.

It does not last. The oil is consumed by UV and washed out by rain. Outdoors in full sun, expect six to ten weeks before the color has visibly faded. On a shaded porch, perhaps a season. This is not a product failure; it is what oil does.

It feeds mildew. Organic oils are food for mold and mildew spores. Oiled teak in a humid climate develops black spotting far faster than unoiled teak — this is the single most common complaint about teak oil and it is a direct consequence of the chemistry. Formulations with added fungicide delay it but do not prevent it.

It darkens with each coat. Successive applications build up in the surface fibers and oxidize, so oiled teak trends progressively darker and eventually muddy over several seasons. Getting back to a clean base requires a two-part teak cleaner and brightener, or sanding.

There is also a genuine safety issue for boat owners: oiled teak is slippery when wet, and it holds dirt in a way that makes a deck look worse rather than better. No competent marine finisher oils a teak deck.

🔥 Spontaneous Combustion Warning: Rags soaked in linseed or tung oil generate heat as the oil cures and can ignite on their own. This is a real, documented cause of house and shop fires. Spread used rags flat outdoors to dry completely, or submerge them in water in a sealed metal container, before disposal. Never leave them balled up in a trash can.

What Is Teak Sealer?

Teak sealer takes a different approach. Instead of an organic oil that feeds mildew and evaporates, it uses a synthetic resin carrier and finely dispersed pigments to lock color into the surface. The resin is usually a modified acrylic or alkyd, and the pigment is what produces the color rather than an optical effect from oil.

The results follow from that difference:

  • It lasts far longer. Nine to twelve months on horizontal outdoor surfaces, and up to two years on vertical or sheltered ones, against six to ten weeks for oil.
  • It does not feed mildew. There is no organic food source, so mildew growth is dramatically reduced.
  • It does not darken over time. Pigment is stable; oil oxidizes.
  • It is not slippery. Which makes it the only defensible choice for a boat deck.
  • It reapplies without stripping. A clean surface takes a maintenance coat directly.

The trade-offs are honest ones. Sealer produces a matte-to-satin appearance rather than the wet, glowing look of freshly oiled teak — attractive, but different. Because the color comes from pigment, a poorly applied coat can look slightly uniform or painted rather than showing the wood's own grain variation. And it costs more per bottle, though far less per year of color.

The Third Option: Do Nothing

It deserves more attention than it gets. Untreated teak weathers to a silver-grey patina over about nine months of outdoor exposure, and that patina is not damage — it is a thin oxidized surface layer, perhaps 0.1mm deep, beneath which the wood is unchanged. Teak left bare for fifty years is still structurally sound teak.

Bare teak needs one thing: an annual wash with mild soap and water and a soft brush, working with the grain. That is the entire maintenance program. No annual reapplication, no stripping, no mildew from an oil layer, no color mismatch as different areas fade at different rates.

The silver-grey look is also, to many eyes, the correct look for teak — it is what teak deck furniture is supposed to look like on a boat or a coastal terrace. If you are choosing a finish out of a sense that bare teak is neglected, it is worth sitting with the untreated option for a season before committing to an annual routine.

The one caveat: use a soft brush and go with the grain. Aggressive scrubbing or pressure washing erodes the soft spring-growth fibers between the harder grain lines, leaving a raised, rough texture that is unpleasant underfoot and traps dirt. This is the most common way teak is genuinely damaged, and it is done in the name of cleaning.

How to Apply Teak Oil

If you have decided on oil — for indoor furniture, for a sheltered piece, or because you simply want that look and accept the schedule — the technique matters.

1. Clean and Brighten First

Oil applied over grey, weathered teak seals the grey in. Use a two-part teak cleaner: part one is an alkaline cleaner that lifts dirt and breaks down the oxidized surface, part two is an acidic brightener that neutralizes it and restores the natural color. Rinse thoroughly between steps and let the wood dry for at least 48 hours.

2. Sand Lightly If Needed

220 grit, with the grain, only if the surface is rough or the cleaner left raised fibers. Teak needs very little sanding and each session removes material you cannot put back.

3. Apply Thin, Wipe Off the Excess

This is where most people go wrong. Flood a small area with oil using a cloth or brush, let it penetrate for 15–30 minutes, then wipe off everything that has not soaked in. Oil left on the surface does not add protection; it cures into a sticky, uneven film that attracts dirt and eventually has to be stripped.

4. Repeat for Two or Three Coats

Allow the previous coat to dry — usually 4–8 hours, longer in humidity — and repeat. Two to three coats is the practical maximum on teak; the wood's own oils limit how much it will accept.

5. Dispose of Rags Safely

See the warning above. This is not a theoretical hazard.

How to Apply Teak Sealer

1. Clean and Brighten

Same as for oil, and just as important — sealer pigment over grey wood produces a muddy, uneven color. Two-part cleaner, thorough rinse, and a full 48 hours of drying. The wood must be completely dry or the sealer will not adhere properly.

2. Mask Adjacent Surfaces

Sealer is pigmented and it will stain fiberglass, gelcoat, and painted surfaces. On a boat, mask carefully. On furniture, protect whatever it stands on.

3. Apply Thin Coats With a Foam Brush or Pad

Work in the direction of the grain in thin, even passes. Sealer is less forgiving than oil about heavy application — a thick coat looks painted rather than natural. Two thin coats always beat one heavy one.

4. Recoat After Full Drying

Most sealers specify two coats with 2–4 hours between them, and a full cure of 24–48 hours before exposure to rain or use.

5. Maintain Annually

Wash the surface, let it dry, and apply a single maintenance coat. No stripping required as long as you do not let it go so long that the surface has greyed through.

💡 Do Not Mix Systems: Sealer will not adhere properly over oiled teak, and oil applied over sealer sits on the surface and stays sticky. If you are switching from one to the other, strip back to bare wood with a two-part cleaner or a light sanding first.

Which Should You Use?

Outdoor Furniture

Sealer, or nothing. Sealer holds the color through a season with one application; oil means reapplying six to eight times a year on a table that sits in the sun. If you like the silver patina, bare teak with an annual wash is the least work of all.

Boat Decks and Marine Brightwork

Sealer, unambiguously, for decks. Oil makes teak slippery when wet, holds dirt, and encourages mildew in exactly the environment where mildew thrives. For vertical brightwork — handrails, trim, cabin sides — a marine varnish or a synthetic teak finish gives a glossier, longer-lasting result than either.

Indoor Furniture

Oil is genuinely good here. There is no UV to break it down, no rain to wash it out, and much less humidity to encourage mildew. Indoor oiled teak holds its look for a year or more and produces a depth that sealer cannot match. This is the application oil was made for.

Shower Benches and Bath Teak

Nothing, or sealer. Teak's natural water resistance is what it is used for in a bathroom. Oil in a constantly damp environment is a mildew invitation.

What to Look For in Either Product

Rather than a brand list, the specifications that predict performance:

For sealer: look for a stated UV absorber package, an explicit mildewcide, and a manufacturer's claimed service life of at least 9 months on horizontal surfaces. Marine-branded products are generally formulated to a higher standard than furniture-aisle equivalents, because the exposure conditions are harsher and the customers are more demanding. The classic reference product in this category is Semco Teak Sealer, and marine chandleries carry several equivalents.

For oil: a higher proportion of tung oil relative to linseed cures harder and lasts longer. Added UV absorbers help meaningfully. An added fungicide is close to essential outdoors. Avoid heavily thinned products that are mostly solvent — they penetrate quickly and disappear just as fast.

For marine-grade wood sealer generally: the meaningful differences from consumer products are UV package strength, salt-water resistance, and flexibility, since a boat's wood moves more than furniture does. If the piece lives near salt water, buy the marine product even for furniture.

The Verdict

For outdoor teak, sealer wins on nearly every axis that matters: it holds color roughly six times longer, it does not encourage the mildew that plagues oiled teak, it stays the color you applied instead of darkening, and it is safe to walk on when wet. The higher price per bottle is more than offset by applying it once a year instead of eight times.

Oil earns its place indoors, where UV and rain are absent and the depth it produces is unmatched, and on any piece where you actively want the freshly-oiled look and treat the reapplication as part of owning it.

And bare teak remains a genuinely good answer. A wash once a year, a silver-grey patina, and a piece of furniture that will outlast most of the house it sits beside.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between teak oil and teak sealer?

Teak oil is a blend of drying oil — usually linseed or tung — thinned with solvent. It penetrates the surface fibers and refracts light to produce a deep, wet, glowing honey-brown. Teak sealer uses a synthetic resin carrier with dispersed pigment to lock color in, producing a matte-to-satin natural tone. The practical differences follow from the chemistry: oil lasts 6–10 weeks outdoors and sealer lasts 9–12 months; oil is organic so it feeds mildew while sealer does not; oil darkens progressively with each coat while pigment stays stable; and oiled teak is slippery when wet while sealed teak is not. For outdoor use, sealer wins on nearly every axis.

Does teak actually need to be oiled or sealed?

No. Teak's natural oil content and high silica content make it resistant to rot, insects, and water without any finish at all, which is why it has been the default boat and outdoor furniture timber for two centuries. Left bare, it weathers to a silver-grey patina — a thin oxidized surface layer roughly 0.1mm deep, beneath which the wood is unchanged. Teak left untreated for fifty years is still sound teak. The only maintenance bare teak needs is an annual wash with mild soap, water, and a soft brush worked with the grain. Finishing teak is a decision about color, not protection: you finish it if you want to keep the honey-brown instead of letting it go grey.

How often do you need to apply teak oil?

Outdoors in full sun, every six to ten weeks — the oil is consumed by UV and washed out by rain, and the color visibly fades on that schedule. On a shaded porch you might get a full season. Indoors, where there is no UV and no rain, a year or more is realistic. That outdoor frequency is the main practical argument against oiling exterior teak: six to eight applications a year on a sun-exposed table is a genuine commitment, and each one adds to the progressive darkening that eventually requires a two-part cleaner or sanding to reverse. Teak sealer applied once annually delivers a similar color result for a fraction of the labor.

Why does my oiled teak have black spots?

Mildew, and the oil is feeding it. Linseed and tung oil are organic and make an excellent food source for mold and mildew spores, so oiled teak in a humid climate develops black spotting far faster than bare teak does. Formulations with added fungicide delay it but do not prevent it. The fix is a two-part teak cleaner — an alkaline cleaner to lift the growth and break down the oxidized surface, then an acidic brightener to neutralize and restore color — followed by a full 48 hours of drying. If mildew keeps returning, switch to a teak sealer, which uses a synthetic resin with no organic food source, or leave the wood bare.

Can I put teak sealer over teak oil?

Not successfully. Sealer will not adhere properly to an oiled surface — the oil in the fibers prevents the resin from bonding, and the result is patchy coverage that lifts within weeks. The same is true in reverse: oil applied over sealer sits on the surface and stays sticky rather than penetrating. If you are switching from one system to the other, strip back to bare wood first with a two-part teak cleaner and brightener, or with a light sanding at 220 grit if the buildup is heavy. Let the wood dry a full 48 hours after cleaning before applying anything, since neither product adheres properly to damp teak.

Should I use teak oil on my boat deck?

No. Oiled teak is slippery when wet, which is a genuine safety problem on a moving deck, and it holds dirt in a way that makes the deck look progressively worse rather than better. It also encourages mildew in exactly the humid, salt-laden environment where mildew thrives. No competent marine finisher oils a teak deck. Use a teak sealer, which holds color for 9–12 months, stays non-slip, and does not feed mildew — or leave the deck bare and wash it annually with mild soap and a soft brush, which is what most experienced owners of teak-decked boats actually do. For vertical brightwork like handrails and cabin trim, a marine varnish gives a glossier and longer-lasting result than either.

How do you apply teak oil correctly?

Clean and brighten first with a two-part teak cleaner, because oil over grey wood simply seals the grey in — rinse thoroughly and let the wood dry at least 48 hours. Sand lightly at 220 grit with the grain only if the surface is rough. Then flood a small area with oil, let it penetrate for 15–30 minutes, and wipe off everything that has not soaked in. That last step is where most people go wrong: oil left on the surface adds no protection and cures into a sticky, uneven film that attracts dirt and eventually needs stripping. Repeat for two or three coats with 4–8 hours between. And spread the used rags flat outdoors to dry — oil-soaked rags generate heat as they cure and are a documented cause of fires.

What is the best sealer for teak furniture?

Look for specifications rather than brands: a stated UV absorber package, an explicit mildewcide, and a manufacturer's claimed service life of at least nine months on horizontal surfaces. Marine-branded sealers are generally formulated to a higher standard than furniture-aisle equivalents because the exposure conditions are harsher and boat owners are less tolerant of failure — Semco Teak Sealer is the long-standing reference product in this category and marine chandleries carry several comparable options. If the furniture lives near salt water, buy the marine product regardless of what the piece is. Apply two thin coats with a foam brush or pad, working with the grain, and mask anything adjacent, because pigmented sealer stains fiberglass, gelcoat, and paint.
