Best Driveway Paint — And Why a Stain Often Beats It

A driveway is the hardest surface around a house to paint: hot tyres, sun, road salt and ground moisture all work on the film at once. Here is what actually holds up, by surface and by traffic.

The best driveway paint for most concrete driveways is a one-part epoxy-acrylic floor paint such as KILZ 1-Part Epoxy Acrylic Concrete & Garage Floor Paint — but only if you want a solid, opaque colour and accept recoating the tyre tracks every few years. If you want colour that cannot peel, a penetrating concrete stain such as SureCrete Eco-Stain is the more durable choice, because it soaks into the surface instead of sitting on top of it. Asphalt is a different material entirely: the realistic "paint" for a blacktop driveway is an asphalt-emulsion sealer, not anything sold for concrete.

That distinction matters more than any brand. A driveway is the harshest place you can put a coating — hotter, wetter, saltier and harder-wearing than a garage floor or a porch — and any product that forms a film on top of the slab is fighting all of those at once. Products that work with the concrete, by staining or resurfacing it, last longer than products that try to seal it off.

⚡ Quick answer: For an opaque colour on sound concrete, use a one-part epoxy-acrylic paint over an etched surface, with grit added, and expect 2–5 years before the tyre lanes need recoating. For colour that will not peel, use a penetrating stain plus a sealer (stain lasts 5–10+ years; reseal every 2–3). For worn, scaled concrete, resurface first. For asphalt, use an asphalt sealer — never concrete paint. Two-part garage epoxy does not belong outdoors in the sun.

Why Driveways Are Hard to Paint

Most paint failures on driveways are not product defects. They are the predictable result of putting a film coating on a surface that is attacked from four directions at once. Understanding them is the difference between a driveway that looks good for five years and one that is peeling in strips by its first spring.

Hot-tyre pickup

A tyre that has just come off a highway can be well above 60 °C (140 °F). Park it on a painted surface and the heat softens the coating directly underneath. As the tyre cools overnight it contracts, and the rubber's plasticisers bond to the softened film; when you drive off, the tyre pulls the paint away with it, leaving tyre-shaped bald patches exactly where the car parks. Garage floors suffer from this too, but a driveway adds direct sun heating the surface from above. One-part acrylic and epoxy-acrylic paints are the most vulnerable, which is why the parking spots are always the first place they fail.

UV exposure

Sunlight breaks down binders. Acrylics handle it reasonably well, which is why exterior concrete paints are acrylic-based. True two-part epoxy does not: it chalks and yellows (ambers) within months in full sun, which is why garage epoxy kits are labelled for interior use. If a product's label says interior only, it is not a driveway product, however tough it is indoors.

Road salt and freeze-thaw

In cold climates, de-icing salt and repeated freezing push water into the top of the slab, where it expands and pops off the surface — called scaling. A film coating does not stop this; if anything, it can trap moisture beneath it and make the delamination worse. Salt brine tracked in under the car also attacks the bond line at the edges of chips and scratches, so small defects grow quickly through a winter.

Moisture vapour from below

This is the one most people never consider. Driveways are poured directly on the ground, almost never over a vapour barrier, so groundwater is constantly moving up through the slab and evaporating out of the top. A breathable coating lets that vapour pass; an impermeable one traps it, and the pressure lifts the film in blisters and sheets. Tape a 2 ft square of clear plastic sheet to the concrete for 24 hours: if the underside fogs or the concrete darkens, the slab is pushing moisture, and only a breathable product — a stain, a breathable acrylic, or a resurfacer — has a realistic chance of staying put.

💡 The honest framing. No driveway paint lasts like a driveway. Even well-applied, a film-forming paint in the tyre lanes is a maintenance coating, like exterior trim paint. If that sounds like too much upkeep, choose a stain or a penetrating concrete sealer instead — nothing on the surface means nothing to peel.

The Five Ways to Colour a Driveway

"Driveway paint" covers five quite different product families. They differ in how they bond, how they fail, and what the driveway looks like afterwards.

Penetrating stains — water-based or acid-reactive — colour the top of the concrete itself. Because there is no film, there is nothing to lift; the colour wears away gradually from abrasion and UV rather than failing in patches. The trade-off is that the result is translucent: existing stains, patches and colour differences in the concrete show through, which can look intentional and stone-like or simply blotchy depending on the slab. Our guide to staining concrete to look like stone covers the techniques that turn that variation into an advantage.

One-part epoxy-acrylic paints are water-based acrylics with epoxy resin added for hardness and adhesion. They roll on like ordinary paint, need no mixing, and give a uniform opaque colour that hides old oil stains and patching. They are the closest thing to a true "driveway paint," and also the family most exposed to hot-tyre pickup.

Solid-colour concrete stains and tinted sealers sit between the two. Products sold as "solid colour concrete stain" — Behr, among others, sells one — are essentially thin, highly pigmented acrylic coatings: more breathable than a paint, but still a film that wears in traffic lanes. Tinted acrylic sealers add a little pigment to a clear sealer to even out colour or deepen a charcoal driveway; they need recoating every two to three years like any acrylic sealer.

Resurfacers are not coatings at all but a thin layer of polymer-modified cement trowelled or squeegeed over the old slab. They are the answer when the problem is surface damage — scaling, spalling, exposed aggregate — rather than colour. The result is fresh grey concrete that can then be stained or sealed.

Asphalt coatings are their own category. Asphalt is flexible, oily when young, and softens in heat; concrete paints and stains are not designed to bond to it or flex with it. The standard way to "repaint" blacktop is an asphalt-emulsion sealer, which restores the black colour. Coloured asphalt coatings exist — acrylic pavement coatings of the kind used on sports courts — but they are specialist products, typically sold through sealcoating suppliers rather than home centres.

What about porch paint or garage epoxy?

Porch and floor paints — Valspar, Behr and KILZ all make one — are excellent on porches, steps and patios, and many are rated for concrete. But they are formulated for foot traffic, and most do not claim to resist vehicles or hot tyres. On a path or a patio they are a fine choice (see our porch paint guide); on a driveway's parking spot they are likely to lift within a season. Two-part garage epoxy and polyaspartic kits are the opposite problem: tough enough for tyres, but most are labelled for interior use, and epoxy ambers and chalks quickly in sunlight while trapping ground moisture. They are the right answer for a garage — compare them in our concrete floor paint guide — not for an exposed driveway.

Preparation: Where Jobs Succeed or Fail

Every product above fails the same way when the surface is wrong, and on a driveway the surface is usually wrong in three ways: it is contaminated with oil, it is too smooth or sealed for anything to soak in, and it is cracked. Budget more time for preparation than for application.

1. Degrease and clean

Oil and tyre residue are the enemies of adhesion; paint applied over an oil stain will lift in exactly that spot. Scrub every stain with a concrete degreaser, rinse, and repeat until water no longer beads on it. Deep, old stains may need a poultice; our guide to removing oil stains from a driveway covers the stubborn ones. Then pressure-wash the whole surface to remove dirt, moss and loose material — a good driveway cleaner run through the washer saves time on a large slab.

2. Etch or profile the surface

Paint bonds mechanically, and smooth, power-trowelled or weathered concrete is too closed for a film to grip. Etching opens the surface to a texture like medium sandpaper. Use a commercial concrete etcher (phosphoric or citric acid based products are gentler and easier to handle than muriatic acid), scrub it in, and rinse thoroughly. The test is simple: sprinkle water on the dry concrete. If it darkens and soaks in within a minute, the surface is open enough for paint or stain. If it beads, there is sealer or a dense surface layer still there — etch again, or grind.

Previously sealed driveways are the most common trap. Acrylic sealers must be stripped with a solvent stripper; penetrating silane and siloxane sealers cannot be stripped and simply have to wear out, which can take years. If water beads and etching does not change it, the driveway is not ready for stain or paint.

3. Repair cracks and spalls

Clean out cracks with a wire brush and vacuum, then fill with a flexible concrete crack sealant (for moving cracks) or an epoxy or cement-based patch (for static ones). Patch spalled areas with a polymer-modified repair mortar. Be aware that patches absorb stain differently from the original concrete — under a translucent stain they will show; under an opaque paint they disappear. Let repairs cure fully before coating.

4. Check moisture and weather

Run the plastic-sheet test described above on several spots, including low areas where water collects. New concrete needs at least 28 days to cure before any coating, and longer is better. Apply when the surface and air are between roughly 10 °C and 32 °C (50–90 °F), with no rain forecast for at least 24 hours, and avoid direct midday sun on hot days: a slab that is too hot flash-dries the paint before it can wet out and bond.

💡 Two thin coats, not one thick one. A thick coat of driveway paint stays soft longer and is far more prone to hot-tyre pickup and solvent entrapment. Thin the first coat slightly if the label allows it, let it penetrate, and roll a second coat at the stated coverage rate once the recoat window is reached. Then keep cars off for the full cure time on the label — usually several days, and a week is a safe default.

Making It Slip-Resistant

A satin or semi-gloss paint over smooth concrete becomes genuinely slippery when wet, and on a sloped driveway in winter that is a real hazard. Opaque paints and film-forming sealers should always get an anti-slip additive; stains and broom-finished resurfacers usually do not need one, because the concrete texture remains.

There are two approaches. Mix-in additives — fine polymer beads or grit sold specifically for floor paint — are stirred into the final coat and give an even, mild texture that is easy to clean. Broadcast grit — fine silica sand or aluminium oxide scattered onto the wet final coat, then locked in with a light extra coat — gives more aggressive traction and is better on slopes. Whichever you use, keep stirring mix-in additives as you roll so they do not settle to the bottom of the tray, and test a small area first: too much grit makes the surface hard to clean and wears tyres faster.

At minimum, add traction to the slope, the apron where the driveway meets the street, and any walking route to the door.

Which to Choose: Concrete vs Asphalt

Start with what the driveway is made of and how it is used, not with colour.

Asphalt, faded greyAsphalt emulsion sealerFormulated to bond to and flex with blacktop

Concrete driveways give you real choices. If the car parks in the same spot every day, the penetrating route wins on durability by a wide margin, because the failure mode that kills paint — hot-tyre pickup — simply does not apply to a stain. Choose paint when uniformity matters more than maintenance: a driveway with years of oil shadows, mismatched patches or a colour you dislike will not be rescued by a translucent stain. A middle route is to stain the main slab and paint only decorative borders or walkways that see no tyres.

Asphalt driveways are simpler. For black, an emulsion sealer applied every two to three years is the maintenance coating the material is designed for. For colour, you are looking at acrylic pavement coatings, usually applied over a sealed and stable surface by a contractor; DIY results with concrete paints on blacktop are consistently poor. If you are weighing a new surface altogether, our comparison of asphalt vs concrete driveways covers how each ages.

Traffic matters as much as surface. A single car that parks in a garage and only drives across the driveway is far easier on a coating than a car that sits on the same spot in the sun all day. Turning wheels while stationary — power steering scrub — tears film coatings quickly, so a driveway where cars turn and manoeuvre will wear faster at those points than one used in a straight line.

Mistakes That Make It Peel

  • Painting over a sealer. The single most common cause of peeling. If water beads, the paint has nothing to grip.
  • Skipping the etch. Pressure-washing cleans; it does not profile. Smooth concrete needs etching or grinding.
  • Ignoring the moisture test. A slab pushing vapour will blister any film coating, however good.
  • Using interior garage epoxy outdoors. It ambers in sunlight and traps ground moisture.
  • Driving on it too soon. A coating that is dry to the touch is not cured. Hot tyres on uncured paint are guaranteed pickup.
  • Painting asphalt with concrete paint. It cracks and flakes as the asphalt flexes and softens.
  • Painting in the heat of the day. A hot slab flash-dries the paint before it can bond.
  • Leaving it slippery. No grit on a sloped, painted driveway is a fall waiting for the first frost.

The Short Version

For a concrete driveway with cars parking on it, a penetrating stain under a breathable sealer is the finish that lasts, because there is nothing on the surface for hot tyres, salt or ground moisture to lift. If you need a solid colour, a one-part epoxy-acrylic paint such as KILZ 1-Part Epoxy Acrylic Concrete & Garage Floor Paint is the right product — applied thin, over an etched and degreased slab, with grit, and treated as a coating you will touch up. Damaged concrete should be resurfaced before anything goes on it, and asphalt should get an asphalt sealer, not paint. Once the colour is down, protecting it is a sealing job; our concrete driveway sealer guide covers the products that do that best.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best paint for a concrete driveway?

A one-part epoxy-acrylic concrete paint rated for exterior use, such as KILZ 1-Part Epoxy Acrylic Concrete & Garage Floor Paint or Seal-Krete Epoxy-Seal. These are water-based acrylics with epoxy added for hardness, they tolerate UV far better than two-part epoxy, and they are breathable enough to stay bonded on most outdoor slabs. Apply two thin coats over degreased, etched concrete, add an anti-slip additive, and keep cars off for the full cure time. If you can accept a translucent finish rather than opaque colour, a penetrating stain is more durable still, because it cannot peel.

How long does driveway paint last?

On a well-prepared concrete driveway, a one-part epoxy-acrylic paint typically lasts two to five years, with the tyre lanes and parking spots wearing first. Areas that see no tyres can look good considerably longer. A penetrating stain lasts five to ten years or more, though it fades gradually and the sealer over it needs renewing every two to three years. Tinted sealers and solid-colour stains last one to three years in traffic. Poor preparation — painting over sealer, oil or unetched concrete — can cut any of these to a single season.

Can you paint an asphalt driveway?

Not with concrete paint. Asphalt contains oils, softens in heat and flexes with temperature, so a rigid acrylic film cracks and flakes. To restore the black colour, use an asphalt emulsion sealer such as Black Jack Drive-Maxx 700, which is formulated for blacktop and lasts one to three years. For a coloured asphalt surface, you need an acrylic pavement coating of the kind used on sports courts, normally applied by a contractor over cured, stable asphalt. New asphalt should be left at least six months before any coating.

Why does driveway paint peel where the car parks?

Hot-tyre pickup. A tyre that has just been driven is hot enough to soften the coating beneath it. As it cools, the rubber's plasticisers bond to the softened paint, and when the car moves the tyre pulls the film away. It is worst with thick coats, coatings that have not fully cured, and paint over a poorly prepared surface where the bond to the concrete is weak. Thin coats, a full cure before parking, proper etching and — on a garage floor — a harder topcoat all reduce it. On a driveway, a penetrating stain avoids it entirely.

Is concrete stain better than paint for a driveway?

For durability, usually yes. Stain penetrates the concrete instead of forming a film, so it cannot peel from hot tyres, blister from ground moisture or flake from freeze-thaw. It wears away gradually rather than failing in patches, and re-staining needs no scraping. Paint is better when you need full opacity: it hides oil stains, patches and uneven colour that a translucent stain will show. If the driveway is fairly uniform and you are happy with a natural, variegated look, stain is the lower-maintenance choice over ten years.

Do I need to etch a driveway before painting?

Almost always. Pressure-washing removes dirt but does not open the surface, and paint needs a slightly rough, porous profile to bond. Sprinkle water on the dry concrete: if it soaks in and darkens within a minute, the surface is ready; if it beads, etch it with a concrete etcher and rinse thoroughly, or grind it. Beading that does not respond to etching means a sealer is still present — acrylic sealers can be stripped, but penetrating silane or siloxane sealers have to wear off before paint or stain will take.

Can I use garage floor epoxy on my driveway?

Two-part garage epoxy is a poor choice outdoors. Most kits are labelled for interior use because epoxy chalks and yellows in sunlight within months, and its impermeable film traps the moisture that rises through a driveway slab, which leads to blistering and delamination. One-part "epoxy" paints are different: they are acrylics modified with epoxy, many are rated for exterior concrete, and they are the product family to use if you want an opaque driveway paint. Read the label for "exterior" before buying.

How much does it cost to paint a driveway?

For a typical 600 sq ft two-car concrete driveway, DIY materials for a one-part epoxy-acrylic paint in two coats run roughly $200–$400, plus $50–$120 for degreaser, etcher, crack filler and rollers, and a pressure washer rental if needed. A penetrating stain with a sealer costs about $200–$450. Professional painting or staining typically runs $2–$5 per square foot depending on preparation. Resurfacing costs more — $300–$600 DIY or $3–$7 per square foot installed — but fixes a damaged surface rather than covering it.

🔧 Key Numbers

  • Paint life in tyre lanes: 2–5 yrs
  • Stain life: 5–10+ yrs (reseal 2–3)
  • New concrete: wait 28+ days
  • New asphalt: wait ~6 months
  • Cars on fresh paint: ~1 week
  • Moisture test: 24 hrs under plastic

Would a Sealer Do the Job Instead?

If the goal is protection rather than colour, a penetrating sealer gives a driveway nothing to peel — and lasts far longer than paint.
