Best Grout Sealer: 6 Picks for Showers, Floors & Backsplashes

Grout is the most porous thing in a tiled room, and the right sealer is almost always a penetrating one. Here is how to choose it, apply it so it actually works, and know when you can skip it entirely.

The best grout sealer for most homes is a water-based penetrating (impregnating) sealer such as Aqua Mix Sealer's Choice Gold, Miracle Sealants 511 Impregnator or StoneTech BulletProof Sealer. These soak into the cement grout and line its pores with a repellent, so water and stains bead on the surface while the grout can still breathe. They leave no film to peel, yellow or turn slippery, which is exactly the failure that makes surface-coating sealers a poor choice for grout lines.

The product matters less than three decisions around it: sealing only grout that is fully cured and bone dry, keeping the shower or floor away from water until the sealer has cured (typically 24–72 hours), and knowing whether you need to seal at all. Epoxy and urethane grouts do not need sealer, glazed tile does not need sealer, and natural stone tile usually needs sealing as much as its grout does.

⚡ The short answer: Use a penetrating, water-based sealer on cement grout — Aqua Mix Sealer's Choice Gold is the best all-round choice. Apply it to grout that has cured at least 48–72 hours, wipe any excess off glazed tile within 5–10 minutes, and keep the area dry for 24–72 hours afterwards. Reseal shower grout every 1–3 years and floors every 2–5 years, or whenever a drop of water darkens the grout within a few minutes. If your grout is epoxy, skip sealing entirely.

Penetrating vs Membrane (Topical) Sealers

Every grout sealer falls into one of two families, and the choice between them matters more than the brand.

Penetrating sealers — also called impregnating sealers — are carried into the grout by water or solvent. The carrier evaporates and leaves behind a repellent (typically silane, siloxane or fluorochemical compounds) that lines the pore walls without filling them. Water and oil can no longer wick in easily, but water vapor can still escape. That breathability is the whole point in a shower: moisture that gets behind or under the tile can dry out through the grout instead of being trapped. Penetrating sealers are invisible, do not change slip resistance, and cannot peel because there is nothing on the surface to peel. If you want the chemistry in more depth, the same compounds are covered in silane vs siloxane sealers.

Membrane-forming (topical) sealers are acrylic or urethane coatings that build a film on top of the grout. They can add sheen and make grout very stain-resistant while the film is intact. In grout lines, though, the film tends to wear unevenly, scuff in traffic lanes, peel in wet areas and trap moisture beneath it, which leads to whitish, cloudy patches. On glazed tile they leave a haze that is hard to remove. Topical sealers have a place on some concrete and unglazed surfaces — see the concrete sealer guide — but for grout they are the wrong tool.

When the Tile Needs Sealing Too

Glazed ceramic and glazed porcelain tile are already impervious; sealer on the glaze does nothing except leave a haze if you fail to wipe it off. Unglazed and porous tile is different. Natural stone — marble, travertine, limestone, slate, and to a lesser degree granite — absorbs water and oil, and in a shower or kitchen it stains as readily as grout. Unglazed quarry tile, terracotta and cement tile also need sealing. In those rooms, seal the whole surface, tile and grout together, with a stone-rated penetrating sealer such as 511 Impregnator or Sealer's Choice Gold. Matte unglazed porcelain varies; a drop of water left for ten minutes will tell you whether it absorbs. For stone-specific guidance, see the guides to the best granite sealers and the best natural stone sealers.

Water-Based vs Solvent-Based

Penetrating sealers use either water or a solvent (typically mineral spirits or similar petroleum distillates) as the carrier that delivers the active ingredient into the grout.

Water-based sealers now dominate the consumer market, and for good reason. They are low in odor and VOCs, clean up with water, and are much more comfortable to use in a small, poorly ventilated bathroom. Modern water-based fluorochemical formulas match or beat older solvent products on stain resistance. Their main limitation is that the grout must be thoroughly dry, because water in the pores blocks the sealer from penetrating.

Solvent-based sealers tend to penetrate dense materials such as polished granite and some porcelains more readily, and they can tolerate cooler conditions. The cost is strong fumes and flammability, which in a windowless bathroom means a fan, an open door and a respirator rated for organic vapors. They can also darken some stones and grouts slightly more than water-based formulas.

For grout alone, water-based is the right default. Choose a solvent-based product only if the manufacturer of your stone recommends it or you are sealing very dense stone along with the grout.

How the sealer comes out of the bottle decides how fast the job goes and how much cleanup you face afterwards.

On glazed tile, a brush tip or roller wheel is worth the extra time because it puts sealer only where it is needed and leaves almost nothing to wipe off. On stone, where the tile needs sealing too, flooding the surface with a spray or pad is both faster and better, since you want even coverage on everything. In either case, the rule is the same: whatever is still sitting wet on the surface after the dwell time printed on the label — usually 5 to 15 minutes — gets wiped off with a clean, dry cloth.

Showers are where sealer choice matters most and where the most mistakes happen. Sealer does not waterproof a shower — the waterproofing membrane behind the tile does that. What sealer does is slow absorption, which keeps grout from staying damp, discoloring and growing mildew between uses. That only works if the sealer breathes, so use a penetrating product and seal walls and floor. The shower floor and the bottom row of wall tile take the most water and wear out first.

Change of plane — the corners, the joint between wall and tub, and the line where wall meets shower floor — should be flexible sealant, not grout. If yours is cracked grout, cut it out and replace it with 100% silicone before sealing, and give the silicone its full cure time first; our guide to how long caulk takes to dry covers the waiting times.

Floors take abrasion as well as water. Foot traffic wears sealer out of the grout surface over time, so traffic lanes — doorways, in front of the sink and toilet — need resealing before the edges of the room. A natural, no-sheen penetrating sealer keeps slip resistance unchanged, which matters on a bathroom floor that gets wet.

Backsplashes stay dry most of the time, so water is less of a concern than oil. Grout behind a cooktop picks up a fine mist of cooking oil that a water-only repellent will not stop. That is the case for an oil-resistant sealer such as StoneTech BulletProof or Sealer's Choice Gold, and a backsplash sealed with one of them can go several years between coats.

How to Tell If Grout Needs Sealing

The water-drop test takes five minutes and answers the question for any grout, new or old.

  1. Make sure the grout is clean and dry — no recent shower, no recent mopping.
  2. Put a few drops of water on the grout line in several places, including the busiest spots: shower floor, in front of the sink, doorways.
  3. Watch for 3–5 minutes. If the water beads and the grout stays its normal color, the sealer is working.
  4. If the grout darkens within a minute or two, or the water soaks in and disappears, the grout is unsealed or the sealer has worn out. Reseal.

For kitchens, add a drop of cooking oil to a discreet spot and leave it ten minutes before wiping. If it leaves a dark ring, the grout needs an oil-resistant sealer. Remember the results vary across a room: it is common for the shower floor to fail the test while the upper walls still pass, and you only need to reseal what fails.

💡 Brand-new grout: Do not test or seal it straight away. Standard cement grout needs to cure before it will accept sealer properly — most grout manufacturers specify around 48–72 hours, and some recommend longer in cool or humid rooms. Rapid-setting grouts can be ready sooner. Follow the time printed on the grout bag, not the sealer bottle.

How to Seal Grout Properly

Most sealer failures are application failures, and nearly all of them come down to dirty grout, damp grout or too little patience.

1. Clean thoroughly. Sealer locks in whatever is in the grout, including stains and soap scum. Scrub with a pH-neutral or dedicated grout cleaner and a stiff nylon brush. For mildew in a shower, an oxygen bleach cleaner works without the risk that chlorine bleach poses to some colored grouts and stone. Avoid acidic cleaners on cement grout and on marble, limestone and travertine, which they etch.

2. Rinse and dry completely. This is the step people rush. Grout that looks dry can still hold moisture an inch down. Leave a shower unused for at least 24 hours after cleaning, longer in a humid climate, and run the exhaust fan. Water in the pores blocks penetration, and the sealer ends up sitting near the surface where it wears off quickly.

3. Protect what should not be sealed. Tape off adjacent wood, painted trim and metal fixtures. Some sealers can mark polished brass or chrome if left to dry on them.

4. Apply a saturating coat. Work in sections of a few square feet. Apply enough sealer that the grout looks wet throughout, let it soak in for the dwell time on the label, then apply a second wet-on-wet coat on porous grout if the label calls for it. Many penetrating sealers specify a second coat while the first is still damp, roughly 5–15 minutes later.

5. Wipe off the excess. Within the label's window, wipe all excess from the tile faces with a clean, dry, lint-free cloth. Sealer left to dry on glazed tile becomes a sticky or hazy film, and removing it later typically means re-wetting it with more sealer or using a dedicated haze remover.

6. Let it cure before water. Most penetrating sealers are dry to the touch in an hour or two, but cure takes longer. Typical guidance is light foot traffic after a few hours, normal water exposure after 24–72 hours, and full stain resistance after up to several days. In practice: do not shower for 24 hours at minimum, and give it 72 hours if you can, especially with cool temperatures or high humidity. Do not mop the floor with cleaners for several days.

7. Test. After cure, repeat the water-drop test. Spots that still darken missed sealer or were too damp; give them another coat.

Grout That Doesn't Need Sealing

Epoxy grout is a two-part resin rather than a cement, and it is essentially non-porous once cured. It resists stains and water on its own, does not need sealer, and sealer will not penetrate it anyway — it simply sits on top. Epoxy grout costs more and is harder to install, which is why it is common in commercial kitchens and increasingly in high-end showers, but it removes resealing from the maintenance list permanently.

Pre-mixed urethane and acrylic grouts, sold ready to use in a bucket, are also typically marketed as stain-resistant without sealing. Check the product sheet for the specific grout you used, because formulations differ.

Glazed tile never needs sealing, only the cement grout between it. And caulked joints — silicone or urethane sealant at corners and changes of plane — are not grout and should not be "sealed" with grout sealer.

If you do not know what grout is in your shower, a simple clue is the drop test on old, never-resealed grout: cement grout that has gone years without sealer will darken almost instantly, while epoxy will not absorb water at all, and its surface tends to look slightly glossier and feel harder.

Color Sealers and Grout Paint

Color sealers, also called grout colorants or grout stains, are a different category from clear sealers. They are pigmented coatings — usually water-based acrylic or epoxy-modified — that sit on the grout surface and give it a new, uniform color while sealing it. Grout "paint" pens and markers are the same idea in a smaller, less durable form.

They are worth using when the grout is permanently stained, when patch repairs do not match the original, or when you want to change the grout color without regrouting. Polyblend Grout Renew is the most widely available example, and Aqua Mix also sells a grout colorant. They are a poor choice on new, clean grout that only needs protection, because they add a film that a penetrating sealer avoids.

Three rules make color sealers work. First, the grout must be clean, dry and sound; loose or crumbling grout needs replacing, not coating. Second, apply with a fine brush, staying within the joint, and wipe any color off glazed tile before it dries, typically within an hour, though the label is the authority. Third, give it the full cure time before water, which is often longer than for clear sealers. Do not apply a clear penetrating sealer over a colorant unless the colorant manufacturer says to.

How Often to Reseal

Reseal when the water-drop test fails, not by the calendar alone. As a rough guide:

  • Shower floors: every 1–2 years, the highest-wear location in the house.
  • Shower walls: every 2–3 years.
  • Bathroom and kitchen floors: every 2–4 years, with traffic lanes going first.
  • Backsplashes: every 3–5 years.
  • Low-use areas: premium sealers can last considerably longer — manufacturers of the top products cite multi-year to decade-plus protection — but only where water and abrasion are light.

Two habits extend the interval more than any brand choice. Clean with pH-neutral cleaners: strongly acidic and strongly alkaline cleaners, and steam cleaners, break down penetrating sealers faster. And squeegee or towel the shower after use, which cuts both the water load and soap scum buildup. Resealing is simple because penetrating sealers do not build up: clean, dry and apply the same product again, with no need to strip the old sealer first.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best type of grout sealer?

A water-based penetrating (impregnating) sealer is the best type for almost all cement grout. It soaks into the grout and lines the pores with a water- and stain-repellent compound, leaving no surface film, so it cannot peel, haze or make the floor slippery, and the grout can still breathe. That breathability matters in showers, where trapped moisture causes cloudy patches and mildew. Membrane or topical sealers, which form a coating on top, tend to wear unevenly and peel in grout lines. For kitchens, choose a penetrating sealer that also repels oil, such as Aqua Mix Sealer's Choice Gold or StoneTech BulletProof Sealer.

How long after grouting can I seal grout?

For standard cement grout, most grout manufacturers specify around 48–72 hours before sealing, and longer in cool or humid conditions. The grout needs to have cured and released most of its mixing water; sealing too early traps moisture and prevents the sealer from penetrating. Rapid-setting grouts can often be sealed sooner. Follow the time printed on the grout bag rather than the sealer bottle, since it is the grout's cure that sets the schedule. Epoxy grout does not need sealing at all.

How long after sealing grout can I shower?

Typically 24–72 hours, depending on the product, temperature and humidity. Most penetrating sealers are dry to the touch within an hour or two, but they need longer to cure before they reach full water resistance. Wait at least 24 hours in all cases, and 72 hours if the room is cool, humid or poorly ventilated, or if the label says so. Avoid cleaning products on the sealed surface for several days. Showering too soon washes uncured sealer out of the grout and leaves patchy protection.

How do I know if my grout is already sealed?

Use the water-drop test. On clean, dry grout, place a few drops of water and watch for three to five minutes. If the water beads and the grout keeps its color, it is sealed. If the grout darkens quickly or the water soaks in, it is unsealed or the sealer has worn out. Test several spots, especially high-use areas like the shower floor and doorways, because sealer wears unevenly and you may only need to reseal part of the room.

Does epoxy grout need to be sealed?

No. Epoxy grout is a two-part resin that cures to a dense, essentially non-porous solid, so it resists water and stains on its own. Sealer cannot penetrate it and would only sit on the surface. Many pre-mixed urethane and acrylic grouts are also sold as not needing sealer, though you should check the specific product sheet. Only cement-based grout, sanded or unsanded, needs regular sealing.

Should I seal the tile as well as the grout?

Only if the tile is porous. Glazed ceramic and glazed porcelain do not absorb sealer, and any left on them dries to a haze, so wipe it off within the label's dwell time. Natural stone such as marble, travertine, limestone and slate, along with unglazed quarry, terracotta and cement tile, does absorb water and oil and should be sealed along with the grout using a stone-rated penetrating sealer. A drop of water left on the tile for ten minutes will show whether it absorbs.

Can I seal over dirty or stained grout?

You can, but the sealer will lock the stain in. Clean the grout with a pH-neutral grout cleaner or oxygen bleach and a stiff brush, rinse, and let it dry for at least 24 hours before sealing. If stains will not come out, a clear sealer will not hide them; use a grout colorant such as Polyblend Grout Renew, which recolors and seals in one step, or replace the worst sections of grout.

Is grout sealer waterproof?

No. Grout sealer makes grout water-resistant, slowing absorption so water beads and evaporates rather than soaking in, but it does not make a shower waterproof. Waterproofing is the job of the membrane or backer system behind the tile. Sealer's role is to keep grout cleaner, reduce staining and mildew, and help it dry faster between uses. Cracked grout or failing caulk at corners lets water through regardless of sealer and must be repaired first.
