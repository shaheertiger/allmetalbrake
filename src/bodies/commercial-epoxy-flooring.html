Commercial & Industrial Epoxy Flooring: Systems and Costs

A warehouse aisle, a brewery floor and a restaurant kitchen all get called "epoxy flooring," and they need three different systems. Here is how to match the resin and thickness to the facility, and how to read the bid.

Industrial epoxy flooring is not one product. It is a family of resinous systems that runs from a 5-mil roll coat that dust-proofs a storage warehouse to a quarter-inch troweled mortar that takes steel-wheeled carts, and the commercial-kitchen version usually is not epoxy at all but urethane cement. The right choice is set by three things: what the floor will be hit with (wheels, impact, chemicals, hot water), how much downtime the building can absorb, and what condition the slab is in, particularly its moisture.

Get those three right and the brand matters far less than people expect. Get them wrong and a well-made coating from a reputable manufacturer still fails in a year, because it was the wrong thickness or chemistry for the room. The rest of this guide works through the system types, the realistic cost ranges, what each facility type needs, and how to write a bid so that competing contractors are quoting the same floor.

⚡ The short answer by facility: Light-duty warehouse — high-build 100% solids epoxy, roughly $4–$7 per sq ft installed. Forklift-heavy warehouse or manufacturing — epoxy broadcast or slurry at 1/16"–1/8", roughly $6–$10. Commercial kitchen, brewery or food plant with hot washdown — urethane cement with integral cove base, commonly $7–$12+. Showroom or retail that must reopen fast — polyaspartic, roughly $5–$10. These are planning ranges; prep, moisture mitigation, cove base and after-hours work move real quotes substantially.

System Types Compared

Thickness buys impact and abrasion resistance; chemistry decides heat, chemical and UV performance. These are the systems you will see specified in commercial work.

Thin-film and high-build epoxies are two-part resin rolled or squeegeed onto ground concrete. Thin films seal the slab economically but wear through in forklift lanes; high-build 100% solids epoxy is the default for moderate commercial traffic.

Broadcast and slurry systems build thickness by seeding quartz or sand into wet resin, sweeping off the excess and sealing over it. The aggregate adds impact resistance and texture.

Troweled mortars are resin and graded aggregate placed with a trowel. At a quarter inch they take serious impact and can flatten a spalled slab in the same operation.

Urethane cement, also called urethane concrete, is a polyurethane-modified cementitious system. It expands and contracts much more like the concrete underneath than epoxy does, so it survives thermal shock and hot washdown that crack epoxy, and many systems tolerate higher slab moisture. The trade-offs are a limited, mostly matte color range and some ambering under UV.

Polyaspartic is a fast-curing, UV-stable coating: thin, so not an impact system, but it reopens a floor in hours and does not yellow. Our polyurea vs epoxy comparison covers the chemistry.

MMA cures in about an hour per coat, even in working freezers, at the cost of a strong installation odor and a higher price.

What Commercial Systems Cost

Commercial pricing varies more than residential because job conditions vary more, so treat the table ranges as 2026 planning numbers rather than quotes. Our epoxy flooring cost per square foot guide covers the residential end of the same chemistry.

What moves a commercial price up or down:

  • Area and layout. A large open warehouse lands at the low end because crews shot blast and pour in long uninterrupted pulls. A small kitchen full of equipment bases and drains lands at or above the top, and minimum job charges make small rooms costly per square foot.
  • Preparation. Removing an old coating, adhesive or a failed quarry-tile floor can add several dollars per square foot.
  • Moisture mitigation. A slab that fails testing needs a vapor-mitigating primer, typically $1–$3 per sq ft extra.
  • Joints, repairs and cove base. Usually priced per linear foot or as an allowance, and they add up quickly in kitchens and labs.
  • Schedule. Night, weekend and phased work cost more because of labor premiums and repeated mobilizations.
  • Chemistry upgrades. Novolac topcoats for acids, urethane or polyaspartic topcoats for UV and abrasion, and conductive systems all add cost.

Two worked examples help. A 20,000 sq ft distribution warehouse with a sound, dry slab, shot blasted and coated in high-build epoxy with striping, might run roughly $80,000–$140,000. A 1,200 sq ft restaurant kitchen in 1/4" urethane cement with 6" integral cove, slope corrections around drains and a weekend install could plausibly land between $10,000 and $20,000 or more. Both depend heavily on the slab and the access.

💡 Price the floor by the year, not the square foot. A thin film that has to be recoated every three years in a forklift aisle, with a shutdown each time, usually costs more over a decade than a broadcast system installed once. Downtime is the cost most facility budgets leave out.

Warehouses and Forklift Traffic

For warehouses, the build matters more than the brand. Forklift wear is concentrated in the same wheel paths, turns and dock transitions.

Abrasion. Forklift tires grind at a coating, especially where they turn in place. In active aisles, high-build epoxy is the minimum and broadcast or slurry the safer spec, ideally with a renewable urethane or polyaspartic topcoat.

Impact. Dropped pallets, forks and steel parts chip thin coatings. Loading docks and areas under dock plates take the hardest hits and are where a troweled mortar or thicker broadcast pays off.

Joint treatment. The detail most warehouse floors fail at. Fill saw-cut control joints flush with a semi-rigid epoxy or polyurea filler so hard wheels do not chip the edges. Expansion and isolation joints move and must be honored through the coating; bridging them guarantees a crack line.

Line striping. OSHA requires permanent aisles to be appropriately marked where mechanical handling equipment is used. Stripes go on after cure or between base and topcoat so they do not wear off; price them separately.

If a warehouse only needs dust control, densified and polished concrete has nothing to peel. Coatings earn their cost where you need color-coding, chemical resistance or a sealed, presentable surface.

Manufacturing and Chemical Exposure

Manufacturing floors combine traffic with chemistry, and the chemistry decides the system. The point of a seamless floor is that spills cannot reach joints and attack the concrete.

Get the chemical list first: every chemical, its concentration, temperature and how long it sits before cleanup. Resistance charts are rated by exposure time, and a coating that handles a wiped-up acid splash may not handle it pooled overnight.

Standard vs novolac epoxy. General-purpose bisphenol-A epoxies resist oils, fuels, many alkalis and mild acids. For strong acids, aggressive solvents and secondary containment, novolac epoxy is the usual upgrade, either as the full system or as the topcoat. It costs more and is less flexible, so it is specified where needed rather than everywhere.

Seamless is the point. Coves at walls and equipment pads, careful drain terminations and treated joints keep liquids on top of the floor; a gap at the wall is a leak.

Specialty systems. Electronics assembly may need static-dissipative or conductive epoxy, engineered and tested as part of an ESD control program.

Commercial Kitchens

If you are searching for the best slip-resistant epoxy flooring for commercial kitchens, the industry's usual answer is not epoxy. It is urethane cement, and the reason is heat.

Thermal shock and hot washdown. Kitchens dump hot water, steam and hot oil on floors that may be cool. Standard epoxy expands at a very different rate from concrete, and repeated temperature swings stress the bond line until it cracks or lets go. Urethane cement moves much more like concrete. Heavier 3/8" builds are typical near kettles and fryers; check the rated service temperature for the thickness you buy.

Integral cove base. The floor is carried 4–6 inches up the wall with a curved transition, leaving no corner for debris and water and replacing the vinyl or tile base where kitchens traditionally harbor grime.

Slip resistance with aggregate. Texture comes from aggregate broadcast into the system or added to the topcoat: heavier near fryers and dish areas, lighter in dry storage.

Drains and slope. Resin follows the slab, so slope correction belongs in the scope, and the coating must tie into drain bodies and trench drains with anchor cuts, because drain edges are where kitchen floors usually start to fail.

Health-code cleanability. Local health departments enforce versions of the FDA Food Code, which calls for smooth, durable, nonabsorbent, easily cleanable floors in food prep areas, with coved, sealed floor-wall junctions where floors are water-flushed. Requirements vary by jurisdiction, so confirm with your health department. Seamless resin removes the grout lines quarry tile depends on.

Downtime. If the kitchen cannot close for more than one night, MMA is the usual alternative to urethane cement.

Food and Beverage Plants

Breweries, dairies, bakeries, meat processing and beverage plants combine everything that is hard on a floor: hot caustic cleaning, food acids such as lactic and citric acid, sugars, fats, constant water, forklifts and freezer temperatures. Urethane cement is the common base spec, with MMA where shutdown windows are measured in hours.

Food acids and sugars attack exposed concrete quickly, so a coating breach becomes a pothole fast. Slope to drains, cove walls and curbs, and detail every penetration. For USDA- or FDA-inspected plants, ask the manufacturer for documentation that the system suits food plant use.

Running freezers limit you to cold-curing chemistry, which is MMA's main advantage; cooler-to-ambient transitions benefit from urethane cement.

Retail, Showrooms, Healthcare and Labs

Retail and showrooms care about appearance and reopening time more than impact. Polyaspartic over an epoxy base, decorative flake, quartz, or metallic epoxy are the common choices. Anywhere with sunlight through glass, a UV-stable aliphatic topcoat matters because bare epoxy ambers. Overnight installs with polyaspartic are a big part of its appeal for stores that cannot close. Our epoxy flake flooring guide covers the decorative broadcast look.

Healthcare wants seamless, disinfectant-resistant surfaces with integral cove. Resinous floors are harder and louder than sheet vinyl or rubber, but win in wet, heavy-cart areas such as sterile processing, kitchens and loading.

Laboratories need resistance to the chemicals actually in use, which often means a novolac or other upgraded topcoat.

Slip Resistance

Smooth, glossy resin is slippery when wet. Slip resistance is added with aggregate: fine silica or polymer grit mixed into the topcoat for a light texture, or quartz and aluminum oxide broadcast into the system for a heavier one. Aluminum oxide is harder and sharper than quartz and keeps its grip longer under traffic.

The trade-off is cleanability: deep texture holds grease, so specify texture by zone and plan the cleaning program with the floor.

Slip test reporting is a muddle. DCOF testing to ANSI A326.3 was written for ceramic tile and other hard-surface flooring, and you will see it quoted for resinous floors too, alongside static coefficient values from older methods and pendulum results in some specifications. The numbers are not interchangeable. Ask which test was used, wet or dry, and on which texture, then ask for a sample board with the proposed texture and walk on it wet.

Moisture Testing and Mitigation

Vapor rising through the slab is the most common cause of resinous floor failure, often months after installation. Test before the bid is final; the manufacturer's data sheet sets the limit.

ASTM F2170 (in-situ relative humidity). Probes go into holes drilled typically to 40% of slab depth for a slab drying from the top, equilibrate, and are read as percent RH. Most manufacturers now prefer it because it measures moisture within the slab.

ASTM F1869 (calcium chloride). A dish sealed under a dome measures surface vapor emission in pounds per 1,000 sq ft per 24 hours. It is older and more sensitive to conditions, but still appears in many specs.

Test enough locations, under normal operating conditions. Many standard epoxies have limits around 75–85% RH or about 3 lbs MVER; check the specific product.

Mitigation primers. A too-wet slab gets a moisture-mitigating epoxy primer first, typically 100% solids, some rated to very high RH. They add roughly $1–$3 per sq ft, far less than replacing a failed floor. Urethane cement often tolerates higher moisture on its own. Surface Profile and Preparation

Resin bonds mechanically, so the concrete must be clean, sound and roughened. The reference is the International Concrete Repair Institute's surface profile guideline, which defines Concrete Surface Profile levels from CSP 1 (nearly flat) to CSP 10 (very rough), with physical replica chips for comparison.

  • Thin films: generally CSP 1–3, achieved by diamond grinding.
  • High-build and self-leveling: roughly CSP 2–5, by grinding or shot blasting.
  • Broadcast, mortar and urethane cement: commonly CSP 3–6, often shot blasted, with anchor or keyway cuts at edges, drains and terminations so the system cannot peel back.

The manufacturer's data sheet governs. On commercial jobs, acid etching is rarely acceptable; it does not remove contaminants or old coatings and leaves residue. Our acid etching vs grinding guide explains why mechanical prep wins even on small floors. Oil-soaked concrete in older plants may need degreasing, heat treatment or removal of the contaminated layer before any coating will bond.

Planning the Downtime

Downtime drives system choice as much as performance does. Plan backwards from the shutdown you can actually get.

Install time. A high-build epoxy on a large, empty floor can be prepared and coated in a day or two, then needs time to cure. Broadcast and mortar systems take several days because each layer must cure before the next. Polyaspartic and MMA compress the install to a shift or two.

Return to service. Epoxy typically allows foot traffic after about 24 hours and forklifts after 3–7 days, depending on temperature and product. Urethane cement often reopens to light traffic within a day. Polyaspartic and MMA can reopen the same day. Full chemical resistance usually takes longer than the traffic window, so hold off on harsh cleaning until the data sheet allows it. Our epoxy cure time guide explains why cold slabs slow everything down.

Conditions. Most resins need the slab and air within a temperature range, and the slab surface should stay at least 5°F above the dew point to prevent amine blush and bond problems. Unheated buildings in winter may need temporary heat or a cold-tolerant chemistry.

Odor and phasing. 100% solids epoxy is relatively low odor; MMA needs isolation and ventilation. Phasing keeps production running but adds mobilizations and seams, so put seams at doorways or under racking.

Writing and Comparing Bids

Commercial bids vary widely because contractors fill in unspecified details differently. The fix is to define the floor before asking for prices, so every bid quotes the same work.

A good bid request or specification includes:

  • Areas and drawings, with square footage for each zone and the system required in each.
  • The exposure: traffic type (foot, pallet jack, forklift, steel wheels), chemicals, temperatures, washdown method and cleaning chemicals.
  • The system build-up: manufacturer and product for each layer, thickness of each layer and total, aggregate type, texture and topcoat.
  • Preparation method and target CSP, plus removal of existing coatings or tile.
  • Moisture testing: who does it, which method, how many tests, and how mitigation will be priced if needed.
  • Repairs and details: crack and spall allowance, joint treatment by joint type, cove base height and linear footage, drain terminations, slope correction, line striping.
  • Schedule: working hours, shutdown windows, phasing and return-to-service requirements.
  • Warranty terms and exclusions.

Line returned bids up on total thickness, preparation, mitigation, cove and joints, and warranty, not just the bottom line. A bid 30% lower often has a thinner build, assumes a dry slab, or omits cove and joint work. Ask for references on similar facilities and a mock-up area before the full install.

💡 One question that sorts bidders quickly: "What happens if the moisture test fails?" A contractor who answers with a specific primer, an RH limit and a price per square foot has done this before. One who says it will probably be fine is guessing with your floor.

Warranties and Manufacturers

Commercial floors usually carry two layers of warranty. The installer warrants workmanship, commonly for one to a few years. The manufacturer warrants the material, and some offer a combined system warranty when an approved or certified installer uses their full system. Read the exclusions: moisture vapor, chemical exposure beyond the published ratings, impact damage, improper cleaning and slab cracking are commonly excluded. A warranty that excludes moisture on a slab nobody tested is worth very little.

Ask who honors it if the installer closes, and whether the manufacturer will inspect the prepared surface before coating.

Manufacturers. Established resinous flooring makers include Sika, Stonhard, Tnemec, Dur-A-Flex, Elite Crete Systems and Sherwin-Williams, among others. Stonhard is known for both making and installing its systems, giving single-source responsibility; most others sell through approved contractors. Product lines differ, so compare the specific system for your exposure; a strong local installer using a reputable system matters more than the logo on the pail.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does commercial epoxy flooring cost per square foot?

Roughly $3–$5 installed for a thin-film roll coat, $4–$7 for high-build 100% solids epoxy, $6–$10 for a 1/16"–1/8" broadcast or slurry system, and $8–$12 or more for a quarter-inch troweled mortar. Urethane cement commonly runs $7–$12+ and polyaspartic $5–$10. Large open floors land at the low end; small rooms with drains, equipment and cove base land at or above the top. Moisture mitigation, coating removal and after-hours work are the usual additions.

What is the best flooring for a commercial kitchen?

Urethane cement is the usual spec wherever there is hot water or steam washdown, because it tolerates thermal shock that cracks and delaminates standard epoxy. A typical kitchen system is about 1/4" with broadcast aggregate for slip resistance, thicker near kettles and fryers, with a 4–6 inch integral cove base and proper drain terminations. If the kitchen can only close for one night, MMA is the fast-cure alternative.

What epoxy system is best for a warehouse with forklifts?

A broadcast or slurry epoxy at 1/16"–1/8" in active aisles, with a troweled mortar at docks and other impact zones; high-build epoxy works in lighter areas. Joint treatment matters as much as the coating: fill control joints flush with a semi-rigid filler and honor moving expansion joints rather than bridging them. A urethane or polyaspartic topcoat adds abrasion resistance and can be renewed later.

How long does industrial epoxy flooring last?

It depends far more on thickness and exposure than on brand. A thin film in a forklift aisle may need recoating within a few years, while the same film in dry storage lasts much longer. Broadcast, mortar and urethane cement systems in the right application commonly last ten years or more, with periodic topcoat renewal in traffic lanes. A good system on a poorly prepared or wet slab can still fail within a year.

Do commercial floors need moisture testing before coating?

Yes. Vapor rising through the slab is the most common cause of blistering and delamination, and most manufacturers require testing to support the warranty. ASTM F2170 relative humidity probes are the preferred method; the ASTM F1869 calcium chloride test still appears in many specs. If readings exceed the product's limit, a moisture-mitigating primer typically adds $1–$3 per square foot.

What is the difference between epoxy and urethane cement?

Epoxy is a two-part resin that cures into a hard, chemically resistant film or mortar in a wide range of colors and looks, but it handles thermal shock poorly. Urethane cement is a polyurethane-modified cementitious system that moves more like concrete as temperatures change, tolerates hot washdown and often higher slab moisture, and is the standard for kitchens and food plants. Its color range is narrower and mostly matte.

How long does a commercial floor have to be shut down?

From one night to about a week. Polyaspartic and MMA can be installed and reopened within a shift or two, and many urethane cements allow light traffic within a day. High-build epoxy usually takes foot traffic after about 24 hours and forklifts after 3–7 days, and multi-layer broadcast and mortar systems take several days to install first. Large facilities are often phased by zone.
