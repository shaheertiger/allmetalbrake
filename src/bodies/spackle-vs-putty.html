Spackle vs. Joint Compound vs. Painter's Putty

Three products, three completely different jobs, and one paint aisle that makes them look interchangeable. Here is what each one is for, how long each takes to dry, and why patches reappear.

Three products sit next to each other in every paint aisle in America — spackle, joint compound, and painter's putty — and they are used interchangeably by almost everyone who buys them. They are not interchangeable. Each was formulated for a specific job, each fails in a characteristic way when used for the wrong one, and the failures show up months later as cracks reopening, patches flashing through paint, or a wall that looks fine until the light hits it sideways.

This guide sorts out what each product is, when to use which, how long each takes to dry, and how to pick the right one for the repair in front of you.

⚡ Quick Answer: Use spackle for small holes and dings in drywall and plaster — nail holes, screw holes, dents, chips — anything under about 1/2 inch. Use joint compound for taping drywall seams, skim coating, and any repair larger than a few inches. Use painter's putty for filling nail holes in wood trim and window glazing before painting — it stays slightly flexible and is not for drywall. The most common mistake is using joint compound for a small hole (it shrinks and you patch twice) or spackle over a large area (it is expensive and hard to sand flat).

What Is Spackle, and What Is Spackling Used For?

Spackle — the name comes from an old trade brand that became generic, in the same way as Kleenex — is a lightweight, gypsum-or-vinyl-based paste formulated for small repairs. Its defining characteristics are that it dries fast, shrinks very little, and sands easily.

Modern spackle comes in two broad families:

  • Lightweight spackle (vinyl or acrylic based, with a filler like sodium silicate microspheres) — dries in 30 minutes to a couple of hours, shrinks almost imperceptibly, sands with almost no effort, and is the right choice for nail holes and small dings. It is not strong, so it will not bridge anything structural.
  • All-purpose or heavyweight spackle (gypsum based) — stronger and harder, takes longer to dry, sands with more effort, and is better for deeper holes and for repairs that will take some contact.

What spackling is used for, concretely: nail and screw holes, picture hanger holes, small dents from furniture and door handles, chipped drywall corners, small cracks in plaster, and anchor holes. The upper bound is roughly a 1/2-inch hole for a single application and a few inches for a repair built up in layers over a backing.

What it is not for: taping drywall joints (it has no working time and cannot be feathered over the distance a joint needs), skim coating a wall (prohibitively expensive and impossible to work smooth over a large area), or filling anything that needs structural bridging.

Joint Compound vs. Spackle

Joint compound — "mud" on any job site — is a gypsum-based product formulated for a completely different job: covering the seams between sheets of drywall, embedding paper or mesh tape, and being feathered out over a wide area to disappear into the surrounding surface.

The properties that make it good at that make it poor at small repairs. It has a long open time, so you can work it; it shrinks noticeably as the water leaves, so a deep fill needs two or three coats; and it dries slowly, typically overnight between coats.

The Four Types of Joint Compound

If you are buying joint compound, the type matters, and the buckets look nearly identical.

All-purpose — the default. Works for taping and topping, sands reasonably, and is what most people should buy for general repair work.

Taping — a harder, stronger formulation with better bond, intended specifically for embedding tape. It sands poorly. Use it for the first coat over tape, not the finish coat.

Topping — softer and much easier to sand, with less bond strength. Intended for the final one or two coats. Do not use it to embed tape; it will not hold.

Lightweight all-purpose — lower density, less shrinkage, far easier to sand. Excellent for repair work and for anyone who is not a professional taper. This is the one most DIYers should buy.

You will also see setting-type compound, sold as a powder in bags marked with a number — 20, 45, 90 — which is the working time in minutes. This cures by chemical reaction rather than evaporation, so it sets in that window regardless of humidity, shrinks almost nothing, and is enormously strong. It is the right product for deep fills, for repairs that must be finished the same day, and for damp environments. The trade-off is that it is very hard to sand once set, so it is used for the fill coats with an easier compound on top.

Painter's Putty vs. Spackle

Painter's putty is the one most often misused, because the aisle placement suggests it is a drywall product and it is not.

Traditional painter's putty — DAP painter's putty is the archetype, and DAP '33' is the classic glazing formulation — is a linseed-oil-based compound with a stiff, clay-like consistency. It was developed for two jobs: bedding glass into wood window sash (glazing putty), and filling nail holes in wood trim before painting.

What makes it right for wood is that it never becomes fully rigid. Wood moves with humidity, considerably and continuously. A rigid filler in a nail hole in a baseboard will crack and pop out over a couple of seasons; a slightly flexible putty moves with the wood and stays put.

Its drawbacks are real, though. Oil-based putty takes a long time to skin over — anywhere from a few hours to several days depending on the product and conditions — and it cannot be sanded. It is applied by pressing it into the hole with a putty knife or thumb and wiping flush in one pass, so the application has to be right the first time. It also must be primed before painting, because uncured linseed oil will bleed through latex paint and leave a yellow ring.

Latex-based painter's putty solves most of that: it dries in an hour or two, cleans up with water, takes paint readily, and can be lightly sanded. It is less flexible than the oil version, which matters on exterior trim more than interior.

🔧 The Simple Rule: Drywall and plaster → spackle or joint compound. Wood trim, doors, and window sash → painter's putty or a dedicated wood filler. Using drywall products on wood produces cracked, popped-out patches within a year.

Spackling Putty — A Note on the Name

"Spackling putty" is a term that circulates but does not describe a distinct product. It is used loosely to mean either lightweight spackle or a stiff-bodied patching compound. When you see it on a label, read the actual description: if it says drywall, plaster, or wallboard, it is spackle; if it says wood, trim, or glazing, it is putty.

The four variables that move those numbers:

Depth of fill. By far the biggest factor. A skim of spackle over a nail hole is paintable in an hour. The same product packed a half-inch deep into an anchor hole may take most of a day, because the surface skins over and traps moisture beneath it.

Humidity. Air-drying products lose water to the air, so they dry as fast as the air will accept the moisture. At 70% relative humidity, expect to roughly double the label times. A bathroom with no ventilation can extend a one-hour spackle patch to half a day.

Temperature. Below about 55°F, drying slows sharply. Below 40°F, water-based compounds may not cure properly at all and can fail later.

Air movement. A fan moving air across the wall — not blowing directly at it, which can crust the surface — cuts drying time substantially. This is the easiest variable to control and the most useful.

How to Tell When It Is Actually Dry

Color is the reliable indicator for most products. Wet spackle and joint compound are noticeably darker; both dry to a uniform lighter shade, and most modern spackles are formulated with a color-change indicator that shifts from pink or blue to white when dry. If the patch shows any darker area — particularly a ring around the edge or a spot in the center — it is still wet underneath.

The temptation to paint early is worth resisting specifically. Paint over a damp patch and you seal the moisture in; the patch shrinks under the paint film as it finally dries, and you end up with a visible depression and sometimes a crack. Give it the extra hour.

How to Patch a Hole Properly

Nail Holes and Small Dings (under 1/8 inch)

Press lightweight spackle into the hole with a flexible putty knife, drawing the blade across at a low angle so the compound is forced in and the excess is scraped off flush. Do not build a mound — you will only have to sand it back. Let it dry, sand lightly with 150–220 grit on a sanding sponge, and prime the spot before painting. Skipping primer is the reason patches "flash" — appear as dull spots through the finish coat — because bare compound absorbs paint differently than the surrounding wall.

Medium Holes (1/8 to 1/2 inch)

The same approach, but expect to do it twice. Fill, let dry, and check for a shallow depression as the compound loses water; apply a second thin coat, feather it wider than the first, sand, and prime.

Larger Holes (1/2 inch to 2 inches)

Spackle alone will not bridge a void. Use a self-adhesive mesh patch over the hole, then apply joint compound over it in two or three thin coats, feathering each coat wider than the last — the final coat should extend 6–8 inches beyond the hole. Thin coats feathered wide are what make a patch invisible; one thick coat is what makes it show.

Anything Larger

Cut back to a rectangle, install a drywall patch with backing strips, tape the seams, and finish with three coats of joint compound. At this scale you are doing drywall work, not patching, and spackle has no role.

⚠️ Always Prime a Patch: Spackle and joint compound are porous and absorb paint at a completely different rate than the surrounding painted wall. Paint straight over a patch and it will show as a dull or shiny spot in raking light no matter how flat you sanded it. A dab of primer takes ten seconds and is the difference between an invisible repair and one you notice every day.

Choosing the Best Spackle for the Job

Rather than a brand ranking — the formulations across the major brands are closer than the marketing suggests — the useful advice is about matching product type to task.

For nail holes and general touch-ups: a lightweight vinyl spackle with a dry-time color indicator. It is the most forgiving product in the aisle, sands with a fingertip, and the color change removes the guesswork.

For dents that will take contact — door handle impacts, corner chips, anywhere in a hallway: an all-purpose or heavyweight gypsum spackle. It is harder when cured and will not crumble under a knock.

For deep holes you need to finish today: setting-type compound in a 20- or 45-minute formulation. Mix only what you can use inside the window, because it will set in the pan.

For plaster: either a gypsum spackle or a setting compound. Plaster repairs are usually deeper than they look and benefit from the strength.

For wood trim: painter's putty or a dedicated wood filler — never spackle, which is too rigid for a material that moves.

For exterior use: neither spackle nor joint compound. Use an exterior-rated patching compound or an epoxy wood filler, both of which are formulated to survive water and freeze-thaw cycling.

The Bottom Line

Three products, three jobs. Spackle fills small holes in drywall and plaster fast and sands easily. Joint compound covers seams and large areas and is feathered out to disappear. Painter's putty fills nail holes in wood and stays flexible enough to move with it. Using each for its intended purpose costs nothing extra and removes the most common causes of a repair that reappears.

Beyond product choice, two habits do more for the result than anything else: fill in thin layers rather than one thick one, and prime every patch before painting. Nearly every visible repair in a well-painted room is the result of skipping one of those two steps.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between spackle and joint compound?

They are formulated for different scales of work. Spackle is a lightweight, fast-drying paste that shrinks very little and sands with almost no effort — designed for nail holes, dings, and small chips up to about half an inch. Joint compound is a gypsum-based product with a long working time, made for covering drywall seams, embedding tape, and being feathered out over a wide area to disappear into the wall. Joint compound shrinks noticeably as it dries, so a deep fill needs two or three coats and typically an overnight wait between them. The practical rule: if the repair is smaller than your palm, use spackle; if it is larger or involves tape, use joint compound.

How long does spackle take to dry?

A thin skim of lightweight spackle over a nail hole is touch-dry in 15–30 minutes, sandable in about an hour, and paintable in one to two hours. A deep fill in the same product can take four to six hours, because the surface skins over and traps moisture beneath. Heavyweight gypsum spackle runs roughly twice as long throughout. Four variables move those numbers: depth of fill is by far the biggest, then humidity (at 70% RH, expect to double the label times), then temperature (drying slows sharply below 55°F and water-based compounds may not cure properly below 40°F), then air movement. Most modern spackles include a color-change indicator that shifts from pink or blue to white when dry — trust that over the clock.

What is painter's putty used for?

Filling nail holes in wood trim before painting, and bedding glass into wooden window sash — the two jobs it was formulated for. What makes it right for wood is that it never becomes fully rigid. Wood moves continuously with humidity, and a rigid filler in a baseboard nail hole will crack and pop out within a couple of seasons, while slightly flexible putty moves with it and stays put. Traditional oil-based putty like DAP '33' takes hours to days to skin over and cannot be sanded, so it is pressed in and wiped flush in one pass. Latex painter's putty dries in an hour or two, cleans up with water, and can be lightly sanded, at the cost of some flexibility. Neither belongs on drywall.

Can I use spackle instead of joint compound?

For small repairs, yes — spackle is actually the better product there, because it shrinks less and dries faster. For anything involving drywall tape or an area larger than a few inches, no. Spackle has almost no open working time, so it cannot be feathered out over the distance a taped seam requires, and it is far too expensive to skim-coat with. Trying to tape a seam with spackle produces a ridge you cannot flatten. The reverse substitution is also a mistake: using joint compound for a nail hole means it shrinks as it dries and you come back to patch the same hole a second time.

Can I use spackle on wood?

It is not the right product. Spackle cures rigid, and wood expands and contracts with humidity through every season, so a spackle patch in trim will crack, then pop out, usually within a year. Use painter's putty for nail holes in trim that will be painted, or a dedicated wood filler for larger fills — solvent- or water-based filler for interior work that will be painted, and a two-part epoxy filler for exterior wood or anywhere structural integrity matters. If the wood will be stained rather than painted, use a stainable wood filler and test it on an offcut first, because fillers rarely take stain at the same rate as the surrounding wood.

Do I need to prime spackle before painting?

Yes, and skipping it is the single most common reason a repair stays visible. Spackle and joint compound are porous and absorb paint at a completely different rate than the surrounding painted wall, so paint applied directly over a patch dries to a different sheen. The patch then shows as a dull or shiny spot in raking light no matter how perfectly you sanded it — painters call this flashing. A dab of any decent primer over the patch takes seconds and equalizes the absorption. This matters more with higher-sheen paints (eggshell, satin, semi-gloss) than with flat, but flat walls flash too, just less obviously.

What is the best spackle to buy?

Match the product type to the job rather than chasing brands, because the major formulations are closer than the marketing suggests. For nail holes and general touch-ups, buy a lightweight vinyl spackle with a dry-time color indicator — it is the most forgiving product in the aisle. For dents that will take contact, such as door handle impacts and hallway corners, buy an all-purpose or heavyweight gypsum spackle, which cures harder and will not crumble under a knock. For deep holes you need finished the same day, buy a setting-type compound in a 20- or 45-minute formulation. For plaster, use gypsum spackle or setting compound. And for exterior repairs, use neither — buy an exterior-rated patching compound or epoxy filler.

What are the different types of joint compound?

Four, and the buckets look nearly identical. All-purpose is the default: adequate for both taping and topping and the sensible general-purpose choice. Taping compound is harder with better bond strength, intended specifically for embedding tape, and it sands poorly — use it for the first coat only. Topping compound is softer and much easier to sand but has little bond strength, so it belongs on the final one or two coats and must never be used to embed tape. Lightweight all-purpose has lower density, less shrinkage, and sands far more easily, which makes it the best choice for most DIY repair work. Separately, setting-type compound comes as a powder labeled 20, 45, or 90 — the working time in minutes — and cures chemically rather than by evaporation, making it strong, low-shrinkage, and ideal for deep fills and same-day work.
