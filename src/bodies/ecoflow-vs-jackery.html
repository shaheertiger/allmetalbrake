If you have searched for a "Jackery solar generator" or an "EcoFlow power station," you have probably noticed that the two brands chase each other model for model. Each sells a small camping box around 300 Wh, a 1 kWh all-rounder, a 2 kWh step-up and a big wheeled unit aimed at home backup. Each uses lithium iron phosphate (LFP) cells on its current models, charges from the wall in about an hour at the 1 kWh size, and bundles its own folding solar panels. On the surface, it looks like a coin toss.

It is not quite one. The two companies have made different bets, and those bets show up in the details that decide what living with the box is like. EcoFlow builds platforms: most of its stations accept add-on batteries, talk to a smart home panel, and expose a lot of settings through an app. Jackery builds appliances: its popular Explorer v2 models are lighter for their capacity, simpler to run, and fixed in size. Neither approach is better in the abstract. Which one suits you depends on whether you expect your needs to grow, how far you plan to carry the thing, and how much you want to fiddle with it.

This comparison works through the lineups one size class at a time, then looks at the things spec sheets bury: ecosystems, charging behavior, weight per watt-hour, apps and warranty. Everything here is described from the manufacturers' published specifications rather than hands-on testing, and model details change often, so confirm the figures on the listing you buy from. If you are still deciding how big a battery you need at all, start with our portable power station buying guide and come back here once you know your size class.

⚡ The short answer: Choose EcoFlow if you want room to grow — add-on batteries, 240 V home backup, a smart panel and the most output per size class. Choose Jackery if you want the lightest, simplest box at a given capacity and you know roughly how much storage you need. At the popular 1 kWh size, the EcoFlow Delta 3 Plus wins on output and expandability; the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 wins on weight and simplicity. For home backup, EcoFlow's Delta Pro 3 is the more complete system.

The Two Brands in Brief

Jackery popularized the portable power station in the U.S. and still owns the phrase "Jackery solar generator" in a lot of people's heads. Its Explorer line has always prioritized portability and simplicity: a handle on top, a clear display, a sensible set of outlets, and few decisions to make. The current v2 generation moved the popular sizes to LFP cells, added much faster wall charging and introduced app control, but the products are still built around the idea that a power station should be a self-contained appliance you buy at the size you need.

EcoFlow came to market later and made charging speed its calling card, then built outward into a full energy ecosystem. Its River line covers small portable units, the Delta line covers the 1 kWh to 4 kWh range, and the Delta Pro models are designed to sit at the center of a home backup system with extra batteries, smart panels, alternator chargers and rigid solar panels. The products are typically a little heavier and more feature-dense, with more configuration options exposed through the EcoFlow app.

Both brands sell the same models under different bundle names. An "EcoFlow solar generator" or "Jackery solar generator" is simply one of the stations below sold with the brand's folding panels and cables — there is no separate machine inside. Capacity is a wash; the two batteries differ by two watt-hours. The meaningful gap is the inverter. The River 3 Plus can deliver 600 W, which covers a small blender, a projector, a mini fridge, a soldering station or a CPAP with room to spare. The Explorer 300 Plus tops out at 300 W, which is plenty for laptops, a camera charger, string lights, a fan and a 12 V cooler, but not for anything with a heating element or a meaningful motor.

The River also has an expansion path: EcoFlow's optional extra battery for the River 3 series roughly triples its capacity, turning a day-trip box into a weekend box without buying a second station. The Jackery stays at 288 Wh.

Worked example — two nights at a campsite: a 12 V compressor cooler averaging 45 W for 48 hours (2,160 Wh) is already far beyond either unit on its own, which surprises a lot of first-time buyers. Drop the cooler to ice and the budget looks very different: LED lights at 10 W for 10 hours (100 Wh), four phone charges (80 Wh) and a camera battery or two (30 Wh) total about 210 Wh, comfortably inside either unit's roughly 245 Wh of usable energy, even before a small panel tops it up. For a trip that includes a powered cooler, either add 100–200 W of solar or step up to the 1 kWh class. Our camping power station guide works through full trip budgets like this one.

Small-class winner: EcoFlow, on the strength of double the output and the expansion option. If your list is strictly phones, laptops and lights, the Jackery is just as capable — and for that job you may not need a power station at all; see our roundup of portable chargers for power outages.

What the 300 W Gap Means

A 1,500 W ceiling and a 1,800 W ceiling sound close, but a lot of household appliances live between them. Many full-size microwaves draw 1,200–1,500 W from the wall, kettles and toasters often draw 1,500–1,800 W, and a hair dryer on high is commonly around 1,800 W. On the Jackery, those loads either trip the inverter or leave no room for the fridge to cycle on at the same moment. On the EcoFlow, most of them fit, as long as nothing else large is running. If your outage plan includes making coffee or heating food in a microwave, that is a practical reason to lean EcoFlow.

For motor loads, the comparison is closer than the continuous figures suggest. A refrigerator compressor may pull 600–1,200 W for a moment at start-up, and the Jackery's 3,000 W surge rating swallows that comfortably. Both units start a typical kitchen fridge without drama.

Worked example — a summer evening outage: the power drops at 6 p.m. You want the fridge (averaging about 63 W on a 1.5 kWh-per-day model), the router (20 W), two LED lamps (20 W) and a box fan (50 W) until the grid returns. 2 kWh Class: Explorer 2000 v2 vs. an Expanded Delta

At 2 kWh the brands take genuinely different routes. Jackery sells a single 2 kWh box. EcoFlow's most natural way to the same capacity is a Delta 3 Plus with one of its extra batteries attached, which roughly doubles storage. That gives you a fair way to compare the two philosophies directly.

Usable energy is effectively identical, so this is a decision about form and output. The Jackery delivers 400 W more continuous output and does it in a single 40-pound case with one set of outlets, one charger and nothing to connect. That makes it the better RV and road-trip unit, and the simpler unit to hand to someone else in an emergency.

The EcoFlow stack wins on flexibility. Buy the Delta 3 Plus this year, add the battery when you find 1 kWh too tight, and you can still unclip the main unit and take a lighter box camping. If a few years from now you want even more, a second extra battery pushes the system further. You are paying for that modularity with lower peak output and more pieces.

Worked example — an overnight winter outage with CPAP: the furnace is gas but its blower is plugged in, so it needs about 400 W while running and perhaps 30% of the time on a cold night (roughly 120 W average). Add the fridge (63 W average), CPAP (40 W) and router (20 W) and the average load is about 243 W. Either 2 kWh setup lasts about 7 hours by the runtime rule — long enough to get through a night if you let the fridge rest for a few hours. Plugging a furnace blower into a station requires a properly installed inlet or a transfer switch rather than an improvised cord, which we cover in our home backup power station guide.

2 kWh winner: Jackery, for anyone who wants this capacity in one easily moved box. Choose the EcoFlow stack if you value starting smaller and growing, or splitting the system between home and trips.

Whole-Home Class

Above 3 kWh, portable power stations stop being carry-around boxes and become wheeled home batteries. This is where EcoFlow has its strongest product. The Delta Pro 3 combines 4,096 Wh of LFP storage, a 4,000 W inverter and native 120/240 V output, and it slots into a system of extra batteries, a smart home panel and high-wattage solar inputs.

Jackery's answer is its larger Explorer Plus systems, led by a roughly 5 kWh flagship designed around expansion batteries, 240 V output and a transfer switch for home use. Model names, capacities and bundles in this tier change frequently, so rather than quote figures we cannot pin down for every current variant, we describe the comparison in terms of what each platform offers.

Worked example — a two-day storm outage: a household wants to keep a fridge (1.5 kWh/day), a chest freezer (about 1 kWh/day), the furnace blower (about 3 kWh/day in cold weather), lights and internet (about 0.8 kWh/day) and a well pump that uses perhaps 1 kWh/day. That is roughly 7.3 kWh a day. The Delta Pro 3 on its own provides about 3,480 Wh usable — just under half a day at that rate. One extra battery roughly doubles it; adding 1,600 W of panels in a sunny spell can put back several kilowatt-hours a day at the realistic 60–75% of rating over four to five peak sun hours. That is how battery systems reach multi-day coverage: storage to get through the night, solar to refill during the day, and some discipline about what stays on.

Both brands can be configured to do this job. EcoFlow's advantage is that the Delta Pro 3 platform has been through more generations of home-backup hardware and offers more ways to integrate with a house. Before you commit to either, compare the full installed cost — station, extra batteries, panel hardware and electrician — with a standby generator using our Generac generator cost guide, and with a portable gas unit using our home generator guide. For long outages with heavy loads, gas or propane is often still the cheaper answer.

Whole-home winner: EcoFlow. The Delta Pro 3 is the more complete, more proven home system. Jackery's large units are worth a look if you prefer the brand or find a strong bundle, but compare the ecosystem carefully.

EcoFlow's ecosystem is broader and older. If you want to start with a 1 kWh station and gradually build toward a solar-backed home system, EcoFlow has more pieces that fit together. Jackery's ecosystem is narrower but well integrated where it exists: the SolarSaga panels pair cleanly with every Explorer, and its large-model expansion batteries and transfer switch cover the home-backup basics.

One caution applies to both: an ecosystem is a lock-in. Add-on batteries from one brand do not fit the other, and smart panels are brand-specific. Solar panels are the exception — as long as the connector, voltage and current fall within the station's published input limits, panels from either brand or a third party will charge any of these units. That makes panels the safest accessory to buy first.

Charging Speed and Solar

EcoFlow made very fast wall charging its signature early on, and Jackery has largely caught up in the v2 generation. At the 1 kWh size, both brands now quote roughly an hour from empty to full on a household outlet — the Jackery in an "emergency" or fast mode enabled through its app, the EcoFlow by default. The practical difference is small, but it is worth knowing that a Jackery straight out of the box may charge more slowly until you change the setting.

Why fast charging matters: in a rolling outage or a storm where the grid flickers back for an hour or two between failures, a station that refills in an hour is ready for the next drop. One that takes four or five hours is not. Both brands' current units clear that bar.

Apps, Warranty and Support

Apps

Both brands now offer phone apps that connect over Wi-Fi or Bluetooth and show state of charge, input and output watts, and estimated runtime. EcoFlow's app is the deeper of the two: it exposes charge and discharge limits, charging speed, AC and DC output toggles, standby timers and, on the larger units, home-panel circuit control and energy scheduling. That depth is great for people who like to tune a system and slightly overwhelming for those who do not.

Jackery's app is simpler, and the stations themselves are designed to be used without it. The main reason to install it is to switch on fast charging and to adjust a few battery-protection settings. If you are buying a power station for a parent or a relative who will never open an app, that design choice counts in Jackery's favor.

One tip for either brand: set the charge limits and update the firmware once, at home, before you need the station. Outages are a bad time to discover that a feature lives behind an app login.

Warranty

Both brands offer roughly five years of warranty coverage on their current LFP models, which is the clearest signal that each trusts its cells and inverters for the long haul. Terms vary by model, by retailer and by whether you register the product, so read the warranty page for the specific unit and keep proof of purchase. Buying from the brand directly or an authorized retailer avoids disputes about grey-market units.

Support and Longevity

Both companies are large, established and widely stocked in the U.S., with replacement cables, chargers and accessories easy to find. Both use LFP chemistry rated for thousands of cycles on current models, so either should outlast the warranty with sensible storage — keep it at 50–80% charge, top it up every few months, and avoid charging below freezing.

The Alternatives: Bluetti and Anker SOLIX

EcoFlow and Jackery are not the only serious choices, and comparing them only to each other can hide a better deal.

Bluetti often competes on price. The Bluetti Elite 100 V2 matches the Delta 3 Plus on paper with 1,024 Wh of LFP storage and 1,800 W of output, in a roughly 25-pound case that is lighter than the EcoFlow. Its ecosystem and app are less developed, but if it is discounted below both big names, it is a strong 1 kWh buy.

Anker SOLIX competes at both ends. The SOLIX C300X is a 288 Wh LFP unit with a 300 W inverter at about 9 pounds, a natural rival to the small Jackery for charging duty. At the top, the SOLIX F3800 pairs 3,840 Wh of LFP storage with a 6,000 W, 120/240 V inverter — more output than the Delta Pro 3, at about 132 pounds. If your critical load is a big 240 V well pump or emergency EV charging, the F3800 deserves a place on the shortlist alongside the EcoFlow.

Choose Jackery if you will carry the station often, you know the size you need, and you want an appliance that works without fuss. Jackery wins on portability at every size and offers the best single-box 2 kWh unit of the two.

If you are torn at 1 kWh, ask one question: will you ever want 2 kWh? If the answer is "maybe," buy the Delta 3 Plus and add a battery later. If the answer is "no," the Explorer 1000 v2 is lighter and just as capable for the jobs you have in mind. And whichever you choose, the sizing arithmetic matters more than the logo — the right size from either brand beats the wrong size from the "better" one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is EcoFlow or Jackery better?

Neither is better across the board; they are built for different priorities. EcoFlow generally offers more output at each size class, broader expansion with add-on batteries, smart home panels and a deeper app, which makes it the stronger choice for home backup and anyone who expects their needs to grow. Jackery generally offers lighter units for the capacity, simpler controls and the best single-box 2 kWh option, which makes it the stronger choice for camping, RV trips and buyers who want an appliance rather than a system. Both use LFP batteries on current models and both offer roughly five years of warranty coverage.

What is the difference between a Jackery solar generator and a Jackery power station?

Only the bundle. A Jackery solar generator is a Jackery Explorer power station sold together with one or more of the brand's SolarSaga folding panels and the cables to connect them. The station inside is identical to the one sold alone. The same is true of an EcoFlow solar generator, which is a River, Delta or Delta Pro station packaged with EcoFlow panels. Bundles can be good value when discounted, but you can also buy the station alone and add panels later, including third-party panels that fall within the station's published solar input voltage and current limits.

Which is better for camping, EcoFlow or Jackery?

For most campers, Jackery has the edge at 1 kWh and above because its units are lighter for their capacity: the Explorer 1000 v2 stores 1,070 Wh at about 24 pounds, against about 28 pounds for the EcoFlow Delta 3 Plus, and the Explorer 2000 v2 puts 2 kWh in a single 40-pound box. At the small end, EcoFlow's River 3 Plus wins because its 600 W inverter runs more devices than Jackery's 300 W Explorer 300 Plus and it accepts a snap-on extra battery. Either way, use DC outputs where you can to stretch runtime.

Which is better for home backup during power outages?

EcoFlow is the stronger home-backup platform. At 1 kWh, the Delta 3 Plus offers 1,800 W of output, enough for a microwave or coffee maker alongside a fridge, and it accepts add-on batteries. At the top, the Delta Pro 3 combines 4,096 Wh of storage, 4,000 W of output and native 120/240 V with a smart home panel, extra batteries and high solar input. Jackery offers large Explorer Plus systems with expansion and a transfer switch, but its ecosystem is narrower. For any hardwired circuits, use a transfer switch or interlock installed by a licensed electrician.

Can I use Jackery solar panels with an EcoFlow power station, or the other way around?

Usually yes, with the right adapter. Solar panels are the most interchangeable part of either system because a panel simply supplies DC power. What matters is that the panel's voltage and current, alone or wired with others, fall within the station's published solar input range, and that the connectors match or are adapted correctly. Wiring panels in series raises voltage, and open-circuit voltage climbs further on cold, sunny mornings, so leave a safety margin below the maximum. Add-on batteries and smart panels, by contrast, are brand-specific and do not cross over.

How long will an EcoFlow or Jackery power station run a refrigerator?

Use the rule runtime equals capacity in watt-hours times 0.85, divided by the average load. A modern refrigerator uses about 1 to 2 kWh a day, or roughly 40 to 85 watts on average once the compressor cycling is accounted for. On that basis, the EcoFlow Delta 3 Plus or Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 runs a typical fridge for about 10 to 20 hours, a 2 kWh unit for roughly one day, and the Delta Pro 3 for about two days. Both 1 kWh units can handle the 600 to 1,200 watt surge when the compressor starts.

Are EcoFlow and Jackery power stations expandable?

Most EcoFlow stations are. The River 3 Plus accepts a snap-on extra battery, the Delta 3 Plus can be paired with extra batteries to reach about 2 kWh and beyond, and the Delta Pro 3 scales into the tens of kilowatt-hours. Jackery's popular Explorer v2 models, including the 1000 v2 and 2000 v2, are fixed-capacity, so the upgrade path is buying a larger unit or a second one. Jackery's large Explorer Plus home-backup models do support expansion batteries. If you are unsure how much capacity you will eventually want, starting with an expandable platform avoids buying twice.

Do EcoFlow and Jackery have the same warranty?

Broadly, yes. Both brands offer roughly five years of warranty coverage on their current LFP power stations, a big improvement on the two-year terms common a few years ago. The exact length and conditions can vary by model, by retailer and by whether you register the product, and accessories such as panels may carry separate terms. Buy from the manufacturer or an authorized retailer, register the unit, and keep your receipt. Storing the station at 50 to 80 percent charge and away from freezing temperatures also protects the battery and keeps you within the warranty's normal-use expectations.
