Best Ceiling Paint — Flat, Thick and Forgiving Overhead

A ceiling is the one surface lit from the side, painted upside down and seen from every angle in the room. Ceiling paint is formulated for exactly that, and the stained or steamy ceilings need something more.

For a normal living-room or bedroom ceiling, the best paint is a dedicated flat or ultra-flat ceiling white — Benjamin Moore Waterborne Ceiling Paint (508) if you can get to a Benjamin Moore dealer, Behr Premium Plus Ceiling Paint if you are buying at a big-box store. Both are thicker than wall paint so they spatter less, flatter than wall paint so they hide the seams and patches that side-light exposes, and slow enough to dry that you can keep a wet edge across the whole ceiling.

The exceptions are where people go wrong. A water stain needs a shellac or oil-based primer before any ceiling paint, or it bleeds straight back through. A bathroom needs a mildew-resistant paint in a slightly higher sheen. And a popcorn ceiling needs testing before a roller goes anywhere near it. Get those right and the product choice in between is a fairly small decision.

⚡ The quick answer: Ordinary ceiling — an ultra-flat ceiling white such as BM 508 or Behr Premium Plus Ceiling, two coats, 3/8" or 1/2" nap. First-timer worried about missed spots — KILZ Color Change, which goes on pink and dries white. Water stain or smoke damage — spot-prime with Zinsser B-I-N first, then topcoat the whole ceiling. Bathroom — Zinsser Perma-White in eggshell or satin. Budget roughly one gallon per 150–200 sq ft of ceiling for two coats on smooth drywall.

Why Ceiling Paint Is Different

Every surface in a house gets a paint tuned to its particular abuse — a concrete floor paint is built for abrasion and hot tyres, a deck paint for sun and standing water. A ceiling's problems are optical and practical rather than physical. Flat wall paint will mostly work, but three properties matter far more overhead, and a product tuned for all three makes the job noticeably easier.

1. Sheen: Flat or Ultra-Flat Hides Everything

Ceilings are lit from the side. Daylight from a window and light from wall fixtures skim across the surface at a low angle — raking light — and exaggerate every taped seam, nail pop, patch and roller ridge. Any sheen reflects that light and shows the flaws; a dead-flat finish scatters it, so the ceiling reads as one even plane. Ceiling paints sit at the very bottom of the sheen scale, often sold as ultra-flat, in a bright white with high light reflectance that bounces light back into the room.

2. Viscosity: Less Spatter, Less Drip

Ceiling paint is thicker and more gel-like than wall paint. It sits on the roller instead of flinging off as the roller spins, and stays put instead of running. Rolling directly over your own head and furniture, that low-spatter body is the feature you will appreciate most.

3. Open Time: Avoiding Lap Marks

A lap mark is the stripe where fresh paint was rolled over paint that had started to dry. It is the most common ceiling failure, because the surface is large and continuous with no corners to hide a break. Ceiling paints are generally formulated to stay workable a little longer, giving you time to keep a wet edge moving across the room, and the flat sheen hides any laps that do form.

The trade-off is washability. Ceiling paint is not designed to be scrubbed and goes shiny where rubbed — fine overhead, and a reason not to use leftover ceiling paint on walls in a busy room.

Water Stains and Stain-Blocking

Water stains are the reason most people repaint a ceiling, and the reason most first attempts fail. The brown ring is minerals and tannins carried out of the drywall, insulation or framing, and they are water-soluble. Roll water-based paint over them and the paint redissolves the stain and carries it straight back to the surface — two or three coats later, the ring is still there. The fix has a strict order:

  • Fix the leak. Roof, bath trap, condensing duct — painting first only hides the evidence of the next one.
  • Let it dry completely, then press the drywall. If it is soft, crumbling or sagging, cut out and replace that section.
  • Repair the surface. Scrape bubbled paint, fill and sand flush. Our spackle vs. joint compound guide explains which filler suits which repair.
  • Spot-prime with shellac or oil-based primer, extending two or three inches past the visible stain, because the faint outer edge bleeds too.
  • Paint the whole ceiling. New white on an old ceiling nearly always shows, because the old paint has yellowed and the patch dries to a different sheen.

Smoke, soot and nicotine follow the same logic. Wash them first with a degreaser or trisodium phosphate substitute, rinse, let dry, then seal with shellac, which also locks in smoke odour.

💡 "Stain-blocking" on a ceiling paint can is not the same as a primer. Those paints handle faint discolouration. A visible brown water ring needs a shellac or oil-based primer underneath. If in doubt, prime — a quart of B-I-N is cheap insurance against painting the ceiling twice.

Colour-Changing Ceiling Paint

The practical problem with painting a white ceiling white is that you cannot see what you have done. Wet white over dry white looks almost identical from below, so thin spots and missed strips appear only when daylight rakes across the ceiling the next day.

Colour-changing ceiling paints add a dye that shows pink or purple when wet and fades to white as the paint dries, usually within an hour. Every stroke is visible while you roll; once dry, the colour is gone. KILZ Color Change is the most widely available, and the idea is genuinely useful. Two notes: check cut-in edges promptly, since missed spots vanish once the colour fades; and the colour shows coverage, not hide — an evenly pink ceiling can still need a second coat over a darker or stained surface.

Bathrooms and Kitchens

Bathroom ceilings take more moisture than any other surface, because warm, humid air rises and condenses on the coolest surface it meets. Flat paint absorbs it, mildew grows in the film, and grey speckling appears above the shower. Three things fix it, in order: ventilation — a fan ducted outside and run for 20–30 minutes after a shower does more than any paint; a mildew-resistant paint such as Zinsser Perma-White; and a slightly higher sheen, eggshell or satin, so condensation runs off and the ceiling can be wiped.

Kill existing mildew before painting — a diluted bleach solution or mildew cleaner, rinse, dry — or it grows straight back through. While you are up there, check the caulk line where the ceiling meets the shower walls; our caulk guide explains why the chemistry matters more than the brand.

Kitchens add grease. The ceiling above a range carries a film of cooking oil that new paint will not bond to, so degrease and rinse first, and use a washable eggshell if the hood is not vented outside.

Popcorn and Textured Ceilings

First: if the house was built or remodelled before the mid-1980s, have popcorn texture tested for asbestos before you scrape, sand or otherwise disturb it. Asbestos was used in sprayed acoustic ceilings until the late-1970s ban, and existing stock was used for some years after. Painting intact texture does not disturb it; scraping does.

  • Test a small area. Wet a patch with a spray bottle. If it softens, the binder is water-soluble and a heavy roller of water-based paint can pull the texture off in sheets — spray instead, or seal lightly with an oil-based primer first.
  • Use a 3/4" or 1" nap, load it heavily, and roll once, lightly, in one direction. Back-and-forth rolling is what loosens popcorn.
  • Expect about half the stated coverage, because texture has far more surface area.
  • Consider an airless sprayer for large textured ceilings; it fills the texture without rubbing it.

Knock-down and orange-peel textures are firmly bonded and paint like drywall with a 1/2" or 3/4" nap. And if you ever plan to remove popcorn, do it before painting: paint seals the texture against the water that softens it for scraping.

How Much Paint You Need

Ceiling area is length times width — a 12 × 14 ft bedroom is 168 sq ft. Ceiling paints typically publish coverage around 350–400 sq ft per gallon on smooth drywall; rolling overhead, plan on about 350. One coat can do if you are refreshing a clean white ceiling with the same white; yellowed, stained, patched or coloured ceilings need two. Double the area, divide by 350, round up.

Tools That Make the Difference

Extension pole. Non-negotiable. Rolling from a stepladder means moving it every two feet and losing the wet edge. An adjustable pole of roughly 4–8 ft lets you roll an 8- or 9-ft ceiling from the floor in long, even strokes. Choose a metal threaded tip; plastic threads strip.

Roller nap. A 9" roller with 3/8" nap for smooth drywall, 1/2" for light texture, 3/4"–1" for popcorn. A good woven or microfibre cover sheds less lint and spatters less than the cheapest covers.

Angled brush. A 2–2½" angled sash brush, synthetic bristles, for cutting in the perimeter and around fixtures.

The rest. A stepladder for cutting in, a 5-gallon bucket with a roller grid (holds more than a tray and is harder to step in), drop cloths over everything, and painter's tape along the tops of the walls.

Technique: Rolling Without Lap Marks

1. Prepare. Cover furniture, switch off the power to fixtures and lower their canopies. Patch nail pops and cracks and spot-prime the patches, since unprimed filler dries to a dull spot (the spackle vs. putty guide covers which filler to use). Dust the ceiling with a broom.

2. Cut in a section at a time. Brush a 2–3" band along one wall and around fixtures just ahead of the roller, so the band is still wet when the roller meets it. Cutting the whole room an hour before rolling leaves a visible picture-frame border.

3. Work across the short dimension. Start in a corner and roll strips about 3–4 ft wide across the narrow width of the room. Shorter strips mean you return to the wet edge sooner, and the wet edge is the whole battle.

4. Load, spread, lay off. Load fully, roll a W over about 3 × 3 ft, fill it in, then finish with light parallel strokes in one direction. Don't press hard or rework paint that has turned tacky.

5. Finish toward the light. Run the final strokes toward the main window, parallel to the incoming light, so the roller ridges don't cast shadows. Keep that finishing direction on the second coat.

6. Don't stop mid-ceiling, and judge the result only when fully dry, in daylight. Wet flat paint always looks patchy.

The Short Version

Ordinary ceiling: a flat or ultra-flat ceiling paint — Benjamin Moore 508, Behr Premium Plus, or Sherwin-Williams Eminence for a tired ceiling — or KILZ Color Change if judging white over white worries you. Water stain: fix the leak, spot-prime with Zinsser B-I-N, paint the whole ceiling. Bathroom: Zinsser Perma-White in eggshell or satin, and run the fan. Popcorn: test for asbestos and water-sensitivity first. Then pole, cut in a section at a time, roll across the short dimension, keep a wet edge, and finish toward the light.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best paint for a ceiling?

A dedicated flat or ultra-flat ceiling paint. Benjamin Moore Waterborne Ceiling Paint (508) is the best all-round choice from a Benjamin Moore dealer; Behr Premium Plus Ceiling Paint is the best big-box value; Sherwin-Williams Eminence has the most hide for dull or yellowed ceilings. All are thicker than wall paint so they spatter less, and flatter so they hide seams under side-light. Stained ceilings need a shellac or oil-based primer first, and bathrooms need a mildew-resistant paint in a slightly higher sheen.

Can I use regular wall paint on a ceiling?

Yes — a flat or matte wall paint works, it is just harder to apply. Wall paint is thinner, so it spatters and runs more overhead, and it often has a little more sheen, which makes seams and roller marks more visible. Avoid eggshell or satin wall paint on ceilings except in bathrooms. Leftover flat white is fine for a small ceiling; for a whole room, ceiling paint is worth buying.

How do I paint over a water stain on the ceiling?

Fix the leak, let the area dry completely, then spot-prime with a shellac primer such as Zinsser B-I-N or an oil-based stain blocker. Water-based paint applied straight over the stain redissolves it and the ring bleeds back through however many coats you add. Extend the primer a few inches past the visible edge, then paint the entire ceiling — a patch of new white on an older ceiling nearly always shows. If the drywall is soft or sagging, replace that section first.

Does colour-changing ceiling paint actually work?

Yes. Paint such as KILZ Color Change goes on pink and dries white, so you can see coverage while you work instead of discovering missed strips in daylight the next day. Check cut-in edges promptly, because once the colour fades, missed spots are invisible again. It shows coverage, not hide: a darker or stained ceiling may still need two coats or a primer.

Should a ceiling be flat or eggshell?

Flat, in almost every room. Ceilings are lit from the side, and any sheen reflects that raking light and exaggerates seams, patches and roller marks; flat finishes scatter it and hide lap marks better. The exception is a bathroom, laundry room or poorly vented kitchen, where eggshell or satin resists moisture and mildew and can be wiped. Patch carefully there, because the sheen will show flaws.

How much ceiling paint do I need for a room?

Multiply length by width, double it for two coats, and divide by about 350 sq ft per gallon. A 12 × 14 ft bedroom (168 sq ft) needs about one gallon for two coats; a 15 × 20 ft living room needs about two. Popcorn and heavy texture roughly halve coverage. Above about four gallons, a 5-gallon bucket is usually cheaper and keeps the colour consistent.

How do I avoid lap marks and roller lines on a ceiling?

Keep a wet edge. Cut in only a section ahead of the roller, roll in strips across the short dimension of the room so you return to the wet edge quickly, and never stop mid-ceiling. Spread each load in a W, then finish with light parallel strokes running toward the main window so the roller ridges don't cast shadows. Don't rework paint that has turned tacky, and use a flat or ultra-flat paint, which hides laps far better than eggshell.
