Polymeric Sand Alternatives — What Else Works in Paver Joints

Most polymeric sand failures are installation errors that a different product will not fix. Here is how to tell those apart from the cases where an alternative is genuinely the right call.

Polymeric sand is the default recommendation for paver joints, and for most installations it deserves to be. But it has a genuine failure rate, a set of conditions it does not tolerate, and a haze risk that makes some people unwilling to use it a second time. There are real alternatives, and in specific situations they are the better choice rather than the cheaper one.

This guide covers what actually goes wrong with polymeric sand, the six alternatives worth considering, and which of them fits which problem.

⚡ Quick Answer: The strongest alternatives are PermaSand and EnviroSAND — the first is a lower-cost polymeric-style product with a wider tolerance for damp conditions, the second a plant-based binder system that stays permeable and does not haze. For wide or irregular joints, use a wide-joint-rated stabilising sand. For ground that keeps moving, use plain coarse jointing sand. For permeable installations, use open-graded chip. Nothing matches polymeric sand for a standard patio on stable ground.

The Real Problems With Polymeric Sand

Understanding the failure modes is what tells you whether an alternative is warranted, because most polymeric sand problems are installation errors rather than product faults — and swapping products will not fix an installation error.

Haze on the Paver Surface

The most notorious problem. Any polymeric sand left on the paver faces when you introduce water cures there as a film that is very difficult to remove. Textured and tumbled pavers are the worst affected because their surface relief holds sand that a casual sweep misses.

This is an installation error, not a product failure, and the cure is a leaf blower held at a low angle before any water touches the surface. But it is an unforgiving one, and it is why some installers move to products that do not haze.

Cracking and Crumbling

Cured polymeric sand is firm and only slightly flexible. On ground that continues to move — expansive clay, unsettled fill, tree roots — the joints crack and break loose, and the fragments do nothing useful. This is a genuine limitation of the chemistry, not an error.

Joint Width Limits

Most products specify joints between about 1/8 inch and 1 inch, at a minimum depth of 1 to 1.5 inches. Narrower joints cannot hold enough sand to bind. Wider joints crack because the unsupported span of cured binder is too great. Irregular flagstone frequently falls outside both bounds.

The Weather Window

Polymeric sand needs dry pavers, dry joints, and a reliable dry spell after watering. In a wet climate or a wet season that window can be genuinely difficult to find, and installing outside it is the most common cause of a failed joint.

Reduced Drainage

A cured polymeric joint is much less permeable than plain sand. For a conventional patio that is fine. For a permeable paver system installed to satisfy stormwater requirements, it defeats the purpose entirely.

The Alternatives Worth Considering

1. PermaSand

PermaSand is a polymeric-family jointing sand with a formulation that is more tolerant of imperfect conditions than most. In practice it is more forgiving of a slightly damp substrate and less prone to hazing, at the cost of a joint that is somewhat softer and less durable than a premium polymeric product.

Use it when: you want polymeric-style performance without the strict weather window, or you have had haze problems before. Skip it when: the installation is a driveway or anywhere joints take heavy scrubbing loads, where a premium polymeric product will last longer.

2. EnviroSAND

EnviroSAND uses a plant-based organic binder rather than a synthetic polymer. It binds the sand firmly enough to resist washout and weeds, but it remains water-permeable, and it does not produce the cured haze that synthetic polymers do.

The permeability is the interesting property. It makes EnviroSAND one of the few stabilised jointing products appropriate for permeable paver systems, and it avoids the trapped-water problems that can occur when a fully sealed joint sits over a wet base.

Use it when: drainage through the joints matters, when haze risk is unacceptable, or when you want a lower-impact product. Skip it when: you need maximum joint hardness under vehicle traffic.

3. Wide-Joint Stabilising Sand

Several manufacturers make formulations specifically rated for joints up to 4 inches, using a different binder ratio and a coarser aggregate to prevent the cracking that standard polymeric sand suffers in wide joints.

Use it when: you have irregular flagstone, large-format slabs, or any joint over an inch. This is the correct product for those situations and standard polymeric sand is not.

4. Plain Coarse Jointing Sand

The traditional answer, and still the right one in specific cases. It is inert, permanently flexible, cheap, needs no weather window, cannot haze, and can be topped up at any time with no preparation.

It washes out in heavy rain, strips under a pressure washer, and does nothing about weeds or ants — so expect to re-sweep annually.

Use it when: the ground will keep moving, the installation is informal or temporary, you expect to lift and relay sections, or the joint geometry rules out anything bound.

5. Open-Graded Chip (Permeable Systems)

Permeable paver systems use a small, angular, single-sized crushed aggregate — typically a 1/8 to 3/8 inch chip — in the joints rather than sand. Water passes straight through into an open-graded base and infiltrates the subgrade, which is the entire point of the system.

Use it when: the installation is a designed permeable system. Do not substitute anything bound into one, and do not use chip in a conventional installation, where it will not lock the pavers together.

6. Stone Dust and Screenings

Common in older installations and in some regions. It packs firmly and costs almost nothing, but it holds moisture, stains light-coloured pavers, and grows weeds enthusiastically. We would not choose it today except as a low-cost joint filler on a rustic path where appearance is not a concern.

What About Glue or Mortar?

"Glue sand for pavers" appears as a search regularly, and the honest answer is that it is a bad idea in a flexible paver installation. Mortaring or gluing joints turns a flexible pavement into a rigid one without giving it the structural base a rigid pavement needs. The joints crack, water gets in, and the repair is far harder than re-sanding. Mortared joints belong on a properly built concrete base, not on a sand-set patio.

A Note on White and Coloured Polymeric Sand

White polymeric sand looks striking when it goes in and rarely stays that way. It shows every trace of organic debris, tannin runoff from overhanging trees, tyre marking, and general dirt, and on an exposed patio it will read as grubby within a season.

It also makes haze far more visible during installation, because white residue on a dark paver is obvious in a way grey residue on a grey paver is not — which is a real argument for using it only if you are confident in your cleaning technique.

Grey is the safe default on grey and charcoal pavers, tan on warm buff and terracotta tones. And judge any colour from a dry cured sample rather than the bag, because all of them dry substantially lighter than they look wet.

Fixing Failed Polymeric Sand Before You Switch Products

Before concluding that polymeric sand does not work for your patio, it is worth checking whether the installation was the problem. In our experience the great majority of failures trace to one of five things.

  1. Damp pavers or joints at installation. The binder starts curing before the sand reaches full depth, producing a crust over loose fill. Dew counts. A patio rinsed the previous evening counts.
  2. Joints not filled to full depth. Sweep in, compact, sweep in again, and repeat until the joints stay full about 1/8 inch below the chamfer. A half-filled joint has half the strength.
  3. Sand left on the paver faces. Blow the surface completely clean with a leaf blower at a low angle before any water. Check chamfers and textured surfaces carefully.
  4. Wrong watering. Follow the staged misting sequence on the label exactly. A hose blast flushes binder out of the joint and onto the surface, weakening the joint and creating haze simultaneously.
  5. Rain during cure. Most products need several hours of dry weather after watering, some 24. Check the forecast with a real margin.

If all five were done correctly and the joints still failed, the cause is more likely ground movement, joint geometry, or drainage — and that is when an alternative product genuinely helps.

🔧 Removing Failed Polymeric Sand: There is no chemical shortcut. Clear the joints mechanically with a joint-cleaning blade on an oscillating multi-tool, a masonry chisel, or a joint rake, to a depth of at least an inch. Blow the joints out thoroughly, let everything dry, and then install the replacement product. Fresh material bonds to sound cured material below it but not to loose crumbs.

The Verdict

For a standard residential patio, walkway, or driveway on stable ground with joints between 1/8 and 1 inch, nothing outperforms a quality polymeric sand, and most reported failures are installation errors that a different product will not fix. Work through the five failure modes above before switching.

Where an alternative is genuinely warranted, match it to the reason:

  • Haze anxiety or an unreliable weather window → PermaSand or EnviroSAND.
  • Drainage through the joints matters → EnviroSAND, or open-graded chip in a designed permeable system.
  • Joints wider than an inch or irregular flagstone → a wide-joint-rated stabilising sand.
  • Ground that keeps moving, or work you expect to lift → plain coarse jointing sand, and accept the annual top-up.

And avoid the two false solutions: mortaring or gluing the joints of a flexible pavement, which creates a rigid surface on a base never designed for one, and white polymeric sand on an exposed patio, which will not stay white.

Frequently Asked Questions

What can I use instead of polymeric sand?

It depends on why you are avoiding it. PermaSand is a polymeric-family product that is more tolerant of damp conditions and less prone to hazing, at the cost of a somewhat softer joint. EnviroSAND uses a plant-based binder that stays water-permeable and does not produce cured haze, which makes it one of the few stabilised products suitable for permeable installations. For joints wider than an inch or irregular flagstone, use a wide-joint-rated stabilising sand, which uses a different binder ratio to prevent the cracking standard polymeric sand suffers in wide joints. For ground that keeps moving, use plain coarse jointing sand and accept an annual top-up. For a designed permeable system, use open-graded chip.

What are the common problems with polymeric sand?

Five, and most are installation errors rather than product faults. Haze is the notorious one — any sand left on the paver faces when you introduce water cures there as a film that is very hard to remove, and textured pavers are worst affected because their relief holds sand a casual sweep misses. Cracking and crumbling happen on ground that keeps moving, since cured polymeric sand is only slightly flexible. Joint width limits catch people out: most products need joints between 1/8 and 1 inch at a minimum depth of 1 to 1.5 inches. The weather window is demanding — dry pavers, dry joints, and a reliable dry spell after watering. And a cured joint is much less permeable, which defeats a permeable paver system entirely.

Is PermaSand better than polymeric sand?

Not better, but more forgiving. PermaSand sits in the polymeric family with a formulation that tolerates imperfect conditions better than most — it handles a slightly damp substrate more readily and is less prone to hazing, which addresses the two things people most often struggle with. The trade-off is a somewhat softer joint with a shorter service life, roughly three to six years against five to ten for a premium polymeric product. That makes it a reasonable choice for a patio where you have had haze problems before or where you cannot reliably get the dry weather window, and a poor choice for a driveway or anywhere joints take heavy turning-tyre loads and need maximum hardness.

What is EnviroSAND and how does it differ?

EnviroSAND uses a plant-based organic binder instead of a synthetic polymer. It binds firmly enough to resist washout and suppress weeds, but two properties set it apart: it remains water-permeable, and it does not produce the cured haze that synthetic polymers leave on paver faces. The permeability makes it one of the few stabilised jointing products appropriate for permeable paver systems, and it avoids the trapped-water problems that can arise when a fully sealed joint sits over a persistently wet base. Its limitation is joint hardness — under vehicle traffic a premium synthetic polymeric sand will outlast it. Use it where drainage matters, where haze risk is unacceptable, or where you want a lower-impact product.

Can I use regular sand instead of polymeric sand between pavers?

Yes, and in some situations it is the better choice rather than just the cheaper one. Plain coarse jointing sand is inert and permanently flexible, so it accommodates ground that keeps moving — expansive clay, unsettled fill, tree roots — where polymeric joints crack and break loose. It needs no weather window, cannot haze, costs very little, and can be topped up at any time with no preparation, which makes it right for installations you expect to lift and relay. The cost is maintenance: it washes out in heavy rain, strips under a pressure washer, and does nothing about weeds or ants, so plan on re-sweeping the joints annually. What you must not use is fine sand — play sand, masonry sand, or raw silica — which washes out far faster.

How do I remove failed polymeric sand from joints?

Mechanically — there is no chemical shortcut. Use a joint-cleaning blade on an oscillating multi-tool, a masonry chisel, or a purpose-made joint rake to clear the joints to a depth of at least an inch, then blow them out thoroughly with a leaf blower and let everything dry completely before installing the replacement. Fresh polymeric sand bonds to sound cured material below it but will not bond to loose crumbs, so removing the failed material properly is what determines whether the second attempt works. Note this is different from removing cured haze from the paver faces, which starts with a stiff brush and water, moves to a proprietary haze remover, and only as a last resort to a dilute acid masonry cleaner that will etch the pavers.

Should I use white polymeric sand?

Rarely. White polymeric sand looks striking when it goes in and almost never stays that way — it shows every trace of organic debris, tannin runoff from overhanging trees, tyre marking, and general dirt, and on an exposed patio it will read as grubby within a season. It also makes installation haze far more visible, because white residue on a dark paver is obvious in a way grey residue on a grey paver is not. Grey is the safe default on grey and charcoal pavers, and tan suits warm buff and terracotta tones. Whatever colour you choose, judge it from a dry cured sample rather than from the bag or a freshly watered joint, because all of them dry substantially lighter than they look wet.

Can I glue or mortar paver joints instead of sanding them?

You can, and it is a bad idea in a sand-set installation. A paver patio is a flexible pavement: it works by letting individual units move slightly against one another while the joint sand transfers load between them. Mortaring or gluing the joints makes the surface rigid without giving it the structural base that a rigid pavement requires — a reinforced concrete slab rather than compacted aggregate and bedding sand. The result is that the rigid joints crack under normal ground movement, water enters through the cracks, and the repair is considerably harder than re-sanding would have been. Mortared joints are appropriate on pavers laid over a properly built concrete base, and nowhere else.
