Sealing an Asphalt Driveway — The Pros and Cons

Contractors say every three years; some engineers say it does nothing. Both have a point, and the disagreement is really about what sealing is expected to achieve.

Whether to seal an asphalt driveway is one of the few home-maintenance questions where competent people genuinely disagree. Sealcoating contractors say every three years. Some pavement engineers say it does nothing structural and may do harm. Both positions contain real arguments, and the disagreement is usually about what sealing is expected to achieve.

Here is what sealcoating actually does, what it does not, when it is worth doing, and the specific circumstances where it is the wrong call.

⚡ Where the evidence lands: Sealcoating is cosmetic and protective, not structural. It shields the asphalt binder from UV, fuel and oil, which meaningfully slows surface ravelling — but it adds no strength, does not stop cracks, and does nothing about a failing base. Seal a sound driveway every 3–5 years and it will look good and last longer. Seal a failing one and you have painted over a problem.

What Sealcoating Actually Does

Asphalt is aggregate held together by bitumen binder. That binder is a petroleum product, and three things attack it.

UV oxidation. Sunlight breaks down the binder at the surface, which is why asphalt fades from black to grey. As the binder oxidises it becomes brittle and loses its grip on the aggregate, which then releases — the loose stone you sweep up is called ravelling.

Fuel and oil. Petroleum solvents dissolve petroleum binder. A drip under a parked car softens the asphalt directly beneath it.

Water. Not directly damaging to sound asphalt, but it carries the ravelled surface away and, more importantly, gets into any crack and undermines the base.

Sealcoat is a thin surface film that intercepts the first two. It is not asphalt and it adds no thickness worth speaking of — a typical application is a fraction of a millimetre. Its function is sacrificial: it takes the UV and the fuel exposure so the binder underneath does not.

The Case For Sealing

  • It slows ravelling substantially. This is the strongest argument and the best-supported one. A driveway that never gets sealed greys, roughens and starts shedding aggregate; the difference in surface condition at fifteen years between sealed and unsealed is obvious.
  • It resists fuel and oil damage. Where cars park and drip, this matters.
  • Appearance. A freshly sealed driveway is uniformly black and looks maintained. For anyone selling a house, this is a real and immediate return.
  • It makes cleaning easier and gives a smoother surface for snow clearing.
  • It is cheap relative to the alternative. Materials for a two-car driveway run $90–$180, against $4,200–$7,800 to replace it. Even a modest life extension pays for itself many times over.

The Case Against

  • It does nothing structural. Sealcoat adds no strength and no load capacity. A driveway failing because its base is thin or poorly compacted will keep failing, sealed or not.
  • It hides problems. A uniform black surface conceals the early cracking that would otherwise prompt a repair while it is still cheap.
  • Over-sealing causes its own failure. This is the strongest argument against, and it is real. Applied too often or too thickly, sealer builds a layer that is more brittle than the asphalt beneath it and cracks independently — producing a network of fine cracks in the coating that has nothing to do with the pavement's condition. Annual sealing is a common and genuine mistake.
  • Coal tar sealers carry health and environmental concerns. Coal tar emulsion contains polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons at far higher concentrations than asphalt emulsion, and a number of US jurisdictions have banned it. Where it is legal it is durable and cheap; the asphalt emulsion alternative avoids the issue.
  • Poor application looks worse than no sealing. Streaks, tracking, and sealer applied too thick and left tacky are common with rushed jobs.
  • It reduces traction slightly when wet and fresh, and can be noticeably slick on a sloped driveway until it fully cures.

How Often — and Why Annual Is Wrong

Every three to five years is the interval that balances protection against build-up. The signal to watch is colour and texture rather than the calendar: when the surface has faded to grey and feels slightly rough but is not yet shedding stone, the binder is oxidising and a fresh coat is due.

Sealing annually — which some contractors will happily sell — is actively counterproductive. Each coat adds to a layer that is harder and less flexible than the asphalt below it. Once that layer is thick enough, it cracks on its own, and those cracks look alarming while telling you nothing about the pavement. Thick sealer also stays soft longer, tracks into the house, and shows every mark.

The other timing rule: new asphalt needs about 90 days before its first seal. Fresh asphalt is still releasing volatiles, and sealing early traps them, producing a surface that stays soft and marks easily.

Sealer Types

Asphalt emulsion — asphalt suspended in water. Low odour, easy to apply, environmentally the safest of the common options, and the mainstream choice. Less durable than coal tar, typically 3–4 years.

Coal tar emulsion — more resistant to fuel and oil and generally longer-lived, at 4–5 years. Also the subject of PAH concerns and banned in a growing number of jurisdictions. Check local rules before buying.

Asphalt emulsion with polymer or acrylic additives — the middle ground, and the category worth paying up for. The additives improve flexibility, adhesion and life without the coal tar issues.

Fast-dry and fill-and-seal products — convenience formulations. Fill-and-seal versions contain fine aggregate and can bridge hairline cracking, but they still do not replace proper crack filling.

The Right Sequence

Sealcoating is the last step, not the only one. In order:

  1. Clean thoroughly. Sweep, blow, and remove every loose stone — sealer bonds to whatever is on the surface, so a loose stone left in place is a stone the sealer bonds to instead of the driveway.
  2. Kill and remove vegetation from cracks, roots included.
  3. Degrease oil spots. Sealer will not bond over oil, and untreated spots are the first places the new coat fails.
  4. Wash and let it dry fully.
  5. Fill the cracks and let the filler cure — see our crack filler guide for matching product to crack width.
  6. Patch any holes and let them cure.
  7. Then seal, in two thin coats rather than one thick one.

Skipping step five is the most common error. Sealcoat does not fill cracks — it flows into them and leaves a depression, and the water keeps getting into the base underneath.

DIY or Contractor?

Sealcoating is one of the more DIY-friendly exterior jobs, and the quality gap between a careful homeowner and an average contractor is smaller than in most trades — because the outcome depends far more on preparation than on skill with the applicator.

Doing it yourself costs $90–$180 in materials for a two-car driveway plus a squeegee-brush applicator, and takes most of a weekend once cleaning, degreasing and crack filling are included. The main risks are under-cleaning, applying too thick, and starting too late in the day to finish before dew.

Hiring it out runs roughly $0.15–$0.30 per square foot for materials and labour, so $250–$450 for a typical driveway. What you are buying is speed and equipment — a commercial squeegee machine lays a more even coat than hand application — plus, from a good contractor, proper preparation.

The failure mode with contractors is the rushed job: sealing over dirt and loose stone, skipping crack filling, and diluting sealer to stretch it. Two questions filter most of that out. Ask what they do about cracks before sealing, and ask whether they dilute the product and by how much. Anyone who says cracks do not need separate filling, or who is vague about dilution, is selling a coat of paint over a problem.

What It Costs

A 600 square foot driveway needs roughly 10–15 gallons for two coats, at $20–$35 per 5-gallon pail for asphalt emulsion and more for polymer-modified products. Crack filler adds $15–$40 depending on how much cracking there is. The total DIY outlay for a full job — clean, degrease, fill, seal twice — lands between $120 and $250.

Set against a full replacement at $4,200–$7,800 and a resurfacing overlay at $2,500–$4,500, the maintenance spend is small enough that the argument turns entirely on whether the driveway is structurally sound. On a sound driveway it is clearly worthwhile; on a failing one it is money that would be better saved toward the work that is actually needed.

Conditions

50–85°F and rising, no rain forecast for 24 hours, and not in blazing direct sun, which skins the surface before the sealer bonds. Keep off for 24 hours and keep vehicles off for 48. Two thin coats beat one thick one — thick sealer cures slowly, stays tacky, and tracks indoors on shoes and tyres.

🔍 Before you seal, do the screwdriver test. Push a long screwdriver into any suspect area with firm hand pressure. If it stops in the first few millimetres, the problem is at the surface and sealing is worthwhile. If it sinks an inch or the asphalt flexes underfoot, the base has failed and sealing is money spent on a driveway that needs patching or replacing.

The Verdict

Seal a sound asphalt driveway every three to five years, after filling the cracks, and it will look better and last longer — probably closer to twenty years than twelve. That is a real return on $90–$180 of materials and a weekend.

Do not seal annually, do not seal new asphalt inside 90 days, and do not seal a driveway with alligator cracking or standing-water depressions, because sealing a structural failure is the one case where the sceptics are entirely right.

And be clear about what you are buying. Sealcoating protects the surface. It does not strengthen the pavement, close cracks, or fix anything happening underneath.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sealing an asphalt driveway actually worth it?

For a sound driveway, yes. Sealcoat is a sacrificial film that shields the bitumen binder from UV oxidation and from fuel and oil, which are what break the binder down and release the aggregate — the loose stone called ravelling. The difference in surface condition at fifteen years between a sealed and unsealed driveway is obvious. At $90–$180 of materials for a two-car driveway against $4,200–$7,800 to replace it, even a modest life extension pays for itself many times over. What sealing does not do is anything structural: it adds no strength, does not close cracks, and does nothing about a failing base. Judged as surface protection it is clearly worthwhile; judged as a repair it is not.

What are the downsides of sealing a driveway?

Four real ones. It does nothing structural, so a driveway failing from a thin or poorly compacted base keeps failing regardless. It hides early cracking that would otherwise prompt a cheap repair. Over-sealing causes its own failure — applied annually or too thickly, sealer builds a layer more brittle than the asphalt beneath, which then cracks independently in a fine network that has nothing to do with the pavement's condition. And coal tar sealers carry genuine health and environmental concerns from their polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon content, which is why a growing number of jurisdictions have banned them. Poor application also looks worse than no sealing at all: streaks, tracking, and sealer left tacky are common on rushed jobs.

How often should you seal an asphalt driveway?

Every three to five years, and annually is a genuine mistake rather than extra care. Each coat adds to a layer that is harder and less flexible than the asphalt underneath, and once it is thick enough it cracks on its own — producing alarming-looking crazing that tells you nothing about the pavement. Thick sealer also stays soft longer, tracks into the house on shoes and tyres, and marks easily. Rather than working to a calendar, watch the surface: when it has faded from black to grey and feels slightly rough but is not yet shedding aggregate, the binder is oxidising and a coat is due. New asphalt is the exception — it needs about 90 days to cure and off-gas before its first seal.

Does sealcoating fix cracks?

No, and expecting it to is the most common sequencing error. Sealcoat is a thin surface film — a fraction of a millimetre — and it flows into a crack rather than filling it, leaving a depression and allowing water to keep reaching the base underneath. Cracks must be filled first with a polymer-modified rubberised crack filler matched to the crack width, and the filler must cure before sealing over it, usually 24 to 48 hours. Fill-and-seal products contain fine aggregate and can bridge genuine hairline crazing, but they do not substitute for filling anything you could fit a coin into. The correct order is clean, degrease, fill cracks, cure, patch holes, then seal.

Can you seal a driveway that has alligator cracking?

You can, and it achieves nothing. Alligator cracking — interconnected cracks forming small polygons like reptile skin — means the aggregate base beneath has failed and the asphalt is flexing under load with nothing solid to bear on. Sealing covers the pattern for one season and it reappears identically, because the cause is entirely underneath the surface you treated. That area needs a full-depth patch: cut out the failed section extending about 12 inches beyond the visible damage, excavate to firm subgrade, rebuild the base in compacted lifts, and repave. If alligatoring covers more than roughly a quarter of the driveway, replacement is the economic choice. Test with a screwdriver: if it sinks an inch under hand pressure, the base has gone.

What is the difference between coal tar and asphalt emulsion sealer?

Chemistry, durability and regulation. Coal tar emulsion resists fuel and oil better and typically lasts four to five years against three to four for asphalt emulsion, and it is usually cheaper. But it contains polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons at far higher concentrations than asphalt-based products, which has led a growing number of US jurisdictions to ban it — check local rules before buying, because availability varies by state and even by municipality. Asphalt emulsion is asphalt suspended in water: low odour, easy to apply, and environmentally the safer choice. The category worth paying up for is asphalt emulsion with polymer or acrylic additives, which improves flexibility, adhesion and service life without the coal tar concerns.

When should you not seal a driveway?

Five situations. New asphalt under about 90 days old, which is still releasing volatiles that sealing would trap, leaving a permanently soft surface. A driveway sealed within the last two years, where another coat contributes to brittle build-up. Anything with alligator cracking or depressions that hold water, both of which indicate base failure that sealing merely conceals. A surface that has ravelled so far it is essentially loose aggregate, where sealer bonds to stone that then comes away — that needs a resurfacing overlay instead. And any time the weather is wrong: below 50°F, with rain forecast inside 24 hours, or in blazing direct sun that skins the surface before it can bond.

How long does driveway sealer take to dry?

Foot traffic after about 24 hours and vehicles after 48, though both depend heavily on conditions. Sealer cures by water evaporating from the emulsion, so humidity and temperature drive the timeline more than the product does — a humid, cool day can double the figures on the label. Apply at 50–85°F and rising, with no rain forecast for 24 hours, and avoid blazing direct sun, which skins the surface before the sealer bonds properly underneath. Two thin coats cure far better than one thick one: heavy sealer stays tacky for days, tracks into the house on shoes and tyres, and shows every tyre mark. If it is still tacky after 48 hours, it was applied too thick.
