Asphalt Driveway Resurfacing Cost — What You'll Really Pay in 2026

A new 1.5–2 inch overlay costs a fraction of a full tear-out — but only if the base underneath is sound. Here is what each option costs per square foot, what a proper quote includes, and how to tell which job your driveway actually needs.

Resurfacing an asphalt driveway — laying a new 1.5 to 2 inch layer of hot-mix asphalt over the old surface — typically costs $2 to $4 per square foot, or roughly $1,800 to $4,500 for a standard two-car driveway once minimum charges, edge milling and a little base repair are included. That is usually 40–60% of what a full tear-out and repave costs, which is why contractors offer it and homeowners like it.

The catch is that an overlay is only as good as what it sits on. Over a sound base with surface wear, it buys ten to fifteen years. Over a failed base, the old cracks come straight back up through the new asphalt within two or three seasons and the money is gone. This guide covers what each option costs, what a proper resurfacing quote includes, and how to tell which job your driveway really needs.

⚡ Typical 600 sq ft driveway, 2026 ranges: Sealcoating $150–$450 (pro) or $60–$180 DIY. Patching a few failed areas $300–$1,200. Resurfacing with a 1.5–2" overlay $1,800–$3,500. Mill and overlay $2,500–$4,500. Full removal and replacement $4,500–$9,000. If more than about a quarter of the surface shows alligator cracking, ruts or soft spots, resurfacing is the wrong job.

Overlay, Sealcoat, Patch or Replace?

These four jobs are often discussed as if they were grades of the same thing. They are not. Each solves a different problem, and buying the wrong one is how driveway money gets wasted.

Sealcoating

Sealcoating is a thin liquid coating — usually an asphalt emulsion or refined coal tar, often with sand filler — that slows oxidation, keeps water out of fine surface pores and restores the black colour. It adds no strength and fills nothing larger than hairline cracks. On a driveway in good structural shape it is the cheapest way to extend life; on a driveway that is already cracked and settling it is paint on a problem. Our breakdown of what it costs to seal a driveway covers DIY and contractor pricing in detail, and how often to seal a driveway explains why every two to three years beats every year.

Patching and crack repair

Patching fixes isolated failures: a pothole, a sunken corner, a patch of alligator cracking where a delivery truck once parked. Done properly, the failed area is cut out square, the base underneath is re-compacted or replaced, and new hot mix is laid and rolled. Expect $300–$800 as a minimum for a crew visit, or $100–$300 per small cold-patch repair if you do it yourself. Linear cracks are a separate, cheaper job — routing and filling with hot-pour or a quality asphalt crack filler runs $0.50–$3 per linear foot.

Resurfacing (overlay)

An overlay is a new layer of hot-mix asphalt, typically 1.5 to 2 inches compacted, laid over the existing surface after cleaning, repairs and a tack coat. It gives you a genuinely new wearing surface — smooth, black, watertight — while reusing the old asphalt and base as the foundation. It is a structural improvement to the surface, not to the ground beneath.

A mill and overlay grinds off the top 1–2 inches first so the new surface ends up at the original height. It costs more but avoids the problems of raising the driveway: garage thresholds, sidewalks and drainage all stay where they were.

Full replacement

Replacement removes the old asphalt, regrades and compacts the gravel base (adding new stone where needed), and lays 2–3 inches of new asphalt — sometimes in two lifts on longer or heavier-use driveways. It is the only option that fixes a failed base, poor drainage or the wrong slope. It is also the moment to reconsider materials altogether; our asphalt vs concrete driveway comparison covers the cost and lifespan trade-off.

What a Resurfacing Quote Should Include

Two overlay quotes at $2.25 and $3.75 per square foot can both be fair — or the cheap one can be leaving out half the job. These are the line items that separate a lasting overlay from a two-year cosmetic one.

Cleaning and crack preparation. The old surface must be swept, blown clean and free of vegetation, oil and loose material. Open cracks wider than about 1/4 inch should be cleaned and filled before the overlay. Every crack left untreated is a line along which reflective cracking will appear in the new asphalt.

Base repair of failed areas. Any alligatored, sunken or soft section must be cut out, the base dug down to firm material, re-compacted or rebuilt with crushed stone, and patched before overlaying. Expect $5–$12 per square foot of repaired area. A quote that says "overlay entire surface" with no mention of repairs on a driveway that visibly has them is a quote for an overlay that will fail.

Milling at the edges and transitions. Even when the whole driveway is not milled, the contractor should grind a "keyway" or butt joint where the new asphalt meets the garage slab, the street apron and any sidewalk. This lets the overlay taper to zero thickness without a feathered edge that ravels and breaks away within a year or two. Full milling of the whole driveway adds roughly $0.50–$1.50 per square foot.

Tack coat. A thin spray of diluted asphalt emulsion on the old surface bonds the two layers together. It costs very little — on the order of $0.10–$0.25 per square foot — and skipping it is one of the most common causes of overlays that slip, shove or peel in sheets where cars turn. Ask about it specifically.

Thickness, stated as compacted. The quote should name the finished, compacted thickness, not the loose depth. Asphalt compacts by roughly 20–25%, so "two inches" spread loose may end up nearer 1.5. As a rule of thumb, hot-mix asphalt runs about 110 pounds per square yard per inch of thickness, so a 2-inch overlay on 600 square feet needs roughly 7 tons. If the tonnage on a quote does not support the thickness it promises, ask why.

Edge treatment and compaction. Exposed edges along lawns should be compacted and ideally tapered at roughly 45 degrees rather than left as a vertical lip. Unsupported edges are where overlays crumble first. A vibratory roller, not just a plate compactor, should be used on the main surface.

Cleanup, haul-away and permits. Milled material and debris should be removed or, if you want it, left as recycled base for a parking pad. If the work touches the public apron at the street, many municipalities require a permit, typically $50–$300.

💡 The two lines cheap quotes leave out. Base repair and edge milling. Both add real cost, and both are where overlays fail first. If a quote is 30% below the others, check whether it includes cutting out the failed areas and keying into the garage and street — it usually does not.

What Moves the Price

Size and minimum charges. Mobilising a paving crew costs the same whether the job is 300 or 1,000 square feet. Small driveways pay a much higher effective price per foot; some homeowners coordinate with neighbours to share a crew visit, and contractors are often willing to discount when they can do several driveways on one street in one day.

Thickness. Going from 1.5 to 2 inches adds about a third more asphalt. On a 600 square foot job that is roughly 2 extra tons — a few hundred dollars that buys meaningfully better resistance to cracking and rutting. Below 1.5 inches, most standard residential mixes cannot be compacted properly, so a 1-inch "overlay" is a warning sign rather than a saving.

Condition of the existing surface. Base repairs and crack preparation can add 15–40% to the overlay price on a tired driveway. This is the single largest variable between quotes for driveways of the same size.

Access and transitions. Tight access, steep slopes, curves, a long walk from the street for the paver, and multiple transitions (garage, walkway, apron, patio) all add hand work. Each threshold that needs milling adds time.

Region and season. Asphalt plants in cold climates close for the winter, and hot mix needs air and ground temperatures of roughly 50°F and rising to be laid well. Spring and early summer are peak demand. Late summer and early fall often bring better scheduling and occasionally better prices, while still leaving the new surface weeks of warm weather to cure.

Oil prices. Asphalt binder is a petroleum product, and contractors increasingly quote with an escalation clause or a short validity window. A quote held for 30 days is normal; one that can change after you sign is worth reading carefully.

When Resurfacing Works — and When It Won't

The structural part of a driveway is not the black surface you see; it is the compacted gravel base and the soil beneath it. Asphalt is flexible and gets its strength from what supports it. An overlay adds surface, not support, so the deciding question is whether the base is still doing its job.

Good candidates for an overlay

  • Surface wear: grey, oxidised, rough or raveling asphalt, with loose stones on the surface but no sagging.
  • Linear cracks: straight or meandering cracks with sound asphalt on either side, especially those that can be sealed first.
  • Isolated failures: a few small potholes or alligatored patches that can be cut out and repaired before overlaying.
  • Reasonable age: generally driveways under about 20 years old that have had at least occasional maintenance.
  • Good drainage: water runs off rather than pooling, and the edges are not washing out.

Signs the base has failed — replace instead

  • Widespread alligator cracking: the interlocking "crocodile skin" pattern is the signature of base or subgrade failure, not surface ageing. Once it covers more than roughly 25–30% of the driveway, patching it all costs nearly as much as replacing it.
  • Ruts and depressions: wheel-path ruts or bowls that hold water mean the base has deformed under load.
  • Soft or spongy areas: asphalt that flexes underfoot or under a car, often in spring thaw, sits on saturated base.
  • Recurring potholes in the same places after repair.
  • Heaving and frost damage from poor drainage, or tree roots lifting large sections.
  • Previous overlays: a driveway that has already been overlaid once or twice may be too high at the garage and street to raise again without full milling.

The cost logic is straightforward. An overlay on a sound driveway costs about half of replacement and lasts more than half as long — good value. An overlay on a failing driveway costs half of replacement and lasts a fraction as long, after which you pay for replacement anyway, plus removal of the extra asphalt. Our guide to fixing a crumbling asphalt driveway walks through diagnosing which type of failure you have.

A simple test: drive a loaded vehicle slowly over the worst areas after a few days of rain and watch the surface. If it visibly pumps or deflects, or if water squeezes up through cracks, the base is saturated and failing. Probing a cut-out patch area with a steel rod also tells you a lot — a firm, well-graded stone base resists; soft clay or mud does not.

Resurfacing a Concrete Driveway

Concrete driveways can be resurfaced too, but the materials and the logic are different. The usual product is a polymer-modified cement overlay — a thin skim, typically 1/8 to 1/2 inch, trowelled or squeegeed over a cleaned, pressure-washed slab. It hides scaling, surface spalling, pop-outs and worn or stained finishes.

Professional concrete resurfacing generally runs $3–$7 per square foot for a plain broom or smooth finish, and $7–$15 or more for decorative stamped, stencilled or coloured overlays. On a 600 square foot driveway that is roughly $1,800–$4,200 for a basic finish. Full concrete replacement, by comparison, usually runs $8–$18 per square foot including tear-out.

The same rule applies as with asphalt: a resurfacer fixes the surface, not the slab. Structural cracks, sunken or heaved slabs, and sections that rock underfoot will telegraph through any overlay. Slabs that have sunk evenly can sometimes be lifted first with mudjacking or polyurethane foam leveling, after which a resurfacer is a reasonable finish.

Laying asphalt over a concrete driveway is technically possible and occasionally done, but the joints and cracks in the concrete reliably reflect up through the asphalt as the slabs move with temperature. It is a short-term fix rather than a resurfacing strategy.

DIY concrete resurfacers sold in bags at home centres — Quikrete Re-Cap Concrete Resurfacer is a widely available example — can produce good results on small, sound areas. The difficulty is working time: the mix sets quickly, and a two-car driveway needs a crew moving fast between control joints to avoid lap marks. Materials for a DIY job typically come to $0.75–$2 per square foot.

DIY Resurfacer Products vs a Real Overlay

Home centres sell five-gallon pails labelled "driveway resurfacer" — Henry 532 is a long-running example of the type — and the name causes a lot of confusion. These are heavily filled asphalt emulsion coatings: thicker than standard sealer, with more mineral filler and often polymer or rubber modifiers, so they can bridge hairline cracks, fill surface voids and even out a rough, raveled texture.

What they are not is a new layer of pavement. A heavy-bodied resurfacer coating goes down at a fraction of an inch at most, and cures to a film measured in mils, not inches. It adds no structural strength and will not level dips, fill ruts or bridge a crack that is still moving. Coverage is also noticeably lower than standard sealer, so check the pail's stated coverage per coat when pricing a job.

For the right driveway, though, these products are genuinely useful:

  • Good fit: a structurally sound driveway with a rough, porous, grey surface that standard sealer would soak into and disappear.
  • Good fit: extending a driveway a few more years before a planned overlay or sale, for $100–$350 in materials on a typical two-car driveway.
  • Poor fit: alligator cracking, potholes, ruts or soft spots. The coating will crack along the same lines within a season.
  • Poor fit: applying in cool or damp weather. Most need temperatures of at least 50°F and rising and 24 hours or more without rain, and thick coats cure more slowly than thin sealer.

Treat these products as a heavy-duty sealcoat, budget for reapplication every two to four years, and do the same preparation a sealer needs: clean thoroughly, treat oil spots, and fill cracks first with a proper crack filler. The broader trade-offs of coating asphalt at all are covered in sealing an asphalt driveway: pros and cons.

There is no practical DIY version of a hot-mix overlay. It requires a paver or very experienced hand-raking, a vibratory roller, and asphalt delivered at roughly 275–300°F and placed before it cools. Cold-patch asphalt from bags is fine for potholes but cannot be compacted into a durable full-width surface. If the driveway needs a real overlay, this is a contractor job.

Lifespan and Cost Per Year

Headline price is a poor way to compare these jobs because their lifespans differ so widely. Annual cost — total price divided by realistic years of service — tells you far more. The figures below use a 600 square foot driveway.

DIY resurfacer coating$120 – $3502 – 4 years$40 – $120

Three things stand out. First, sealcoating is cheap per year but does not stop structural decline — it belongs alongside an overlay or replacement, not instead of one. Second, an overlay on a sound base and a full replacement come out close per year, so the decision between them rests on the base condition, not the headline price. Third, the worst value on the table by a wide margin is an overlay on a failing base: you pay most of the cost of a good job for a fraction of the life.

Maintenance shifts all of these numbers. Sealing a new overlay after it has cured — usually after 6–12 months — and every two to three years after that, plus filling cracks promptly, pushes overlays toward the top of their range. Leaving one unmaintained pushes it toward the bottom.

Quote Checklist

Get at least three quotes, in writing, and compare them line by line rather than by total. A good quote answers each of these without being asked.

  • Finished compacted thickness of the overlay, and the estimated tonnage.
  • Mix type — a surface or wearing course mix suited to residential driveways.
  • Base repairs: which areas will be cut out, to what depth, at what price per square foot if more are found.
  • Crack preparation: how existing cracks are cleaned and filled before overlaying.
  • Tack coat named as a line item.
  • Milling: full or edge-only, and exactly where — garage, street apron, walkways.
  • Edge treatment and how drainage will be maintained, including slope away from the house.
  • Compaction equipment — a vibratory roller for the main area.
  • Cleanup and haul-away of debris and milled material.
  • Permits for street-apron work, and who obtains them.
  • Warranty: length and what it covers — workmanship, not just materials. One to two years is common.
  • Price escalation: whether the price is fixed or tied to asphalt cost changes, and how long the quote is valid.
  • Payment terms: a modest deposit is normal; full payment up front is not.
  • License and insurance where your state requires them, and a local address you can verify.

Be wary of door-to-door crews offering "leftover asphalt from a job down the road" at a bargain price. It is one of the oldest home-improvement scams: the material is often laid thin over unprepared ground, the crew is gone by evening, and the driveway fails within months. Legitimate paving contractors schedule work and do not need to sell it on the doorstep that afternoon.

The Short Version

If your driveway is grey, rough and has some linear cracks but no sagging, ruts or widespread alligator cracking, a 1.5–2 inch overlay at $2–$4 per square foot is usually the best value in driveway work — roughly $1,800–$3,500 for a typical two-car driveway, lasting ten to fifteen years with sealing.

If more than about a quarter of the surface shows base failure, replace it at $7–$15 per square foot. Overlaying a failing base is the most expensive way to own a driveway.

If the structure is fine and only the surface looks tired, sealcoat or a DIY resurfacer coating buys a few years for a few hundred dollars. And whichever route you take, insist on base repair, edge milling, tack coat and a stated compacted thickness in writing — those four lines are where cheap quotes cut corners.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to resurface an asphalt driveway?

Typically $2–$4 per square foot for a 1.5–2 inch hot-mix overlay, or $3–$5 per square foot if the old surface is milled first. For a standard two-car driveway of around 600 square feet, that works out to roughly $1,800–$3,500 for an overlay and $2,500–$4,500 for mill and overlay. Small driveways cost more per foot because paving crews carry a minimum charge, commonly $1,500–$2,500. Base repairs on worn driveways can add 15–40% to the price, and asphalt prices move with oil, so quotes vary by season and region.

Is resurfacing cheaper than replacing an asphalt driveway?

Yes, usually by 40–60%. An overlay reuses the existing asphalt and base, avoiding demolition, haul-away and base reconstruction, so it typically costs $2–$4 per square foot against $7–$15 for full replacement. The saving is only real if the base is sound, though. An overlay on a driveway with widespread alligator cracking, ruts or soft spots can fail in two to five years, at which point you pay for replacement anyway — plus removal of the extra layer. Per year of service, a good overlay and a full replacement end up roughly comparable.

How thick should an asphalt overlay be?

1.5 to 2 inches compacted is standard for residential driveways, with 2 inches preferred for durability. Thinner layers are hard to compact properly with typical surface mixes and tend to crack and ravel early, so a 1-inch overlay is a red flag rather than a bargain. Make sure the quote states compacted thickness, not loose depth, since asphalt compacts by around 20–25%. A useful check is tonnage: hot-mix asphalt weighs roughly 110 pounds per square yard per inch, so a 2-inch overlay on 600 square feet needs about 7 tons.

How long does a resurfaced asphalt driveway last?

Ten to fifteen years over a sound base, and toward the top of that range if it is sealed every two to three years and cracks are filled promptly. A mill-and-overlay job can reach twelve to eighteen years. Over a failing base, the old cracks reflect up through the new layer and an overlay may last only two to five years. Full replacement with a properly built base lasts twenty to thirty years, which is why the decision between overlay and replacement should rest on base condition rather than price alone.

Can you resurface an asphalt driveway yourself?

Not with hot-mix asphalt in any practical sense — a proper overlay needs asphalt delivered at roughly 275–300°F, a paver or skilled hand-raking, and a vibratory roller, all within the short window before the mix cools. What you can do yourself is apply a "driveway resurfacer" coating, a heavily filled asphalt emulsion product sold in five-gallon pails. It smooths a rough, porous surface and fills hairline cracks for around $100–$350 in materials on a two-car driveway, but it is essentially a heavy-duty sealcoat that lasts two to four years and adds no structural strength.

How do I know if my driveway needs resurfacing or replacement?

Look at what kind of damage you have. Surface oxidation, raveling, scattered linear cracks and a few isolated potholes suggest a sound base — a good overlay candidate. Widespread alligator cracking, wheel ruts, depressions that hold water, soft spots that flex under a car, and potholes that keep coming back point to base failure, which only replacement fixes. A practical threshold is about 25–30% of the surface: beyond that, cutting out and repairing all the failed areas before an overlay costs nearly as much as starting over. Age matters too; driveways over about 20 years old are usually better replaced.

Do you need to seal a new asphalt overlay?

Yes, but not straight away. Fresh asphalt needs time for its lighter oils to evaporate and the surface to harden — usually six to twelve months depending on climate — before sealing. Sealing too early can trap those oils and leave a soft surface. After that first coat, every two to three years is enough for most driveways; sealing annually builds up a film that can crack and peel. Filling cracks as soon as they appear matters just as much as sealing for reaching the upper end of an overlay's lifespan.

What is the best time of year to resurface a driveway?

Late spring through early fall, whenever air and ground temperatures are reliably above about 50°F and rising. Hot mix cools quickly on cold ground and cannot be compacted properly, and many asphalt plants in cold climates close for the winter. Spring and early summer are peak demand, so late summer and early fall can bring easier scheduling while still leaving warm weeks for the new surface to settle. Avoid booking the last weeks before the local plant closes, when crews are rushing to finish.
