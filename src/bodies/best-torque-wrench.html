For most home mechanics the best torque wrench is a 1/2-inch drive click-type wrench covering roughly 10–150 ft-lb, because that one tool handles lug nuts, brake caliper brackets and suspension bolts — the jobs where getting torque wrong actually hurts. The Tekton 24335 is the value benchmark in that slot. Add a 3/8-inch wrench for 10–80 ft-lb engine and drain-plug work, and an inch-pound wrench only if you work on bikes, small engines or anything with fasteners under about 15 ft-lb.

Paying more buys tighter accuracy (digital wrenches reach ±2%), a calibration certificate, a split-beam mechanism that does not need to be wound down after use, or an angle mode for torque-to-yield bolts. It does not buy much if the wrench is used outside the middle of its range, stored wound up, or used to break fasteners loose — which is how most torque wrenches actually lose their accuracy.

⚡ Quick answer: Buy a 1/2-inch click wrench rated about 10–150 ft-lb (Tekton 24335) for wheels and chassis work — passenger-car lug nuts are typically 76–100 ft-lb. Add a 3/8-inch 10–80 ft-lb wrench for engine work and spark plugs. Choose a split-beam (Precision Instruments C3FR250F) if you hate winding a wrench down after every use, a digital (GEARWRENCH 85077) for ±2% accuracy, and a 2–14 Nm wrench such as the Park Tool TW-5.2 for bicycles. Whatever you buy, use it between 20% and 100% of its scale and store click wrenches at their lowest setting.

Click (Micrometer) Wrenches

A coil spring inside the handle pushes a toggle against the wrench's pivot. Turning the handle compresses the spring to the setting; when the applied torque overcomes it, the toggle breaks over with a click you hear and feel. They are fast to use, cheap to make well, and the right default for most people. Their weakness is that spring: stored compressed, it slowly takes a set and the wrench starts clicking early or late. Dropped, the toggle can shift. Neither shows on the outside.

Beam Wrenches

The oldest design: a flexible main beam bends under load while a separate pointer stays straight and reads against a scale. There is no spring to fatigue, so as long as the pointer returns to zero the wrench is still accurate — which is why beam wrenches are the cheapest reliable option and a good backup for checking a click wrench. The catch is practical. You have to read the scale while pulling, square-on to avoid parallax, which is awkward under a car and impossible in a tight engine bay.

Split-Beam Wrenches

A split-beam wrench signals with a click like a micrometer wrench, but the setting mechanism is a secondary beam that is not under load between uses. You set it with fingertip effort and never have to wind it down. That is the whole case for it, and for frequent users it is a strong one.

Digital (Electronic) Wrenches

A strain gauge in the shaft measures torque directly and shows it on a display. Tolerance is usually ±2%, units switch at a button, many models have a peak-hold mode that records the highest value reached, and some add an angle sensor. They need batteries and gentler handling. For an engine rebuild or anything with torque-plus-angle fasteners, a digital wrench with angle is the cleanest solution.

Drive Size and Torque Range by Job

The rule that matters most when choosing size: a torque wrench is only rated accurate from about 20% to 100% of its scale. A 10–150 ft-lb wrench is verified from 30 ft-lb; a 25–250 ft-lb wrench from 50. Pick the wrench whose middle covers your jobs rather than the one with the biggest number.

1/2-inch drive is the wheel and chassis size. It takes the sockets lug nuts need and the handle length to apply 100+ ft-lb without strain. 3/8-inch drive is the engine-bay size, from roughly 15 to 80 ft-lb. 1/4-inch drive wrenches usually read in inch-pounds, typically 20–200 in-lb, and cover small fasteners, trim, motorcycles and lawn equipment. Accuracy, ISO 6789 and Calibration

The ±4% figure on most click wrenches comes from the standards they are built to. ISO 6789-1:2017, the international standard for hand torque tools, allows ±4% for adjustable click-type wrenches with a maximum above 10 Nm, and ±6% for smaller ones. In the US, ASME B107.300 sets a comparable ±4% clockwise tolerance for click wrenches. Both apply over 20–100% of the scale only — which is why the bottom fifth of the dial is not to be trusted.

±4% sounds loose until you put numbers on it. At a 100 ft-lb lug-nut setting it means 96–104 ft-lb, which is entirely acceptable. At 20 ft-lb on a 150 ft-lb wrench — below its rated band — the real error can be far larger, and on a spark plug in an aluminum head that is the difference between correct and a stripped thread.

Counter-clockwise accuracy is usually looser (CDI publishes ±6% CCW) and some budget wrenches are only calibrated clockwise. If you torque left-hand threads, check that your wrench is rated for them.

Store Click Wrenches at the Lowest Setting

The single most important habit: after every use, wind a click wrench down to the lowest marked setting, not to zero. Leaving the spring compressed lets it take a set and drift; winding past the bottom of the scale can let internal parts move out of position. Tekton, CDI and nearly every other click-wrench maker give the same instruction. If a click wrench has sat unused for months, cycle it three or four times at a mid-range setting before trusting it, to redistribute lubricant in the mechanism.

When to Recalibrate

ISO 6789 recommends recalibration every 12 months or 5,000 cycles, whichever comes first, when no other interval is set — and immediately after a drop or overload. For a home garage that torques wheels a few times a year, every one to two years is reasonable. Professional calibration is usually a modest fee, often in the $30–$80 range, and many tool shops and calibration labs accept mail-in.

You can do a rough home check with a known weight. Clamp the drive square in a vise with the wrench horizontal, set it to a value, and hang a weight from the handle at a measured distance from the drive centre: torque equals weight times distance, so 20 lb at 2 ft is 40 ft-lb. If the wrench clicks well before or after, it needs a professional calibration. This is a sanity check, not a calibration — the wrench's own weight and the exact load point add error — but it catches a wrench that is badly off.

💡 Three habits that keep a click wrench accurate. Wind it to the lowest setting after every use. Never use it to break a fastener loose. Never drop it — and if you do, have it checked before trusting it on anything important. Most torque wrenches that read wrong were not made wrong; they were used as a breaker bar or put away loaded.

Using a Torque Wrench Correctly

Run fasteners down first, then torque. Snug lug nuts or bolts with a ratchet or, carefully, an impact on a low setting, then make the final pull with the torque wrench. An impact gun on full power can put a lug nut well past specification before the torque wrench ever touches it — and a click wrench cannot tell you a fastener is already over-tight; it simply clicks. Our Milwaukee vs DeWalt comparison covers impact wrenches with selectable power modes for this.

Do not use it to loosen. Breaking a seized fastener loose can load a click wrench far beyond its scale, and that is the most common way they lose calibration. Use a breaker bar. The same goes for using a torque wrench as an everyday ratchet.

Pull smoothly and stop at the click. Apply force in one steady motion over the last part of the swing; a jerk overshoots the setting before the mechanism can release. When it clicks, stop. A second pull after the click adds torque beyond the setting.

Hold the handle where it is designed to be held — the centre of the grip or a marked line — and pull at 90 degrees to the wrench. Pulling at an angle or choking up on the handle changes the effective lever and the result.

Extensions in line with the drive do not change the reading. A socket extension between the wrench and the socket is fine; keep it straight and supported. A universal joint at an angle wastes some torque and should be avoided where possible.

Adapters that lengthen the lever do change it. A crowfoot or offset adapter pointing straight out from the head increases the effective length, so set the wrench lower: setting = desired torque × L ÷ (L + E), where L is the wrench's effective length and E the adapter's. An 18-inch wrench with a 2-inch crowfoot for a 50 ft-lb spec is set to 45 ft-lb. Turn the crowfoot 90 degrees to the handle and no correction is needed.

Use the specified thread condition. Most specifications assume clean, dry threads unless they say otherwise. Oil or anti-seize on a dry spec lowers friction, so the same torque produces much more clamp load; anti-seize makers commonly suggest reducing torque by roughly 20–25%. Follow the service manual where it gives a lubricated value.

Lug nuts in a star pattern, in two passes. Torque to about half value in a star pattern, then to full value in the same pattern, with the wheel on the ground so it cannot turn. Re-check alloy wheels after 50–100 miles. Torque-Plus-Angle Fasteners

Many modern engines specify fasteners by torque then angle: for example, a cylinder-head bolt taken to a modest torque and then turned a further 90 degrees, sometimes in several stages. These are often torque-to-yield bolts, deliberately stretched into their elastic limit to give a consistent clamp load that torque alone cannot. Friction makes torque a poor measure of stretch; rotation angle is a much better one.

There are two ways to do it. A dial angle gauge fits between the wrench and socket and has an arm you brace against something fixed; it is inexpensive and works with any wrench or breaker bar. A digital wrench with angle measurement — such as GEARWRENCH's 85196 — does both stages in one tool and records the angle without a reaction arm. Either way, torque-to-yield bolts are generally single use: once stretched, they should be replaced, not re-torqued.

A click wrench is fine for the initial torque stage. What you must not do is estimate the angle by eye; a quarter-turn guessed under a car is rarely 90 degrees.

The Short Version

Buy a 1/2-inch, 10–150 ft-lb click wrench first — the Tekton 24335 — and use it for wheels, brakes and suspension. Add the 3/8-inch 10–80 ft-lb Tekton 24330 when you start working under the hood.

Step up to the CDI 2503MFRMH if you need a calibration certificate or truck-level values up to 250 ft-lb, the Precision Instruments C3FR250F split-beam if you torque often and want a wrench you never wind down, or the GEARWRENCH 85077 for ±2% accuracy and a readout. Bikes and small fasteners need a 2–14 Nm wrench such as the Park Tool TW-5.2.

And look after it. Store click wrenches at the lowest setting, never use any torque wrench to loosen, keep jobs within 20–100% of the scale, and have it checked every year or two. A well-kept budget wrench used correctly beats an expensive one that has been used as a breaker bar.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size torque wrench do I need for lug nuts?

A 1/2-inch drive click wrench with a range of about 10–150 ft-lb covers lug nuts on almost all cars, crossovers and small SUVs, which are typically specified at 76–100 ft-lb. That places the value in the middle of the scale, where the wrench is most accurate. Half-ton pickups commonly run 120–150 ft-lb, still within a 150 ft-lb wrench, but heavy-duty trucks and some vans go higher and are better served by a 30–250 ft-lb wrench. Avoid a 3/8-inch wrench for wheels; most top out around 80 ft-lb.

Which is more accurate, a click or a digital torque wrench?

Digital. Most click wrenches are rated ±4% clockwise to ISO 6789 or ASME B107.300, while electronic wrenches such as the GEARWRENCH 85077 are typically rated ±2% clockwise. Digital wrenches also have no spring to drift, so they hold accuracy better if stored carelessly. For lug nuts and most chassis work ±4% is entirely adequate; the extra accuracy matters more for engine internals and torque-plus-angle fasteners.

Should I store a torque wrench at zero?

Store a click-type wrench at its lowest marked setting, not at zero. Leaving the spring compressed causes it to take a set and drift out of calibration, while winding past the bottom of the scale can let internal parts shift. Tekton, for example, says its 10–150 ft-lb 24335 should be stored at 10 ft-lb. Split-beam, beam and digital wrenches do not need winding down because nothing is under load between uses.

Can you use a torque wrench to loosen bolts?

You should not. Breaking a seized fastener loose can load the wrench well past its maximum, which is one of the most common ways torque wrenches lose calibration, and many click wrenches are only rated for accuracy clockwise anyway. Use a breaker bar or ordinary ratchet to loosen, and keep the torque wrench for the final tightening pull.

How often should a torque wrench be calibrated?

ISO 6789 recommends every 12 months or 5,000 cycles, whichever comes first, if no other interval is set, and immediately after a drop or overload. A home mechanic who uses one a few times a year can reasonably go one to two years. Professional calibration is usually a modest fee, often $30–$80, and a hanging-weight check at home will tell you if a wrench is badly off in the meantime.

Does using an extension change torque wrench readings?

A straight socket extension in line with the drive does not change the reading. An adapter that lengthens the lever — a crowfoot pointing straight out, for instance — does, and you correct by setting the wrench to desired torque × L ÷ (L + E), where L is the wrench's effective length and E the adapter's length. A crowfoot turned 90 degrees to the handle needs no correction. Universal joints at an angle waste some torque and are best avoided.

What torque wrench do I need for spark plugs?

A 3/8-inch drive wrench with a range starting around 10 ft-lb, such as the Tekton 24330, handles most spark plugs. Common 14 mm gasket-seat plugs in aluminum heads are specified around 18–22 ft-lb, which is inside a 10–80 ft-lb wrench's accurate band. Taper-seat plugs often need only 7–15 ft-lb and are better done with an inch-pound wrench. Do not use a 1/2-inch 10–150 ft-lb wrench; spark-plug values sit below its rated range.

Is a split-beam torque wrench worth it?

For frequent users, yes. A split-beam wrench such as the Precision Instruments C3FR250F clicks like a micrometer wrench but its setting mechanism is not under load, so it never needs winding down after use and is set with fingertip effort. That removes the most common cause of click-wrench drift and makes changing values during a job faster. For someone who torques wheels twice a year, a budget click wrench stored correctly is just as accurate for far less.
