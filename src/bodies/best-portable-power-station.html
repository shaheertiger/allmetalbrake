Buying a portable power station is the same arithmetic problem as buying a home generator, with one extra number. A generator only has to deliver enough watts; it runs as long as you keep feeding it fuel. A power station has to deliver enough watts and store enough watt-hours, because the battery is the fuel tank and you cannot top it up from a can. Get both numbers right and almost any of the major brands will serve you well. Get either one wrong and you have a heavy, expensive box that either trips when the fridge starts or goes flat at 2 a.m.

The good news is that the category has matured. Nearly every serious model now uses long-life lithium iron phosphate (LFP) cells, charges from the wall in an hour or two, and accepts solar panels — which is why the same products are sold as "power stations," "battery generators" and "solar generators" depending on whose box you read. This guide explains what those labels mean, how to size one properly, and then names eight widely sold units from EcoFlow, Jackery, Anker SOLIX and Bluetti that cover the common jobs, from a weekend campsite to keeping a house running through a multi-day outage.

⚡ The short answer: For most households wanting outage backup and camping in one box, buy a roughly 1,000 Wh LFP unit with at least 1,500 W of output — the EcoFlow Delta 3 Plus or Jackery Explorer 1000 v2. It will run a fridge, router, lights and phone charging through a short outage, and it lifts into a car. For campsites and day trips, a 250–300 Wh unit such as the EcoFlow River 3 Plus is plenty. For serious home backup — well pumps, 240 V circuits, multi-day outages — step up to a 4 kWh class unit such as the EcoFlow Delta Pro 3 or Anker SOLIX F3800, wired through a transfer switch and paired with solar.

Power Station vs. Gas Generator

A portable power station and a gas generator solve the same problem in opposite ways. A generator makes electricity on demand by burning fuel. A power station stores electricity you made earlier — from the wall, a car, or solar panels — and releases it through an inverter. That difference drives everything else.

The single biggest advantage of a power station is that it can sit in the kitchen. No carbon monoxide, no extension cords through a cracked window, no refuelling in the rain. It is silent, starts instantly, and needs no maintenance beyond the occasional top-up charge. For apartments, townhouses, anyone without a safe outdoor spot for a generator, and anyone powering sensitive electronics or medical devices, that is decisive.

The generator's advantage is energy density. A 5-gallon can of petrol holds roughly the energy of dozens of large power stations, and you can refill it. If your outages last days and you need to run a well pump, central heating and a full fridge, a generator — or a large power station paired with solar, or both together — is the honest answer. Many households now do exactly that: a power station for the first quiet hours and overnight, a generator to recharge it during the day in a long outage.

Sizing It — Watts vs. Watt-Hours

Every power station has two numbers that matter, and confusing them is the most common buying mistake.

Output (watts, W) is how much power the inverter can deliver at once. It decides what you can run. A 600 W unit cannot run a 1,000 W microwave no matter how full the battery is; it will shut off the instant the microwave starts.

Capacity (watt-hours, Wh) is how much energy the battery stores. It decides how long you can run it. A 1,000 Wh battery can, in theory, supply 100 W for ten hours or 500 W for two.

Just as with a generator, motors complicate the output side. A refrigerator compressor, a sump pump or a power tool draws a surge two to three times its running wattage for a moment as it starts. Power stations list a surge or peak rating for this, and you want the largest motor's starting surge to fit under it with everything else already running. Many brands also offer a "power lifting" or "surge" mode that runs resistive loads like kettles above the rated output by lowering the voltage — useful, but not something to rely on for motors or electronics.

Doing the Sum

Size the output first. List everything that must run at the same time and add the running watts, then make sure the biggest motor's starting surge fits under the surge rating. Then size the capacity: for each device, multiply running watts by the hours you need it, add them up, and divide by about 0.85 to allow for inverter losses.

Worked example — overnight internet and medical: router 20 W for 12 hours (240 Wh), CPAP 40 W for 8 hours (320 Wh), phones and a laptop (150 Wh). Total 710 Wh ÷ 0.85 ≈ 835 Wh. A 1 kWh unit covers it with a margin; output needs are tiny, so even a 600 W inverter would do.

Worked example — fridge-focused outage: a refrigerator using 1,500 Wh a day, plus router (480 Wh), lights (200 Wh) and charging (150 Wh). That is about 2,330 Wh a day, or roughly 2,750 Wh with losses. A 1 kWh unit gets you through roughly eight or nine hours; a 2 kWh unit about 17 hours; a 4 kWh unit roughly a day and a half — and indefinitely if solar can put back 2–3 kWh each sunny day.

💡 The fridge does not need to run constantly. A closed, full refrigerator keeps food safe for about four hours without power, and a full freezer for up to two days. Running the fridge for an hour or two every few hours, rather than continuously, can halve the energy it uses from your power station during an outage.

How Long Will It Run?

The rule of thumb: runtime in hours ≈ (capacity in Wh × 0.85) ÷ load in watts. The 0.85 accounts for the inverter converting battery DC to household AC, and for the battery management system holding back a small reserve. Running devices from the DC outputs — USB-C or the 12 V car socket — skips the inverter and gets closer to the full rated capacity.

Two things stand out. First, small electronics run for a remarkably long time even on modest units — a 1 kWh station keeps your internet up for nearly two days. Second, anything that heats or cools drains even large batteries quickly. Space heaters, electric kettles, hair dryers and air conditioners are where power stations struggle, and where a generator or a lot of solar earns its keep.

Battery Chemistry: LFP vs. NMC

Older power stations, and some budget models still on sale, use lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NMC) cells — the same family used in phones and many EVs. Nearly every current model from the major brands has moved to lithium iron phosphate (LFP, sometimes written LiFePO4), and you should insist on it.

Cycle life is the big difference. NMC power stations are typically rated for around 500–800 full cycles before capacity falls to 80%. LFP units are commonly rated for 3,000–4,000 cycles or more to 70–80%. For a unit used daily with solar, that is the difference between three years and a decade.

Safety also favours LFP. Its chemistry is far more resistant to thermal runaway, which matters for something that may sit in a bedroom charging overnight.

The trade-off is weight: LFP stores less energy per pound, so an LFP unit is heavier than an NMC unit of the same capacity. For a box that lives at home or in a vehicle, that is a price worth paying.

Solar Generators Explained

A "solar generator" is not a separate kind of machine. It is a portable power station sold with — or able to accept — solar panels. The "Jackery solar generator," "EcoFlow solar generator" and "Bluetti solar generator" bundles you see are the same power stations above, packaged with the brand's folding panels and cables.

How Much Solar Do You Need?

A panel's rated wattage is what it produces in ideal laboratory sun. In practice, a well-aimed portable panel delivers roughly 60–75% of its rating at midday, and a typical good day gives the equivalent of four to five peak sun hours. So a 200 W panel puts back around 500–700 Wh on a clear day; 400 W of panels around 1–1.4 kWh.

That makes the pairing simple. A 1 kWh station needs roughly 200–400 W of panels to refill in a sunny day. A 2 kWh station needs 400–800 W. A 4 kWh station paired with a 1,600–2,400 W permanent array can refill most of its battery daily, which is what makes indefinite off-grid backup possible.

Check the Input Limits

Every power station has a maximum solar input in watts and an allowed voltage range. Exceed the voltage — easy to do when wiring panels in series on a cold, bright morning, when open-circuit voltage rises — and you can damage the charge controller. Stay within the manufacturer's stated range, and remember that panels from other brands work fine as long as the connector, voltage and current fit.

Portable vs. Fixed Panels

Folding portable panels are convenient for camping and quick to deploy in an outage, but they cost more per watt and are fragile in wind. Rigid residential panels on a ground mount are far cheaper per watt and last decades; if your power station is primarily for home backup and you have the space, they are the better value.

Using One for Home Backup

For short outages, the simplest approach is the one that needs no installation at all: put the power station in the kitchen and plug the fridge, router and a lamp straight into it. Because there is no exhaust, it can sit right next to what it powers, and the cords stay short and safe.

Powering Hardwired Circuits

Furnace blowers, well pumps, and many sump pumps are hardwired or on dedicated circuits, and you cannot plug them into a power station without help. The same rules apply as with a generator. Never backfeed through a wall outlet with a "suicide cord" — it is illegal, it can electrocute line workers, and it bypasses every protection in your panel. The safe options are a manual transfer switch, a panel interlock kit with a power inlet, or a brand's own smart home panel, all installed by a licensed electrician.

UPS Mode

Many current units offer an uninterruptible power supply function: the station passes wall power through to your devices and switches to battery within roughly 10–30 milliseconds when the grid drops. That is fast enough for routers, modems, most desktop computers and many medical devices, so they never notice the outage. Check the manufacturer's stated switchover time against your equipment's tolerance, and note that the fastest true online UPS units are still the safer choice for servers.

120 V vs. 240 V

Most power stations output 120 V only. Many deep well pumps, electric dryers, ranges and central air handlers need 240 V split-phase power. If any of those are on your must-run list, you need a unit with native 240 V output (such as the Delta Pro 3 or SOLIX F3800) or two compatible units linked with the maker's adapter.

A power station is a supplement to good outage preparation, not a substitute for it. If your basement floods in storms, a battery backup sump pump is a more reliable dedicated defence, and a basement dehumidifier will draw more than most stations can sustain.

Using One for Camping and RVs

For campsites, the right size is usually smaller than people expect. A 12 V compressor cooler, lights, phones, a camera and a speaker rarely add up to more than 600–800 Wh a day, which a 300 Wh unit plus a 100–200 W panel can sustain on a sunny trip, and a 1 kWh unit handles for a long weekend without panels at all.

Use the DC outputs wherever you can. Running the cooler from the 12 V socket and charging phones and laptops over USB-C bypasses the inverter and can add 10–15% to your runtime. Turn the AC inverter off when nothing needs it; even idling, it draws power.

RV owners have a different calculation. A 2 kWh unit can run an RV's 120 V outlets, a residential fridge and a CPAP overnight, but rooftop air conditioning — 1,300–2,000 W running and more to start — is beyond everything but the largest stations, and only for an hour or two. If you want air conditioning at a site without hookups, a quiet inverter generator is still the practical tool.

The Features That Actually Matter

Pure sine wave output. Every model on this list produces it, and you should not accept anything less. Modified sine wave inverters, still found on very cheap units, can make motors hum, run hot, and upset sensitive electronics.

Wall charging speed. Current units recharge from empty in one to two hours from a household outlet. That matters more than it sounds: when the power comes back briefly between outages, a fast-charging station refills before it fails again.

Expandability. Some systems accept add-on batteries, letting you start at 1 kWh and grow. If you think your needs may expand — solar, a bigger fridge, 240 V — buy into an expandable platform.

Ports. Count the outlets you will actually use. At least four AC outlets, two high-wattage USB-C ports (100 W or more), and a 12 V car socket cover most people. A dedicated 30 A RV outlet matters if you camp in one.

Weight and handles. A 1 kWh unit at 22–28 pounds is a comfortable carry; 40 pounds is a two-hand lift; above 100 pounds you need wheels and a ramp. Be honest about who will move it and where.

Warranty. The major brands now offer roughly five years on current models. A long warranty is the clearest sign a manufacturer trusts its cells and inverter.

Safety and Storage

Power stations are far safer than generators — no fuel, no exhaust, no carbon monoxide — but they are still large batteries and deserve some care.

Keep it ventilated. Cooling fans need clear air. Do not bury it under blankets, run it in a sealed cupboard, or charge it in direct summer sun.

Mind the cold. Lithium batteries should not be charged below freezing; doing so can permanently damage the cells. Most units block charging below about 32°F (0°C), and discharge capacity falls in the cold. Keep it indoors in winter and bring a cold unit up to room temperature before charging it.

Store it partially charged. For storage, manufacturers typically recommend 50–80% charge, topped up every three to six months. Leaving it at 100% on a shelf, or letting it drain to zero for months, both shorten its life. Many units have a storage mode that manages this automatically.

Use rated cords. Extension cords must be heavy enough for the load and length, just as with a generator. A thin cord on a 1,500 W heater can overheat.

Never open it. Swollen cases, burning smells or error codes mean the unit goes back to the manufacturer, not onto the workbench.

Fridge, internet and lights through a short outage — plus camping: a 1 kWh LFP unit with 1,500–1,800 W output: the EcoFlow Delta 3 Plus, Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 or Bluetti Elite 100 V2. This is the right choice for most households.

Overnight outages, CPAP plus fridge, RV trips: a 2 kWh unit such as the Jackery Explorer 2000 v2.

Whole-home backup, well pumps, 240 V, multi-day outages: a 4 kWh class expandable system — the EcoFlow Delta Pro 3 or Anker SOLIX F3800 — connected through a transfer switch and paired with solar. For outages measured in weeks, pair it with a generator or consider a standby unit; our standby generator cost guide covers what that costs.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a portable power station?

A portable power station is a large rechargeable lithium battery combined with an inverter, a charge controller and a panel of outlets in one box. It stores electricity from a wall outlet, a car socket or solar panels and delivers it through household AC outlets, USB-A and USB-C ports and a 12 V car socket. Unlike a gas generator it produces no exhaust, so it can be used indoors, and it is nearly silent. The trade-off is that its runtime is limited by the size of its battery, measured in watt-hours. Sizes range from about 250 Wh pocket units for phones and laptops to 4 kWh wheeled systems that can back up selected circuits in a house.

What size portable power station do I need?

Size two numbers. For output, add the running watts of everything that must run at once and make sure the largest motor's starting surge fits under the unit's surge rating. For capacity, multiply each device's watts by the hours you need it, add them up, and divide by about 0.85 for inverter losses. As a guide, 250–300 Wh covers phones, laptops and lights; around 1,000 Wh runs a refrigerator, router and lights for most of a day; 2,000 Wh covers an overnight outage with a fridge and CPAP; and 4,000 Wh or more, especially with solar and 240 V output, is whole-home backup territory.

Can a portable power station run a refrigerator?

Yes, if it has enough output to start the compressor and enough capacity to last. A modern refrigerator runs at 100–250 watts but can draw 600–1,200 watts for a moment when the compressor starts, so you want at least 1,000 W of output and ideally 1,500 W or more. Because the compressor cycles on and off, a typical fridge averages around 40–80 watts and uses 1–2 kWh per day. A 1 kWh power station will therefore run it for roughly 10–15 hours, a 2 kWh unit for about a day, and a 4 kWh unit for two days. Keeping the door shut and cycling the fridge rather than running it continuously stretches that considerably.

Is a power station better than a generator for power outages?

It depends on the outage. A power station is better for short outages, apartments, indoor use, sensitive electronics, medical devices like CPAP machines, and anyone who wants silence and no fuel to store — it produces no carbon monoxide, so it can sit in the kitchen. A gas generator is better for long outages and heavy loads, because fuel can be refilled indefinitely while a battery cannot. For multi-day outages, many households combine the two, or pair a large power station with solar panels so it recharges every sunny day.

What is the difference between a solar generator and a power station?

Usually nothing but the box it ships in. "Solar generator" is a marketing term for a portable power station sold with, or designed to accept, solar panels. The power station is the battery, inverter and charge controller; the panels are the charging source. Every model in this guide can be charged from solar, and most accept panels from other brands as long as the connector, voltage and current are within the station's published limits. Buying the station and panels separately is often cheaper than a bundle.

How long does a portable power station last?

There are two answers. Per charge, runtime equals roughly capacity in watt-hours times 0.85, divided by the load in watts — so a 1,000 Wh unit runs a 100 W load for about eight and a half hours. Over its lifetime, a modern LFP power station is typically rated for 3,000–4,000 or more charge cycles before capacity drops to 70–80%, which is around ten years of daily use and longer with occasional use. Older NMC units are commonly rated for 500–800 cycles. Storing it at 50–80% charge, away from heat and freezing temperatures, helps it reach those figures.

Can I use a portable power station indoors?

Yes. Unlike a gas generator, a battery power station produces no exhaust and no carbon monoxide, so it is designed to be used indoors. Give it clear space around its cooling vents, keep it out of direct sun and away from water, and do not charge it below freezing. That indoor safety is the main reason power stations have become the default outage backup for apartments, condos and anyone without a safe outdoor spot at least 20 feet from the house for a generator.

Can a power station run my whole house?

Not a whole house in the normal sense, but the largest units can run the circuits that matter. Systems such as the EcoFlow Delta Pro 3 and Anker SOLIX F3800 offer roughly 4 kWh of storage, 4,000–6,000 W of output and 120/240 V, and they expand with add-on batteries. Connected through a transfer switch or the manufacturer's smart panel, they can power a fridge, freezer, furnace blower, lights, internet and often a well pump. Central air conditioning, electric heat, electric water heaters and electric ranges will drain them quickly. For truly whole-house backup without limits, a standby generator is still the tool.

Which is better, EcoFlow, Jackery, Bluetti or Anker?

All four make capable LFP power stations, and at the same capacity the differences are smaller than the marketing suggests. EcoFlow has the broadest ecosystem of expandable batteries, smart panels and solar accessories and very fast charging. Jackery's units tend to be the lightest for their capacity and the simplest to use. Anker SOLIX offers the highest output in its large units and strong USB-C ports on its small ones. Bluetti often competes on price for equivalent specs. Choose by the capacity, output and features you need, then by price and warranty on the day you buy.
