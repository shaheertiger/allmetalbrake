Polyaspartic Floor Coating Cost per Square Foot

Installed polyaspartic runs about $5–$10 a square foot for a full system and $3–$7 when it is the clear coat over an epoxy base. Here is what each figure includes, what pushes a quote up, and how to tell two "one-day floors" apart.

A professionally installed polyaspartic floor coating costs about $5–$10 per square foot for a full polyaspartic or polyurea system, and about $3–$7 per square foot when polyaspartic is used as the clear topcoat over an epoxy and flake base. On a typical two-car garage of around 500 square feet, that works out to roughly $2,500–$5,000 for a full system and $1,500–$3,500 for the epoxy-based version.

Both are sold as "polyaspartic floors," and that is the first thing to sort out when comparing quotes. The price difference between them is real, but it is not the only variable: grinding, crack repair, moisture mitigation, flake coverage, wall cove and warranty terms can move a quote by a thousand dollars or more on the same garage. This guide explains what each figure includes and how to read a quote line by line.

⚡ Two-car garage (about 500 sq ft), installed: Epoxy base, full flake and polyaspartic topcoat $1,500–$3,500 ($3–$7/sq ft). Full polyaspartic or polyurea system $2,500–$5,000 ($5–$10/sq ft). DIY high-solids epoxy with a polyaspartic topcoat $600–$1,400 in materials. Materials alone for a full polyaspartic system run $3–$7/sq ft, which is why DIY saves less here than with epoxy. Add moisture mitigation or old-coating removal at roughly $1–$3/sq ft each if the slab needs them.

What Polyaspartic Actually Is

Polyaspartic is a member of the aliphatic polyurea family. Conventional polyurea forms when an isocyanate reacts with an amine, and the reaction is so fast — seconds — that it has to be sprayed through heated plural-component equipment. Polyaspartic chemistry swaps in a modified amine, a polyaspartic ester, that slows the reaction to minutes. That is just enough time to mix it in a bucket and apply it with a squeegee and roller, which is what made polyurea-type coatings practical for garage floors.

The properties that matter on a floor follow from that chemistry:

  • Fast cure. Foot traffic in a few hours and vehicles in roughly 24–48 hours, depending on product and temperature, against five to seven days for most epoxies. This is what makes a one-day installation possible.
  • UV stability. "Aliphatic" means the molecule does not yellow in sunlight. Epoxy is aromatic and ambers under UV, which is why bare epoxy near an open garage door or under a window gradually turns yellow. Polyaspartic stays clear.
  • Abrasion and hot-tyre resistance. A cured polyaspartic topcoat is harder-wearing than epoxy and much less prone to the softening that lets hot tyres lift a coating.
  • Wide application temperature. Many formulations can be applied in colder weather than epoxy, which extends the installation season in northern climates.

The trade-offs come from the same speed. Polyaspartic has a short pot life, less time to flow out and release air, and less time to soak into the concrete. It also usually goes on thinner than epoxy, at around 4–10 mils per coat against 10–20 mils for a 100% solids epoxy. That is why many systems still use an epoxy or polyurea base for bond and build, and why application skill shows in the finished floor more than it does with slower products.

You will see the terms "polyurea" and "polyaspartic" used interchangeably in marketing. Strictly, polyaspartic is a slowed-down aliphatic polyurea; some installers use a true polyurea basecoat and a polyaspartic clear, and call the whole thing either name. Ask what goes down in each coat. Our polyurea vs epoxy floor coating guide covers that naming overlap and where pure polyurea is used.

Polyaspartic Topcoat Over Epoxy vs Full Polyaspartic

Most quotes for a "polyaspartic garage floor" are one of two builds, and they are priced differently for good reasons.

Epoxy Base With a Polyaspartic Topcoat — $3–$7/sq ft

The floor is ground, cracks are filled, and a pigmented epoxy basecoat goes down — often 100% solids, building a thick film that wets into the concrete well. Vinyl flake is broadcast into the wet epoxy, the excess is swept and scraped off once it cures, and one or two coats of clear polyaspartic finish the floor. The epoxy provides bond and build; the polyaspartic provides the wearing surface, the gloss and the UV resistance on top. It is the most common professional garage system for a reason, and our epoxy flake flooring guide covers the flake side of it.

The catch is time. Epoxy needs hours to cure before the topcoat can go on, so this is usually a two-day job, and the floor still has an epoxy layer underneath that is less tolerant of cold than the polyaspartic above it. In bright sun, the epoxy base can still amber slightly under a clear topcoat over many years, though the flake hides most of it.

Full Polyaspartic or Polyurea System — $5–$10/sq ft

Every coat is polyaspartic or polyurea: a pigmented base, full flake broadcast, then one or two polyaspartic clears. Materials cost two to three times as much per gallon as comparable epoxy, but every layer cures quickly, so the whole floor can go in on a single day. The result is UV stable top to bottom, so it suits garages with large south-facing doors, sunlit porches and outdoor slabs.

Whether the premium is worth it depends on how much you value one day of downtime instead of a week, and how much sun the floor gets. For an interior garage where you can live without parking for five days, the epoxy-based build delivers most of the performance for less. Our epoxy vs polyaspartic garage floor comparison goes through that decision in detail.

💡 The label is not the spec. A quote that says "polyaspartic floor" could be a full polyaspartic system or an epoxy base with a single polyaspartic clear. Both are legitimate, but they are different jobs at different prices. Ask what chemistry each coat is, how many clear coats are included, and the finished system thickness in mils.

What a "One-Day Floor" Includes

"One-day install" is the main marketing promise of polyaspartic, and it is real — but it describes the working day, not the cure. A typical schedule on a sound two-car slab looks like this:

  • Morning: the garage is already empty (that is your job, done the day before). The crew diamond-grinds the floor with a dust-collecting grinder, hand-grinds edges and corners, and vacuums.
  • Late morning: cracks and spalls are filled, usually with a fast-setting polyurea or epoxy filler, and ground flush.
  • Midday: the pigmented basecoat goes down and flake is broadcast into it to refusal, meaning until no wet coating shows through.
  • Afternoon: once the base sets, excess flake is swept up, the surface is scraped to knock down sharp flake edges, and the clear coat is applied.
  • After: foot traffic typically that evening or the next morning; vehicles after about 24–48 hours depending on product and temperature.

What a one-day install usually does not include: removal of an old coating or sealer, moisture mitigation, major slab repair, and moving shelving and storage. Each of those can add time as well as money. Moisture mitigation in particular often means a moisture-tolerant primer that needs its own cure, which can turn a one-day job into two.

One-day installs are also why preparation is worth questioning. Grinding a two-car garage properly takes hours. A crew that claims to grind, coat, flake and topcoat a large floor in a short morning is either very well equipped or not grinding thoroughly, and a thin, rushed grind is the most common reason fast-cure coatings peel. Our guide to garage floor coating peeling covers how that failure shows up.

What Drives the Price

1. Grinding and Surface Preparation

Diamond grinding is normally built into a polyaspartic quote; if itemised, it typically runs $0.50–$1.50 per square foot. It removes the weak surface layer, opens the pores, and gives the coating a mechanical profile to bite into. Polyaspartic sets so quickly that it has less time to soak into the concrete than epoxy does, so profile matters even more. Shot blasting is an alternative on large floors. Acid etching is cheaper but much less reliable for fast-cure systems, and a reputable polyaspartic installer will not rely on it; see acid etching vs grinding for why.

2. Crack and Spall Repair

Minor hairline cracks are usually included. Wide cracks, spalled areas, pitting from road salt and control joints that you want filled rather than left open are often priced per linear foot or as a lump sum. On an older garage in the snow belt this can add a few hundred dollars. Filled cracks may still telegraph through the coating if the slab keeps moving, and warranties rarely cover that.

3. Moisture Testing and Mitigation

Any bonded coating fails over a slab pushing up water vapour. A good installer tests first — a calcium chloride test or an in-slab relative humidity probe on a professional job, or the plastic-sheet test if you want a quick answer yourself. If the slab fails, a moisture-mitigating primer or vapour barrier system adds roughly $1–$3 per square foot. This is not an upsell to resent: it is the line that decides whether the rest of the money survives.

4. Removing an Old Coating

Grinding off a failing epoxy, paint or sealer takes longer and uses more diamonds than grinding bare concrete, and typically adds $1–$3 per square foot. Invisible penetrating sealers are a common surprise; water beading on the slab reveals them.

5. Flake Coverage and Style

Most garage systems use a full broadcast, which hides slab imperfections and adds texture underfoot. A partial or light broadcast uses less flake but looks busier and shows more of the base colour. Custom flake blends, quartz broadcasts or metallic finishes cost more. A solid-colour floor with no flake is cheaper in materials but shows every scratch and dust mark.

6. Cove Base and Stem Walls

Running the coating 4–6 inches up the walls, or onto the concrete stem walls many garages have, gives a cleaner finish and makes the floor easier to hose out. It is detail work, done by hand, and is usually priced by the linear foot. On a two-car garage it can add a few hundred dollars. Some installers include it as standard; many list it as an option, so make sure two quotes either both include it or both exclude it.

DIY Polyaspartic: Possible, but Harder Than It Looks

DIY polyaspartic kits exist, and several manufacturers now sell slower "homeowner-grade" formulations with extended working time. The practical problem is pot life. Most garage polyaspartics stay workable for roughly 10–30 minutes once mixed, less in a hot garage, and the usable time on the floor is shorter still because the material at the wet edge starts to set and leave lap lines. Epoxy gives 20–45 minutes. With polyaspartic you mix small batches, pour immediately, and keep a wet edge with two or three people working at once.

Other reasons it is harder:

  • Humidity and temperature change the clock. Polyaspartic reacts faster in heat and high humidity, and thick coats applied in humid conditions can trap bubbles. Working before dawn in summer is common advice for a reason.
  • Mistakes are permanent fast. A missed spot or a lap line cannot be worked out after a few minutes. With epoxy you have time to back-roll; with polyaspartic you often do not.
  • Grinding is non-negotiable. Fast-cure chemistry does not forgive a poorly prepared slab. Budget $80–$150 a day for grinder rental plus diamonds and a capable vacuum.
  • The saving is smaller. Full-system materials run $3–$7 per square foot, or roughly $1,500–$3,500 for a two-car garage before rental and supplies. Against a professional price of $2,500–$5,000, the labour saving is real but much smaller than with epoxy.
  • Safety. Polyaspartics contain isocyanates, and many contain solvent. Wear an organic-vapour respirator, gloves and eye protection, and ventilate.

The DIY route that makes the most sense for most homeowners is the hybrid: a high-solids epoxy base with flake, then a polyaspartic clear topcoat. The epoxy gives you a long working window where coverage and flake matter most, and the clear coat is thin and transparent, so a short pot life is much easier to manage. That system costs about $600–$1,400 for a two-car garage and closes most of the performance gap to a professional job. Our guide on how to apply garage floor epoxy walks through it.

Warranties and What They Exclude

Polyaspartic installers commonly advertise "lifetime" or 15-year residential warranties. They are worth having, but the headline length matters less than the terms. Read for these points:

  • What failure is covered. Most warranties cover delamination — the coating peeling or lifting from the slab. Many also cover yellowing from UV. Fewer cover hot-tyre marks, and almost none cover wear.
  • Moisture exclusions. Nearly every warranty excludes failure caused by moisture vapour or hydrostatic pressure from below, unless moisture mitigation was purchased. If the installer did not test, ask how they would ever prove a failure was not moisture.
  • Slab movement. New cracks that open after installation, and existing cracks that reappear through the coating, are almost always excluded.
  • Chemical and impact damage. Brake fluid, some solvents, battery acid, dropped tools and dragged equipment are commonly excluded or limited.
  • Labour vs materials. Some warranties cover the material only; a repair that needs regrinding and recoating is mostly labour.
  • Pro-rating and transfer. A "lifetime" warranty may be pro-rated after a few years, may apply only to the original owner, or may be transferable once for a fee.
  • Maintenance conditions. Some require approved cleaners or periodic inspection to stay valid.

A ten-year warranty from a company that has been installing for twenty years is often worth more than a lifetime warranty from one that started last spring. Get the warranty in writing before you sign, and keep the invoice that names the products used.

Mils and coats are the most revealing numbers. A single thin polyaspartic clear over flake can look identical to a two-clear system on install day and wear through years sooner in the tyre lanes. Ask for the finished film thickness and whether a second clear coat is included or optional.

Solids content matters because polyaspartics range from high-solids, low-VOC formulas to ones that are partly solvent. Solvent evaporates, so a lower-solids product applied at the same wet thickness leaves a thinner cured film. Reputable installers will share the product's technical data sheet; it lists solids, VOC, pot life and recommended thickness.

Grind vs etch is the line that most often explains a quote far below the rest. Grinding is labour and equipment; etching is a bucket of acid. For a fast-cure coating, that difference often decides whether the floor bonds for fifteen years or peels in two.

Patios, Porches and Outdoor Slabs

UV stability is polyaspartic's main advantage outdoors. Epoxy yellows and chalks in direct sun within months; an aliphatic polyaspartic holds its colour and gloss. That makes it the coating installers reach for on covered porches, sunrooms, patios and some pool surrounds, and pricing is broadly similar to a garage job, sometimes higher for small or awkward areas.

Three cautions apply:

  • Slip resistance. A smooth, glossy polyaspartic surface is slippery when wet. Outdoors, ask for an anti-slip aggregate such as aluminium oxide or a textured clear coat, especially on steps, slopes and anywhere near a pool. Flake under a smooth clear coat does not provide enough texture on its own.
  • Moisture from below. Patios and porch slabs are often poured with no vapour barrier, and they sit on wet ground. A bonded coating over a slab with heavy vapour drive can blister or lift. Test before committing.
  • Freeze-thaw and drainage. Water that ponds on a coated slab and freezes stresses the coating at cracks and edges. Proper slope and sound concrete matter more outdoors than in a garage.

If the goal is simply to protect an outdoor slab rather than change its look, a penetrating sealer is far cheaper and has nothing to peel; see our concrete sealer guide.

Choosing an Installer, Not a Brand

People search for the "best polyaspartic garage floor coating" as if the product were the deciding factor. For full professional systems it mostly is not. Many polyaspartic lines are sold only to trained installers, the leading products are chemically similar, and the floor's lifespan depends far more on grinding, film thickness, moisture handling and application in the right conditions than on which label is on the drum. A premium product over a poorly ground slab will fail before a mid-range product over a properly ground one.

What to look for instead:

  • Years installing fast-cure systems specifically. Polyaspartic punishes inexperience more than epoxy does.
  • Local references and floors you can see, ideally three or more years old, not just install-day photos.
  • Willingness to name the products used in each coat and share their technical data sheets.
  • A written scope covering preparation method, coats, thickness, extras and cure times.
  • A moisture test before the final price, not after the coating fails.

The Short Version

Budget $2,500–$5,000 for a professionally installed full polyaspartic floor on a two-car garage, or $5–$10 per square foot. You are paying for one-day installation, UV stability and the longest service life in residential garage coatings.

Budget $1,500–$3,500, or $3–$7 per square foot, for an epoxy and flake base with a polyaspartic topcoat. It delivers most of the durability for less, if you can wait a few extra days to park.

If you want to DIY, the sensible route is high-solids epoxy with a polyaspartic clear at $600–$1,400, rather than a full polyaspartic system with a 10–30 minute pot life.

Whichever you choose, test the slab for moisture before any money is spent.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does polyaspartic floor coating cost per square foot?

About $5–$10 per square foot installed for a full polyaspartic or polyurea system, and $3–$7 per square foot for an epoxy base with flake and a polyaspartic topcoat. Materials alone for a full polyaspartic system run roughly $3–$7 per square foot. Moisture mitigation and removal of an old coating each add roughly $1–$3 per square foot, and small garages cost more per square foot because of minimum charges.

How much does a polyaspartic garage floor cost for a 2-car garage?

Roughly $2,500–$5,000 for a professional full polyaspartic system on a 500-square-foot two-car garage, and $1,500–$3,500 for an epoxy base with full flake and a polyaspartic topcoat. A DIY high-solids epoxy with a polyaspartic clear costs $600–$1,400 in materials, plus grinder rental.

Is polyaspartic worth the extra cost over epoxy?

It is worth it if you need the floor back in service in a day or two, if the floor gets direct sunlight, or if you want the longest residential service life. Polyaspartic is UV stable, so it does not yellow the way epoxy does, and it resists abrasion and hot-tyre pickup well. For an interior garage where you can wait five to seven days to park, an epoxy base with a polyaspartic topcoat gets most of the same wearing surface for less money.

Can I apply polyaspartic floor coating myself?

Yes, and DIY kits exist, but it is harder than epoxy. Most polyaspartics have a pot life of roughly 10–30 minutes, shorter in heat, so you need to mix small batches, pour immediately and keep a wet edge with two or three people. Because full-system materials cost $3–$7 per square foot, the saving over professional installation is also smaller than with epoxy. Most homeowners get a better result with a high-solids epoxy base and a polyaspartic clear topcoat, where the short pot life only applies to a thin, transparent coat.

Is a one-day polyaspartic floor really done in one day?

The installation usually is, on a sound slab: grinding, crack repair, basecoat, flake, scraping and clear coat can all happen in one working day because each layer cures in hours. Foot traffic typically follows that evening or the next morning, and vehicles after roughly 24–48 hours depending on product and temperature. Removing an old coating, moisture mitigation and major repairs are usually extra and can add a day.

What do polyaspartic floor warranties usually exclude?

Almost all exclude failure caused by moisture from the slab unless moisture mitigation was included. Most also exclude cracks that open or reappear through the coating, chemical and impact damage, and normal wear. Some cover materials but not labour, are pro-rated, or apply only to the original owner. Read the written terms before signing.

Can polyaspartic be used on a patio or outdoor concrete?

Yes. Its UV stability is the main reason installers use it outdoors, since epoxy yellows in sun. The cautions are slip resistance — a glossy coating is slippery when wet and needs an anti-slip aggregate — moisture rising through slabs poured without a vapour barrier, and freeze-thaw damage where water ponds. Test the slab for moisture first.

What is the best polyaspartic garage floor coating?

For professional systems, the installer matters more than the brand. Many polyaspartic lines are sold only to trained contractors, the leading products are chemically similar, and floor life depends mostly on grinding, film thickness, moisture testing and application conditions. Choose an installer with years of fast-cure experience, local floors you can inspect, a written scope listing each coat and the finished thickness in mils, and a written warranty.
