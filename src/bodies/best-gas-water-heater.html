For most households replacing an existing gas tank, the best gas water heater is a 50-gallon atmospheric-vent model with a first-hour rating in the 80s and a 9- or 12-year warranty, installed in the same spot and connected to the same vent. That covers a family of three to four with ordinary showers. A 40-gallon tank suits one or two people; a household that runs two showers and a dishwasher at once needs a first-hour rating of 90 gallons or more, which usually means a higher-input burner rather than a bigger tank.

Two things matter more than the brand. The first is the first-hour rating (FHR) printed on the yellow EnergyGuide label, which tells you how much hot water the heater delivers in a busy hour — tank size alone does not. The second is venting: whether your house has a working chimney or B-vent decides whether you can use a simple atmospheric unit or need a power-vent or direct-vent model that costs considerably more to buy and install.

⚡ The quick answer: 1–2 people: a 40-gallon tank, FHR around 65–75 gallons. 3–4 people: a 50-gallon tank, FHR 80–90. 5+ people or a large soaking tub: FHR 90+ — a high-input 50-gallon or a 75-gallon. Existing chimney or B-vent that drafts properly: atmospheric. No chimney, or the furnace has gone high-efficiency: power vent or direct vent. A like-for-like atmospheric swap typically runs $1,500–$3,000 installed; a power-vent conversion $2,500–$4,500.

Sizing by First-Hour Rating, Not Gallons

A tank's gallon number tells you how much water it holds. It does not tell you how much hot water you can use in your busiest hour, because a gas burner keeps reheating while you draw. The first-hour rating combines both — usable stored hot water plus what the burner recovers in sixty minutes — and it is the number on the yellow EnergyGuide label that actually predicts whether the last person in the morning gets a cold shower.

Work it out from your peak hour. A shower on a 2.0 gpm head running eight minutes uses roughly 16 gallons of mixed water, of which perhaps 10–12 is hot. A dishwasher takes 4–6 gallons, a clothes washer on warm 7–15, a kitchen sink session 2–4, and filling a standard bathtub 15–25 of hot water. Add up what genuinely happens in your busiest hour, then choose a heater whose FHR is equal or a little higher.

40 vs 50 Gallons

A 40-gallon gas tank typically delivers a first-hour rating of about 65–72 gallons; a 50-gallon with a similar burner, about 80–90. The 50 costs perhaps $100–$250 more, is a few inches taller or wider, and loses slightly more heat standing still. For one or two people the 40 is enough and the saving is real. For three or more, the 50 is the safer default, and a family that is right on the line should generally size up — running out of hot water is the complaint people remember, while the standby penalty of 10 extra gallons is a few dollars a year.

Before buying a larger tank, check it physically fits: measure height to any obstruction, the width of the door or stairs it must pass through, and clearance around the draft hood. A tall 50-gallon commonly stands around five feet, and short models exist for low basements and under-stair closets.

Recovery Rate and BTU

Recovery is how many gallons per hour the burner can heat through a given temperature rise, and it scales directly with burner input. The arithmetic: a 40,000 BTU burner at roughly 76% recovery efficiency puts about 30,400 BTU an hour into the water; heating one gallon by 90°F takes about 750 BTU; so recovery is around 40 gallons an hour at a 90°F rise. A 65,000 BTU high-input burner recovers more than 60 gallons an hour, and a 100,000 BTU condensing burner over 100.

That is why a high-input 50-gallon can out-deliver a standard 75-gallon in a busy hour, and why it is often the better upgrade than a bigger tank — as long as the gas line can supply it. In northern climates with incoming water near 40°F, the temperature rise is larger and every heater delivers less; size up a notch.

Atmospheric, Power Vent or Direct Vent: Venting Decides What Fits

Most buying guides treat venting as a detail. It is the decision that sets everything else, including most of the cost difference between quotes.

Atmospheric (natural draft) heaters rely on hot exhaust rising through a draft hood into a vertical metal vent — a masonry chimney with a liner or a double-wall Type B vent through the roof. They are the simplest, cheapest and quietest gas tanks, need no electricity, and with a standing-pilot or thermopile-powered gas valve keep making hot water through a power cut. If your current heater is atmospheric and the vent drafts properly, replacing like for like is almost always the right answer.

The catch is the orphaned water heater. If the furnace that once shared the chimney has been replaced with a high-efficiency model that vents in PVC, the water heater is now the only appliance in a flue sized for two. A cold, oversized flue drafts poorly and can condense and spill combustion gases. The fix is a correctly sized liner or a switch to power venting; a competent installer will check draft with the heater running before leaving.

Power vent models add a blower that pushes exhaust through PVC, CPVC or ABS pipe, typically out a side wall. They suit houses with no chimney or where the heater must move. They need a 120V outlet, cost several hundred dollars more, are noisier, and stop working in an outage. If backup power matters, put the heater's outlet on your home generator circuit; it draws very little.

Direct vent models are sealed: they take combustion air from outside and exhaust it through a concentric pipe in a short horizontal run through the wall behind the heater. Many need no electricity at all. They suit tight, well-sealed houses, closets and locations where drawing room air is unsafe or impossible, but the vent run is short, so the heater must sit near an outside wall.

Power direct vent combines both: sealed combustion with a blower, allowing long PVC runs for intake and exhaust. Condensing tanks like the Vertex are in this group.

💡 Check the vent before you shop. Look at what leaves the top of your current heater. A metal pipe rising into a chimney or roof means atmospheric — replace like for like. White or grey plastic pipe with a blower on top means power vent. A pipe going straight out the wall behind the tank means direct vent. Matching the existing type is the cheapest route by a wide margin; changing type is where $1,000+ of venting and electrical work appears in a quote.

UEF, Energy Star and Running Cost

Efficiency is rated as Uniform Energy Factor (UEF), a figure between 0 and 1 for gas heaters that accounts for burner efficiency and standby losses over a standard daily draw pattern. Standard 50-gallon atmospheric tanks sit roughly in the high 0.50s to high 0.60s. Power-vent non-condensing tanks reach about 0.65–0.70. Condensing tanks reach into the 0.80s and above. UEF is only comparable between heaters in the same draw-pattern bin (low, medium, high), which is printed beside it on the label.

The money involved is modest at the low end. A household using 50–60 gallons of hot water a day spends very roughly $250–$450 a year on gas for water heating, depending on local gas prices. Going from UEF 0.60 to 0.67 cuts that by around a tenth — perhaps $25–$45 a year. Going from 0.60 to a condensing 0.90 cuts it by about a third, which is meaningful in a big household but takes many years to recover the extra installed cost.

Energy Star certification for gas storage heaters marks the top slice, which in practice means high-efficiency power-vent or condensing models; most standard atmospheric tanks do not qualify. It matters mainly because utility rebates are often tied to it. The federal 25C tax credit that used to apply to qualifying water heaters ended for equipment installed after 2025, so check your gas utility and state programmes rather than relying on older advice.

6, 9 or 12 Years: What the Warranty Actually Buys

Retail gas tanks come in three warranty tiers, typically 6, 9 and 12 years, and the gap in price is larger than the gap in construction. The tank itself — glass-lined steel — is often essentially the same across tiers within a brand. What changes is usually:

  • Anode protection. Longer-warranty models commonly carry a larger anode or a second one, often a combination anode in the hot outlet. Since the anode is what keeps the tank from corroding, more anode genuinely means longer tank life.
  • Parts coverage. The longer tiers usually cover the gas valve, burner and thermocouple or thermopile for the full term, not just the tank. A gas control valve replacement alone can be a $300–$600 repair.
  • Drain valve and sediment features. Brass rather than plastic drain valves and features to reduce sediment build-up tend to appear on upper tiers.
  • Burner input or efficiency. Sometimes slightly higher, sometimes identical.

Read the fine print: many warranties cover parts only, not labour, after the first year, and most require registration and installation by a licensed professional. Leaks caused by sediment or by an expansion problem may be excluded. Given that gas tanks tend to last eight to twelve years, the 9- or 12-year tier is usually worth the premium if you expect to stay in the house; the 6-year is rational for rentals or a short horizon.

Anode Rods and Flushing

Two routine jobs decide whether a gas tank lasts eight years or fifteen, and almost no one does either.

The anode rod is a sacrificial rod of magnesium or aluminium that corrodes so the tank does not. Once it is consumed, the steel starts to go wherever the glass lining has flaws, and a leak follows within a few years. Check it at year three or four (sooner with softened water, which eats anodes faster), then every couple of years. Replace it when it is down to the core wire for six inches or more. A new rod costs $25–$60; you need a 1-1/16 inch socket, a breaker bar, and enough overhead clearance to pull it out — a flexible segmented anode solves low-ceiling basements. If the hot water smells of rotten eggs, an aluminium-zinc anode or a powered anode usually cures it.

Flushing removes the sediment that settles on the bottom of the tank, directly over the burner. Sediment insulates the water from the flame, wastes gas, causes the rumbling and popping noises older tanks make, and overheats the tank bottom. Once a year: turn the gas valve to "vacation" or "pilot", close the cold supply, open a hot tap, attach a hose to the drain valve and run it until it clears, then briefly open the cold supply to stir the bottom and drain again. In very hard-water areas twice a year is reasonable. If your heater sits in a finished or damp basement, a drain pan piped to a floor drain is cheap protection — and it is worth reading up on waterproofing basement walls if water problems in that space are already familiar.

Also test the T&P relief valve once a year by lifting the lever briefly; if it does not reseat or drips afterwards, replace it. And keep the area around the heater clear: a gas burner needs combustion air, and stored solvents or gasoline nearby are the reason FVIR exists. Running a basement dehumidifier in the same space is fine, but do not enclose the heater in a small sealed room without proper combustion air openings.

Electric resistance tanks are cheap to install and have a high UEF, but electricity per unit of heat usually costs two to three times as much as gas, and their recovery is slow — a 4,500-watt element recovers around 20 gallons an hour, half a gas burner. If you already have gas, standard electric is rarely the better buy.

Heat pump water heaters are the most efficient tank option by far, often using a third or less of the energy of resistance electric, and can undercut gas on running cost depending on local rates. They need a 240V circuit (some newer 120V plug-in models exist), a condensate drain, and a space of several hundred cubic feet that stays above roughly 40–50°F; they cool and dehumidify the room they sit in. Switching from gas usually means new electrical work.

Gas tankless heaters never run out, take up a fraction of the space, and last longer, but cost more to install. They usually need a 150,000–199,000 BTU input, which often means upsizing the gas line to 3/4 inch or larger, new stainless or PVC venting, and a 120V outlet. They also need annual descaling in hard water. For a household happy with a tank, a like-for-like tank replacement is far cheaper.

Code Items Your Installer Should Not Skip

These are the items that inspectors check and that cheap quotes omit. Requirements vary locally, so treat this as a checklist of questions, not a substitute for your code.

  • Thermal expansion tank. If your water supply has a pressure-reducing valve, backflow preventer or check valve — common on newer services — the plumbing is a closed system, and heated water has nowhere to expand. A small expansion tank on the cold line ($50–$150 plus labour) prevents pressure spikes that strain the tank and trip the relief valve. Many jurisdictions require one.
  • T&P relief valve and discharge pipe. Every tank has a temperature and pressure relief valve. Its discharge pipe must be full-size, run downhill without a valve or threaded end, and terminate where code allows — commonly within about 6 inches of the floor or to an approved drain. A missing or capped discharge line is a genuine hazard.
  • Seismic straps. Required in California and other seismic zones: typically two straps, in the upper and lower thirds of the tank, anchored to the wall framing. Cheap and worth doing anywhere earthquakes occur.
  • FVIR and elevation. Gas tanks have been built with flammable vapour ignition resistant combustion chambers since the mid-2000s, which reduces the risk from spilled gasoline vapours. Codes still commonly require a heater in a garage to sit 18 inches above the floor unless it is listed as FVIR; check what your jurisdiction expects, and protect it from vehicle impact.
  • Sediment trap (drip leg) on the gas supply, a manual shut-off valve, and a gas line sized for the burner input.
  • Drain pan where a leak would cause damage — attics, upper floors and finished spaces.
  • Vent connector with an upward slope of at least 1/4 inch per foot for atmospheric heaters, correct clearances to combustibles, and a draft check after install.
  • Combustion air openings for a heater in a closet or small room, and a working CO alarm nearby.

The Short Version

Size by first-hour rating: roughly 65–75 for one or two people, 80–90 for three or four, 90+ for larger households. Check your vent type before you shop and replace like for like where you can; changing from atmospheric to power vent is where quotes jump by $1,000 or more.

For most families on an existing chimney or B-vent, a 50-gallon atmospheric with a 9- or 12-year warranty — the Rheem Performance Plus or Performance Platinum — is the right purchase. One or two people can save with a 40-gallon. No usable chimney means power vent. A big household that wants tankless-level capacity in a tank should look at a condensing model like the Vertex 100.

Then budget for the code items, check the anode at year three or four, and flush annually. Those two maintenance jobs do more for the heater's lifespan than the choice between brands.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size gas water heater do I need?

Size by first-hour rating (FHR), the number on the yellow EnergyGuide label, rather than tank gallons. Add up the hot water your household uses in its busiest hour — roughly 10–12 gallons per shower, 4–6 for a dishwasher, 7–15 for a warm laundry load — and buy a heater whose FHR matches or slightly exceeds it. In practice that means FHR 65–75 (usually a 40-gallon) for one or two people, 80–90 (a 50-gallon) for three or four, and 90 or more for five or more people, which typically means a high-input 50-gallon or a 75-gallon tank. Size up a notch in cold climates where incoming water is near 40°F.

Is a 40 or 50 gallon gas water heater better?

A 40-gallon is enough for one or two people and typically delivers a first-hour rating around 65–72 gallons. A 50-gallon with a similar burner reaches about 80–90 and is the safer default for three or more people. The 50 costs roughly $100–$250 more and loses a little more heat standing, but the efficiency penalty is a few dollars a year, while running out of hot water every morning is a daily irritation. If you are on the line, choose the 50 — provided it physically fits the space and through the doorway.

What is the difference between power vent and direct vent water heaters?

A power-vent heater uses room air for combustion and a blower to push exhaust through plastic pipe, usually out a side wall, which allows long vent runs and placement away from a chimney. It needs a 120V outlet and will not heat water in a power cut. A direct-vent heater is sealed: it draws combustion air from outdoors and exhausts through a concentric pipe in a short horizontal run directly behind the tank, often with no electricity required. Direct vent suits tight homes and closets near an outside wall; power vent suits locations farther from a wall. Power direct vent combines both and is common on condensing tanks.

Is a 12-year water heater worth it over a 6-year?

Usually yes if you plan to stay in the house. The tank itself is often similar across warranty tiers; what typically changes is more anode protection, longer coverage on the gas valve and burner parts, and sometimes a better drain valve. Since gas tanks tend to last eight to twelve years and a gas valve replacement alone can cost $300–$600, the extra coverage is reasonable value. For a rental or a home you will sell within a few years, the 6-year tier is the rational choice. Note that most warranties cover parts only, not labour, after the first year.

How much does it cost to replace a gas water heater?

A like-for-like 40- or 50-gallon atmospheric replacement typically costs $1,500–$3,000 installed, including the unit, labour, permit and haul-away. Switching to a power-vent model, which needs a new PVC vent and an outlet, runs about $2,500–$4,500. Condensing high-efficiency tanks run $4,000–$7,000 because they often need gas line upsizing and a condensate drain. Add $100–$450 for code items such as an expansion tank, drain pan and seismic straps, and $500–$1,500 if an orphaned chimney needs a liner.

Does a gas water heater work without electricity?

A standard atmospheric gas water heater with a standing pilot or a thermopile-powered gas valve does — it needs no outside electricity, so you keep hot water during an outage. Power-vent, power direct-vent and condensing models do not, because the burner cannot fire without the blower. Most gas tankless units also need power. If you rely on one of those and outages are common, put its outlet on a generator or transfer-switch circuit; the draw is small, typically a few hundred watts or less.

Do I need an expansion tank for a new water heater?

You need one if your plumbing is a closed system — that is, if there is a pressure-reducing valve, check valve or backflow preventer on the incoming supply, which is common on newer homes and is often added when a water meter is replaced. Without somewhere for heated water to expand, pressure spikes every time the burner runs, straining fittings and the tank and making the relief valve drip. Many jurisdictions require an expansion tank on any new installation; it costs about $50–$150 plus labour and should be sized for your tank volume and water pressure.

How often should I flush a gas water heater and check the anode?

Flush once a year, or twice in very hard-water areas, to remove the sediment that settles over the burner, wastes gas and causes rumbling noises. Check the anode rod at year three or four and every two years after, sooner if you have a water softener, which consumes anodes faster. Replace it when the core wire shows for six inches or more; a new rod costs $25–$60. These two jobs do more to extend tank life than any difference between brands or warranty tiers.
