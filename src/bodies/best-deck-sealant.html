Best Deck Sealant — Clear vs. Stain, and What Actually Lasts

Sealant and stain are used interchangeably and are not the same product. One stops water; only the other stops the UV that turns your deck grey. That difference decides how it looks in two years.

"Deck sealant" and "deck stain" are used interchangeably in conversation and mean genuinely different things on the shelf. A sealant's job is to keep water out. A stain's job is to keep water out and block ultraviolet light. Since UV is what actually turns a deck grey, that distinction decides whether your deck still looks like wood in two years.

This guide covers what a deck sealant does, when a clear one is the right call and when it is not, how the categories compare, and how to apply and maintain whichever you choose.

⚡ The short version: A clear deck sealant protects against water and does nothing about UV, so the deck still greys — choose it only if you want the silvered look or the wood is new and you plan to stain later. For a deck you want to keep looking like wood, a semi-transparent stain is the right product: it is a sealant with pigment added, and the pigment is what provides UV protection.

What a Deck Sealant Actually Does

Exterior wood fails in a sequence. UV breaks down lignin, the natural polymer binding the wood fibres, and the surface greys and loses cohesion. Water then cycles in and out, swelling and shrinking the fibres until the surface checks and splits. Fungi colonise the damaged, damp surface. Rot is the end of that process, not the beginning.

A sealant addresses the water half. It reduces absorption, which limits the swell-shrink cycling that causes checking, cupping and splitting, and keeps the moisture content low enough to discourage fungal growth. On a horizontal deck surface — which holds water rather than shedding it — that is genuinely valuable.

What a clear sealant cannot do is stop the greying, because greying is UV damage and blocking UV requires pigment. This is the single most common disappointment in deck finishing: the deck was sealed, and it went grey anyway, on schedule.

Why Decks Are the Hardest Case

A deck combines every difficulty at once. It is horizontal, so water sits on it rather than running off. It is fully exposed, so it takes maximum UV. It is walked on, so any surface film wears in traffic lanes. And it is usually the most-looked-at wood on a property.

That combination is why film-forming products — solid stains, deck paints, varnishes — fail badly on deck boards specifically while performing acceptably on railings and siding. Films on a horizontal trafficked surface crack and peel, and peeling means scraping rather than washing before you can recoat.

Penetrating vs. Film-Forming — The Decision That Matters Most

More than brand, more than oil versus water, this determines how your deck ages and how much work each renewal costs.

Penetrating finishes soak into the wood and leave no surface film. They wear away gradually and evenly, so a depleted finish looks faded rather than damaged, and renewal is a clean-and-recoat. Most clear sealants and semi-transparent stains are penetrating.

Film-forming finishes build a layer on top. They last longer on surfaces that do not flex or take traffic, and they fail by cracking and peeling, which means renewal involves stripping or sanding. Solid stains, paints and varnishes are film-forming.

For deck boards, penetrating wins on nearly every practical measure. The exception is a deck already coated with a solid stain or paint — once a film is on there, going back to a penetrating product means removing the film entirely, which is a substantial job.

🔑 One-way door: You can put a film-forming finish over a penetrating one after cleaning. Going the other direction requires stripping the film off completely. If you are undecided, start penetrating — it keeps your options open.

Oil-Based vs. Water-Based

Oil-based sealants penetrate deeper, wet the wood more thoroughly, and are more forgiving of imperfect preparation. They also cure slowly, carry higher VOCs, face restrictions in some states, and — being organic — provide a food source for mildew unless the formulation includes a mildewcide. On older, weathered, thirsty wood, oil is still the better penetrator.

Water-based finishes have closed most of the gap. Modern acrylic resins are more UV-stable than linseed oil, so they hold colour longer. They resist mildew because there is no organic food source. They clean up with water, dry fast enough for two coats in a day, and meet VOC rules everywhere. Their weaknesses are less forgiveness of a damp or dirty substrate and grain raise on the first coat.

For a deck in a humid climate, water-based is usually the better answer specifically because of mildew resistance. For a dry climate and old weathered boards, oil-based penetration is worth having.

Choosing for Your Deck

New Pressure-Treated Lumber

Usually too wet to finish. The treatment process saturates the timber and a finish applied before it dries cannot penetrate. Test by sprinkling water: beading means still too wet, absorbing within a minute or two means ready. That can be weeks or months. The treatment itself protects against rot in the meantime, so there is no urgency.

New Cedar or Redwood

Both have natural extractives giving rot resistance and colour, and both grey quickly under UV. A semi-transparent stain in a tone near the natural wood preserves what you paid for. A clear sealant will leave you with a silver deck within two seasons.

Weathered Grey Deck

The grey layer is UV-degraded fibres that have lost their bond to sound wood beneath — finish applied over it adheres to a layer already detaching. A wood cleaner with sodium percarbonate or oxalic acid dissolves it chemically and restores colour. After that, a semi-transparent stain will look dramatically better than a clear sealant.

Deck With Peeling Solid Stain or Paint

The film has to come off before anything penetrating will work. Strip or sand, or accept that you are staying with a film finish and recoat with the same category after scraping loose material.

Composite Decking

Does not need sealing and generally should not be sealed — most manufacturers void warranties for it. Composite boards fade rather than rot, and cleaning is the appropriate maintenance.

Preparation Is Most of the Job

More deck finishes fail from preparation than from product choice.

Clean Chemically, Not With Pressure

Use a dedicated deck cleaner. Sodium percarbonate (oxygen bleach) lifts dirt, mildew and the grey layer without the fibre damage chlorine bleach causes; oxalic acid brightens and removes tannin and rust staining. Two-part systems pair a cleaner with a brightener, and the brightener matters — it neutralises the alkaline cleaner, and residual alkalinity interferes with adhesion.

Wood alternates soft spring growth and hard summer growth. A pressure washer erodes the soft bands faster, leaving a raised, furry surface that traps dirt and looks worse than what you started with. If you use one, keep it under 1,200 psi with a wide fan tip, held well back and moving with the grain — as a rinse for the chemical cleaner, not as the cleaning method.

Repair and Sand Selectively

Replace rotten boards, set proud nails and screws, and sand only where cleaning left raised fibres. Heavy sanding closes the wood's pores and reduces how much finish it will absorb.

Dry Fully

48 hours of dry weather minimum after cleaning, longer in humidity or on dense boards. Test absorbency with sprinkled water before starting.

Applying It

Conditions: 50–90°F, out of direct hot sun, no rain forecast for 24–48 hours. A deck finished at midday in July sets before it penetrates.

Method: Brush, roll, pad or spray — but always back-brush. Spraying alone lays product on the surface; the brush works it into the grain and into the checks and end grain where water actually enters. Work two or three boards at a time along their full length so you never stop mid-board, which is what creates lap marks.

Coats: Follow the label. Many penetrating products specify one coat and mean it — a second coat on wood that cannot absorb it leaves a sticky surface film that stays tacky and collects dirt. Where two are specified they usually go on wet-on-wet.

End grain and gaps: Board ends absorb many times more water than faces and are where rot starts. Flood the ends, the cut ends at the perimeter, and the gaps between boards where a brush or pad can reach.

Railings first, then boards: Work top-down so drips land on unfinished surfaces, and finish the deck surface last, working toward an exit.

Maintenance

Recoating slightly early is far cheaper than recoating late. A finish with some pigment left takes a wash and a fresh coat; one that has weathered back to grey needs the full clean-and-brighten cycle plus 48 hours of drying first.

Check annually with the water test — sprinkle water on the boards. Beading means the finish is working; absorption that darkens the wood means it is time. Check the south-facing edge and the traffic lanes first; they deplete before the rest and tell you the schedule.

Between recoats, sweep debris off rather than letting it sit — leaves and pine needles hold moisture against the boards and stain them with tannin — and keep planters on feet so water is not trapped underneath.

The Short Version

If you want your deck to keep looking like wood, buy a penetrating semi-transparent stain, not a clear sealant. It is a sealant with pigment added, and the pigment is the only thing that stops UV greying. Expect two to four years on deck boards, and renewal by washing and recoating rather than stripping.

Buy a clear sealant only if you want the silvered look and are protecting against water damage in the meantime, or if the boards are new and you intend to stain later once they have weathered enough to accept it.

Keep solid stains and deck paint on railings, risers and vertical surfaces, where their pigment load buys five to seven years — and off the boards, where they peel.

And spend the effort on preparation: chemical cleaner, brightener, 48 hours of drying, absorbency test, then apply in mild conditions with back-brushing and flooded end grain.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between deck sealant and deck stain?

Pigment, and it decides everything. A clear deck sealant repels water, which limits the swell-and-shrink cycling that checks and splits boards, but contains nothing to block ultraviolet light. Since UV is what breaks down lignin and turns wood grey, a sealed deck still greys on schedule — the most common disappointment in deck finishing. A stain is the same water-repellent chemistry with pigment added, and that pigment is the UV block. Semi-transparent stains keep the grain visible while providing real UV protection and last two to four years on deck boards against one to two for a clear sealant. If you want the deck to keep looking like wood rather than silver, you want a stain.

How long does deck sealant last?

One to two years for a clear sealant on deck boards, two to four for a semi-transparent stain, and three to five for semi-solid. Deck boards are the hardest exterior wood surface there is: horizontal so water sits rather than runs off, fully exposed to UV, and walked on, so anything film-forming wears in traffic lanes. That is why solid stains and deck paint — which carry the most pigment and last five to seven years on railings — manage only two to four on boards, failing by peeling long before the pigment is spent. Check annually by sprinkling water: beading means the finish is working, absorption means recoat. The south-facing edge and traffic lanes deplete first.

Can I use a clear sealer and still keep my deck from greying?

No. Greying is UV degradation of lignin, the polymer binding wood fibres, and blocking UV requires pigment — which is precisely what a clear product lacks. Sealants advertising added UV absorbers slow it, but organic UV absorbers are themselves consumed by the light they intercept, which is why those products are a one-to-two year proposition rather than a solution. If keeping the wood colour matters, you need at minimum a semi-transparent stain, which is a sealant with enough pigment to block UV while still showing the grain. The only way to have both a completely clear finish and no greying is to keep the deck out of the sun.

Should I use an oil-based or water-based deck sealant?

Water-based for most decks, particularly in humid climates. Modern acrylic resins are more UV-stable than linseed oil so they hold colour longer, they resist mildew because there is no organic food source for it, they clean up with water, and they dry fast enough to apply two coats in a day. Oil-based products still penetrate deeper and wet the wood more thoroughly, which matters on older, weathered, thirsty boards, and they are more forgiving of imperfect surface preparation. Their drawbacks are slow cure, higher VOCs with restrictions in some states, and the fact that the oil itself feeds mildew unless a mildewcide is included. Dry climate with old boards, use oil; humid climate, use water-based.

Do I need to seal a new pressure-treated deck?

Eventually, but usually not straight away — new pressure-treated lumber is often too wet to accept a finish. The treatment process saturates the timber, and anything applied before it dries cannot penetrate, so it sits on the surface and fails. Test by sprinkling water on a board: if it beads, the wood is still too wet; if it soaks in within a minute or two, it is ready. Depending on the lumber, climate and season that can be anywhere from a few weeks to several months. There is no urgency, because the treatment itself protects against rot and insects — the finish is for UV protection and appearance, and applying it early achieves neither while wasting the product.

How do I prepare a grey weathered deck before sealing?

The grey is not dirt — it is a layer of UV-degraded fibres that has lost its bond to the sound wood underneath, so any finish applied over it adheres to a layer that is already detaching. Use a deck cleaner containing sodium percarbonate to dissolve it chemically, then a brightener containing oxalic acid to neutralise the alkaline cleaner and restore the wood's tone. Skipping the brightener leaves the surface alkaline, which interferes with adhesion. Do not rely on a pressure washer: it erodes the soft spring-growth bands faster than the hard summer growth, leaving a raised furry surface. If you use one, keep it under 1,200 psi with a wide tip as a rinse only. Then allow 48 hours of drying.

Why is my deck sealant sticky and not drying?

Almost always too much product on wood that could not absorb it. Penetrating sealants and stains work by soaking in, and the wood takes only what it can hold — anything beyond that sits on the surface with nothing to penetrate into, staying tacky and collecting dirt. This happens when a second coat is applied to a deck that was already saturated by the first, when the product was applied to wood that was still damp, or when the boards are dense and simply cannot absorb much. Many penetrating products specify a single coat for exactly this reason. The fix is to wipe away excess while it is still wet; once cured, a sticky film needs cleaning off with the manufacturer's recommended solvent.

Can I put deck stain over an old solid stain or paint?

You can apply another film-forming product — solid stain or paint — over a sound existing film after cleaning and scraping any loose material. What you cannot do is apply a penetrating sealant or semi-transparent stain over it, because those work by soaking into wood and the film blocks them entirely; the result sits on top and never cures properly. Going from a film finish back to a penetrating one means stripping or sanding the film off completely, which on a full deck is a substantial job. This is worth knowing before the first coat ever goes on: film-forming is effectively a one-way door, which is a good argument for starting with a penetrating product and keeping your options open.
