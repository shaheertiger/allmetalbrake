The Best Concrete Sealers — One for Every Kind of Slab

There is no single best concrete sealer, because a driveway, a basement floor and a kitchen countertop fail in completely different ways. Pick the chemistry for the job first, and the brand almost picks itself.

The best concrete sealer for most outdoor slabs is a penetrating silane/siloxane such as Foundation Armor SX5000 WB: it soaks into the concrete, leaves no film to peel or wear, does not change how the surface looks or how slippery it is, and gives the strongest protection against the salt and freeze-thaw damage that actually destroys driveways and walks. Everything else on this list is the right answer to a more specific question — you want a wet look, you are coating a garage floor, or you are trying to dry out a basement.

"Concrete sealer" covers at least five chemistries that do different jobs, and most bad results come from a good product in the wrong place: a glossy acrylic on a sloped driveway that turns slick when wet, or an epoxy on a damp basement slab that lifts in sheets. Choose the type by location and finish, then the product.

⚡ Quick answer by job: Driveways, walks, patios and pool surrounds where you want no change in appearance — penetrating silane/siloxane. Wet, glossy look on stamped or decorative concrete — solvent-based acrylic. Low-odour, lightly enhanced finish — water-based acrylic. Dusty interior or garage slab you want harder — lithium silicate densifier. Garage floor that has to shrug off oil and tyres — epoxy/polyurea-type coating. Damp basement floor or wall — deep-penetrating sodium silicate. Countertops need their own food-safe systems.

1
PENETRATING SILANE/SILOXANE — WATER BASED
Foundation Armor SX5000 WB
🏆 Best Overall

The SX5000 WB is the default for any exterior slab you want protected but not visibly changed. It is a water-based silane/siloxane: small silane molecules travel into the capillary pores and react there, lining the pore walls with a water-repellent layer. Water beads off, but vapour can still escape — exactly what a slab that freezes needs.

Because nothing sits on top, nothing wears off, peels, yellows or turns slick, and traction is unchanged. It goes on as a flood coat from a low-pressure pump sprayer, typically two coats wet-on-wet. The trade-off is that it does nothing cosmetic, and its oil-stain resistance is modest — it slows how fast oil soaks in but will not stop a drip left for a week.

2
LITHIUM SILICATE DENSIFIER
Ghostshield Lithi-Tek 4500
Best Densifier for Interior Slabs

Lithi-Tek 4500 is a lithium silicate densifier: it reacts with the free lime in cured concrete to form more calcium silicate hydrate, the binder that holds concrete together. The surface becomes harder, denser and far less prone to dusting.

It suits bare interior concrete: dusty basement floors, garage floors you do not want to coat, workshop slabs. Lithium silicates are generally less prone than sodium or potassium versions to leaving a white surface residue, and because the reaction becomes part of the concrete, the hardening does not wear off. The limitation is that densifying is not water repelling — a densified slab absorbs less but still absorbs. Outdoors in a salted climate, pair it with a silane/siloxane applied afterwards.

✓ Pros

  • Hardens the surface and stops concrete dusting
  • Hardening is effectively permanent
  • No film, no sheen, no slip change

✗ Cons

  • Weaker water and salt repellency than a silane
  • Needs bare, absorbent concrete to react
  • Excess left to puddle can dry to a white haze
Bottom Line: The right first step for a dusty interior or garage slab you want to leave uncoated — not a substitute for a water repellent outdoors.

3
SOLVENT-BASED ACRYLIC — WET LOOK
Foundation Armor AR350 Solvent-Based Acrylic
Best Wet Look

If you want concrete to look permanently damp — deeper colour, gloss, stamped patterns and exposed aggregate that pop — you want a solvent-based acrylic, and AR350 is a widely used one. It is a solvent-based product, and that is the point: the solvent carries the resin a little way into the surface before flashing off, giving better bonding and more colour depth than water-based acrylics.

A fresh coat of solvent acrylic partly re-dissolves the old one and fuses with it, so recoating is usually a clean-and-roll job rather than a strip. Apply two thin coats; one heavy coat traps solvent and goes cloudy. The trade-offs: the film wears and needs recoating every two to three years outdoors, it is slippery when wet without an anti-slip grit, the fumes are strong, and some states restrict higher-VOC solvent sealers — check before you order.

✓ Pros

  • Deep wet-look colour enhancement and gloss

✗ Cons

  • Slick when wet without an anti-slip additive
  • Strong solvent odour
  • Recoat every 2–3 years outdoors
  • VOC rules restrict solvent sealers in some areas
Bottom Line: The pick for decorative concrete where appearance is the goal — budget for grit and a recoat every few years.

Best Low-Odour Acrylic

Cryli-Tek 5500 is the choice when you want a light film and some colour enhancement without solvent fumes — near the house, in a VOC-restricted state, or on a covered porch. It is water-based, low odour and cleans up with water.

Water-based films usually wear faster, needing attention every one to three years, and rain within the first hours or a cold night can leave a hazy, weak film. Penetrating vs Film-Forming Sealers

Every concrete sealer falls on one side of this line, and it is the decision that matters most.

Penetrating sealers — silanes, siloxanes, silicates and siliconates — soak in and work inside the pores. Silanes and siloxanes make the pore walls water-repellent; silicates react with the cement to densify it. The concrete looks and feels the same, stays breathable, and has nothing to peel or wear off. They are the lowest-maintenance option and the best protection against freeze-thaw and salt.

Film-forming sealers — acrylics, epoxies, urethanes, polyaspartics and polyurea hybrids — build a layer on top. That layer gives colour enhancement, gloss and stain resistance. The cost is maintenance and moisture risk: films wear in traffic lanes, can turn slippery, and impermeable ones can trap rising vapour until they blister or whiten.

One compatibility rule saves a lot of grief: a penetrating sealer needs bare concrete. It cannot get through an existing acrylic or epoxy, so switching from a film to a penetrating product means stripping first.

Choosing a Sealer by Location

Driveways

A driveway takes vehicle traffic, de-icing salt, freeze-thaw cycles and, often, a slope. That combination points at a penetrating silane/siloxane almost every time: it protects against the scaling and spalling that ruin slabs, and it does not make a sloped surface slick. Acrylics suit only decorative driveways in mild climates. Our concrete driveway sealer comparison goes deeper on the driveway-specific options, and the step-by-step driveway sealing guide covers cleaning, timing and application.

Patios, walkways and pool decks

Here the choice is mostly about the look. Plain broom-finished concrete suits a silane/siloxane; stamped, coloured and exposed-aggregate patios usually get an acrylic, because colour enhancement is the point of those finishes — our stamped concrete sealer guide covers that choice in detail. Around pools, slip resistance outranks gloss: use a penetrating sealer, or an acrylic with grit in the final coat. For paver patios, see our water-based paver sealer reviews.

Garage floors

The garage is where a thick film earns its keep, because nothing penetrating stops hot-tyre pickup or a pool of transmission fluid. Choose an epoxy, polyurea-hybrid or polyaspartic system for stain resistance and a finished look — our guide to the best garage floor epoxy compares the main systems. If you simply want a harder, less dusty floor that stays bare, a lithium densifier followed by a silane is cheaper and has nothing to peel. A low-cost middle ground is a concrete floor paint, which is breathable but wears faster.

Basements

Basements are about moisture coming from the soil side. A deep-penetrating silicate is the right type; topical waterproofing paints fail because pressure behind them pushes them off. Fix gutters and grading first, repair cracks separately, and only coat the floor after a moisture test passes. Our basement wall sealer guide covers wall-specific products and when sealing is not enough.

Countertops

Countertops face oil, wine, citrus acids and knives, and the finish must be food-safe once cured, so driveway sealers are wrong here. They typically use a penetrating repellent plus a food-safe topical sealer, wax or specialist coating — see our dedicated concrete countertop sealer guide.

💡 The thirty-second water test. Sprinkle a cup of water on the slab. If it darkens within a minute, the concrete is absorbent and either unsealed or due for resealing. If water beads, a penetrating sealer is still working. If it sits as a sheet on a shiny surface, there is a film on it — and a penetrating product will not get through until that film is removed.

Finish and Slip Resistance

Sealers come in roughly three looks, and the look predicts the maintenance. Natural — no change — comes from silanes, siloxanes and silicates. Enhanced or satin — modest darkening and low sheen — is typical of water-based acrylics. Wet look or glossy comes from solvent acrylics, epoxies, urethanes and polyaspartics; the higher the gloss, the slicker it is when wet and the more scuffs show.

Slip resistance is the hidden cost of a film. A glossy acrylic on a wet, sloped or shaded surface can be genuinely hazardous, so use an anti-slip additive — a fine polymer or aluminium oxide grit stirred into the final coat at the label rate — on steps, ramps, pool decks and driveways. Garage coatings get flakes or grit broadcast into the topcoat. Penetrating sealers avoid the problem entirely.

Salt and Freeze-Thaw Protection

Freeze-thaw damage is the main reason exterior concrete needs sealing. Water soaks into surface pores, freezes and expands by roughly 9%; repeated across a winter, that pops off the top layer — scaling. De-icing salt makes it worse by keeping the surface saturated, and chloride de-icers such as calcium and magnesium chloride can also attack the cement paste and corrode rebar.

A penetrating silane/siloxane is the best defence because it keeps water and dissolved salts out while letting the slab dry. Film-formers protect only while intact; once traffic wears through, water gets under the edges. Densifiers help a little but are no substitute for water repellency in a salted climate.

Three habits matter as much as the sealer: seal before salt season, not after damage appears; keep salt off concrete in its first winter; and never use de-icers containing ammonium nitrate or ammonium sulphate, which attack concrete directly. Sand for traction is kinder than any de-icer.

The water test above is the most reliable signal: when water darkens a penetrating-sealed slab rather than beading, it is time. For acrylics, watch for dull traffic lanes and white patches. Reseal with the same chemistry where possible; changing types usually means removing the old film first.

Application Conditions

More sealer jobs fail on weather and timing than on product choice. General rules (the label always overrides):

  • Temperature: most sealers want roughly 50–90°F and no overnight frost. Water-based acrylics are the least tolerant of cold.
  • Dry concrete: wait at least 24 hours after rain or washing, 48 for penetrating sealers. Moisture trapped under an acrylic causes white, milky "blushing".
  • No rain: you need a dry window of at least 12–24 hours after application; water-based products are most vulnerable in the first few hours.
  • Avoid hot sun: a baking surface flashes solvent off too fast, causing bubbles and roller marks. Work early or late in the day.
  • New concrete: film-formers generally need about 28 days of cure; some penetrating sealers are labelled for earlier use.
  • Clean first: remove oil, rust, efflorescence and old failing sealer. A sealer locks in whatever stain is on the surface.
  • Thin coats: two thin coats beat one heavy one for film-formers. Flood penetrating sealers, then brush out puddles.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best type of concrete sealer?

For most exterior concrete, a penetrating silane/siloxane. It makes the pores water-repellent, protecting against freeze-thaw scaling and salt damage without changing appearance or slip resistance, and there is no film to peel. Choose something else only for a wet look (solvent acrylic), a stain-resistant garage floor (epoxy-type coating), a dust-free interior slab (densifier) or a damp basement (deep-penetrating silicate).

Is a penetrating or a topical sealer better for a driveway?

Penetrating, in nearly every case. Driveways face salt, freeze-thaw, tyre wear and often a slope, and a silane/siloxane handles all of them without a film that wears through or turns slippery. Topical acrylics make sense on decorative driveways in mild climates, with an anti-slip additive and a recoat every two to three years.

Does sealing concrete make it slippery?

Penetrating sealers do not; the surface texture is unchanged. Film-forming sealers can, especially glossy acrylics on smooth concrete when wet. Mix an anti-slip grit into the final coat on steps, pool decks, ramps and driveways.

How long does concrete sealer last?

Silane/siloxane sealers typically last four to seven years on a driveway and longer on low-traffic surfaces. Densifier hardening is permanent, though repellency fades. Solvent acrylics need recoating every two to three years outdoors, water-based acrylics every one to three. Garage coatings can last many years if the slab was properly prepared and dry.

How soon can you seal new concrete?

For most film-forming sealers, wait about 28 days for the slab to cure. Some penetrating sealers are labelled for younger concrete, so check the label. Either way, seal before the first winter and avoid de-icing salt on concrete during its first winter.

Can I put a penetrating sealer over an old acrylic sealer?

No. A penetrating sealer must enter bare concrete pores, and an existing acrylic or epoxy film blocks it, so it just sits on top. Strip the old film chemically or mechanically first. Applying an acrylic over a silane-treated slab depends on the acrylic manufacturer's guidance, since water-repellent surfaces can resist bonding.

Should I use water-based or solvent-based concrete sealer?

For acrylics, solvent-based gives a deeper wet look, better bonding and easier recoating; water-based is lower odour and VOC but usually less durable and more sensitive to cold and rain. Local VOC rules may decide for you. For penetrating silane/siloxanes, modern water-based versions work well on typical driveways and patios.
