What Humidity Should a Basement Dehumidifier Be Set At?

The short answer is 45–50%. The useful answer is why — because basement walls and floors are colder than the air, the number on the dial has to sit well below the point where mould actually starts.

Set a basement dehumidifier to 45–50% relative humidity for most of the year, and check that figure with a separate hygrometer rather than trusting the unit's own display. That range sits comfortably below the roughly 60% level at which mould, mildew and dust mites start to thrive, and it does so without making the machine run for hours chasing a number that buys you nothing extra.

The reason the target is lower than 60% rather than right at it is that basement walls and slabs are colder than the air in the room, and cold surfaces carry a much higher humidity at their face than the room reading suggests. A setting of 50% in the air typically means 70% or more at the coolest wall — which is about as high as you want it. The rest of this guide covers when to go lower, when to switch the unit off entirely, and how to tell from the basement itself whether the setting is right.

⚡ The answer: 50% in summer and humid shoulder seasons, 45% in a finished basement or one with very cold walls, and 40–45% or off in a cold, dry winter depending on what a hygrometer shows. Never let a basement sit above 60% for more than a few days, and there is rarely any reason to set a basement below 40%. Verify every setting with a separate hygrometer placed away from the unit — built-in sensors commonly read several points off.

Why 45–50% Is the Target

Relative humidity is the water vapour in the air as a percentage of the most that air could hold at its temperature. Almost every problem that comes with a damp below-grade space — the musty smell, mould on stored boxes, mildew on fabric, rust on tools, dust-mite allergens — tracks humidity rather than any liquid water you can see.

The EPA's guidance for indoor air is 30–50% relative humidity, and below 60% in all cases to limit mould growth. That upper bound is the one people quote, but it describes the room air, and mould does not grow in the room air. It grows on surfaces, and in a basement the surfaces are colder than the air around them. That is the whole reason a basement setting should sit at the low end of the safe range rather than just under the ceiling.

Dust mites give a second reason. They do not drink; they absorb moisture from the surrounding air, and their populations decline steadily when humidity is held below about 50% and recover quickly above it. For anyone with allergies or asthma who uses the basement as a bedroom, playroom or gym, holding 45–50% is one of the more effective control measures available.

The Cold-Wall Problem: Why the Air Has to Be Drier

When air cools, its relative humidity rises even though the amount of water in it has not changed. Basement air touching a foundation wall or a slab cools to that surface's temperature, and the thin layer of air right at the surface — where mould spores land and wait — carries a much higher humidity than the middle of the room.

Mould generally needs sustained surface humidity in the region of 70–80% or higher to establish itself. In summer a basement might sit at 68°F while the lower part of an earth-backed foundation wall is nearer 58°F. Here is what the room reading means at that wall:

This table is the real argument for 50%. A basement that reads a seemingly reasonable 60% has walls and slab corners sitting at around 85% — squarely in mould territory — and at 70% the coldest surfaces are effectively wet. Dropping the room to 50% pulls the walls back to about 71%, the edge of safe. That margin is why people who set their unit to 60% because "the EPA says below 60" still end up with a musty smell.

The same logic explains sweating cold-water pipes. Incoming mains water in summer is often in the mid-50s Fahrenheit, so if the room's dew point climbs past that, the pipes drip. At 50% and 68°F the dew point is about 49°F, and the pipes stay dry. At 60% it is about 54°F, and they often do not.

Anything that sits directly against a cold surface — a carpet pad on the slab, a box pushed against the foundation, a sofa back against an exterior wall — creates a pocket of still, cool air where the humidity is higher still. Keeping stored items a few inches off the floor and away from the walls is worth as much as five points on the dial.

Adjusting the Setting by Season

A basement's humidity load changes dramatically through the year, and the right setting changes with it. The main driver is outdoor air: warm summer air carries a great deal of water, and when it leaks or is drawn into a cool basement, its relative humidity jumps. Cold winter air carries very little, and once it is warmed indoors it is very dry.

Summer

This is when the dehumidifier earns its keep and when 50% is the right number. One common mistake makes the job much harder: opening basement windows to "air it out" on a warm day. Outdoor air at 85°F and 70% humidity has a dew point in the mid-70s. Bring it into a 65°F basement and it cannot hold that water — the walls, the slab and everything stored down there will collect condensation. In humid weather, keep basement windows and doors to the outside closed and let the dehumidifier do the work. Ventilation dries a basement only when the outdoor dew point is lower than the basement's, which in most of the US means cool, dry days in spring and autumn.

Winter

In a cold climate the winter picture reverses. Outdoor air in January can have a dew point well below freezing, so whatever leaks in is extremely dry once warmed, and a heated basement often settles at 30–40% on its own. A dehumidifier set to 50% will simply sit idle, which is fine — but there are two reasons to treat winter differently rather than ignoring it.

First, foundation walls are at their coldest. With room air at 65°F and the bottom of the wall at 50°F, 50% room humidity puts the wall face near 86%, and even 45% puts it near 77%. If you see condensation on the walls or rim joists in winter, the fix is a lower setting — 40% — plus finding the moisture source, which is usually indoor: a dryer venting poorly, laundry drying on a rack, a shower or an upstairs humidifier set too high.

Second, compressor dehumidifiers work poorly in cold rooms. Output drops as the air cools and the coil can frost; most portable units defrost automatically and are rated to operate down to around 41°F, but in a basement in the 50s they do very little per kilowatt-hour.

The practical rule: if a separate hygrometer reads under 45% for a couple of weeks and there is no condensation anywhere, turn the dehumidifier off, empty and dry the bucket, clean the filter, and switch it back on when the hygrometer climbs past 50% in spring. If the basement stays damp through winter, leave it on at 40–45%.

Why the Built-In Humidistat Reads Inaccurately

Almost every dehumidifier displays a room humidity and lets you choose a target, usually in 5% steps from around 35% to 85%, with a continuous setting at the end. It is natural to assume the display tells you what the basement is doing. Often it does not, for reasons that are physical rather than a sign of a faulty machine.

  • The sensor sits in the unit's own airflow. It reads the air entering the intake, usually low down, where the basement is coolest and the humidity is highest. Some units also pick up warmth from their own compressor and coil, which pushes the reading the other way.
  • It measures one spot. A unit in the middle of a large basement says nothing about the far corner behind the furnace, the closed-off storage room or the area under the stairs.
  • Built-in sensors are inexpensive components. A tolerance of around five percentage points is common, and capacitive sensors drift with age and with dust.
  • The steps are coarse. A 5% increment, plus the unit's own control band, means "50%" can mean the compressor cycles somewhere between the high 40s and mid 50s.

None of this matters much as long as you calibrate against something independent. Put a digital hygrometer — the kind sold for a few dollars to a few tens of dollars — at roughly chest height, several feet from the dehumidifier, ideally in the dampest part of the basement. After the unit has cycled for a day or two, compare. If your hygrometer reads 58% while the unit claims 50%, set the unit to 45% or even 40% and check again a day later. The number on the dehumidifier is just a dial position; the hygrometer is the measurement.

💡 Check your hygrometer with the salt test. Put a capful of ordinary table salt with a few drops of water — enough to make a damp slurry, not a puddle — into a zip-top bag with the hygrometer, seal it, and leave it at room temperature for 8–12 hours. A saturated salt solution holds the air at almost exactly 75% relative humidity. Whatever your hygrometer reads, the difference from 75% is its error; add or subtract it from every reading afterwards. Two cheap hygrometers that disagree by eight points is common, and this settles which one to believe.

Continuous vs Auto Mode

Most units offer two basic ways to run: auto (sometimes labelled with the humidity target itself), where the compressor stops once the sensor reads at or below the setting, and continuous (often shown as "CO" or "Cont"), where the compressor runs regardless of what the humidistat says. Some models add an automatic or "smart dry" mode that picks its own target; treat that like auto mode and verify the result with a hygrometer.

When to Use Auto

Almost always. Auto mode is what makes a dehumidifier sensible to leave running for months: it removes water when the basement needs it and stops when it does not. Once you have calibrated the setting against a hygrometer, auto at 45–50% handles the overwhelming majority of basements. Many units cycle the fan after the compressor stops to sample the room air; that uses far less power than the compressor.

When to Use Continuous

Continuous mode has three legitimate uses:

  • Drying out after water got in. After a leak, a backup or a flooded floor, you want maximum removal for days, and the room reading is irrelevant until materials have dried.
  • A built-in sensor you cannot trust. If the unit's sensor reads so far off that even its lowest setting will not hold the room at 50% on your hygrometer, continuous mode lets you run it until the hygrometer is happy and then switch back.
  • External control. Some people put the unit on continuous and control it with a separate humidity controller or smart plug linked to a remote sensor. This only works if the dehumidifier restarts automatically after power is cut and restored — many do, but check the manual, because a unit that waits for a button press after every power cycle will simply stay off.

Continuous mode has costs. The unit draws full power around the clock, it can over-dry the basement in mild weather, and in a cool basement it spends more time frosting and defrosting. It also needs continuous drainage — a hose to a floor drain or a built-in pump — because with a bucket, the unit shuts itself off when the bucket is full regardless of mode. Treat continuous as a temporary setting for a specific job, not a way of life.

What a Too-Low Setting Costs

If 50% is good, 35% is not better — just more expensive.

A typical 50-pint dehumidifier draws roughly 450–700 watts while its compressor runs. At a 500-watt draw and electricity at 15–20 cents per kilowatt-hour, every hour of compressor time costs 7.5–10 cents. Running twelve hours a day costs about $0.90–$1.20 a day — very roughly $27–$36 a month through summer. Running around the clock costs twice that.

Pushing the setting lower raises the bill faster than the number suggests, for two reasons. First, a dehumidifier pulls less water out per kilowatt-hour as the air gets drier, because there is less water in each cubic foot that passes over the coil. Capacity ratings are measured at 60% humidity; at 40% the same unit removes considerably less per hour. Second, the drier you hold the basement, the faster moisture moves into it — through concrete, through gaps around the rim joist and from upstairs — because the difference in vapour pressure that drives it is larger. You are bailing harder against a stronger inflow.

The combined effect is that in many basements, holding 40% instead of 50% in summer adds a large share to running time, and the hour or two a day of extra compressor time is pure cost: below 50%, mould and dust mites are already held back, so the extra dryness buys nothing. If the basement is so wet that the unit runs all day just to hold 50%, the answer is not a lower setting — it is a bigger unit or less water getting in.

Very low settings also dry out wood: below about 35%, flooring, stair treads and furniture shrink and can crack.

Finished vs Unfinished Basements

Finished Basements: 45–50%, Never Above 55%

A finished basement is more vulnerable than an unfinished one, because it is full of materials mould likes to eat — the paper facing on drywall, wood studs, carpet, carpet pad, upholstered furniture — and because many of the cold surfaces are now hidden. The foundation wall behind a framed and drywalled partition is still cold, and the cavity between them can run far damper than the room. Carpet laid directly on a slab is the riskiest finish of all: the underside of the pad sits at slab temperature.

Aim for 45% in summer if the walls are uninsulated or the floor is carpeted on bare concrete, 50% if the walls were properly insulated and the slab has a subfloor. Take hygrometer readings at floor level in the coolest corner and inside any closet built against an exterior wall, not just in the middle of the room. A musty smell in a finished basement where the room reads fine almost always points to a wall cavity or the space under the carpet.

Unfinished Basements: 50%, With Some Tolerance

An unfinished basement of bare concrete, block and open joists is more forgiving: there is less to feed mould, and every surface is visible. 50% is still the right default, and the upper edge of 55% is tolerable for short stretches if nothing organic is stored there. In practice almost every unfinished basement does store something organic — cardboard boxes, wood framing, paper, fabric, a workbench — and the sill plate and rim joist are wood regardless. Wood held at 50% humidity settles to around 9% moisture content; at 80% it climbs to about 16%, and decay fungi begin to get a foothold around 20%. Keeping the air near 50% keeps the framing well clear of that.

Bare block walls deserve a note of their own. Cinder block is porous and can wick ground moisture straight through, so an unfinished block basement often has a higher moisture load than a poured one. A masonry sealer on the interior face will not stop water under pressure, but it can cut vapour coming through the block noticeably, and that shows up as shorter dehumidifier run times.

That last row is the one that most often gets misdiagnosed. If the dehumidifier runs constantly, fills its bucket or pumps water all day, and still never reaches 50% on a hygrometer, turning the dial lower changes nothing. Either the unit is undersized for the space — our guide to what size dehumidifier a basement needs works through capacity by square footage and dampness — or water is entering faster than any portable unit can remove it.

The second possibility is common. Visible seepage at the wall-floor joint, tide marks, white efflorescence on the block or puddles after heavy rain all mean liquid water, and a dehumidifier is the wrong tool for liquid water. Start with the outside: gutters that overflow next to the foundation and downspouts that discharge within a few feet of the wall are the cheapest and most frequent cause, and keeping gutters clear is often the first fix. Our step-by-step on how to fix a damp basement covers diagnosis in order of cost, and waterproofing basement walls covers the work when grading and gutters are not enough.

A Simple Test for Vapour Through the Slab or Wall

Tape a two-foot square of clear plastic sheet tightly to the bare concrete, all four edges sealed, and leave it for 24–48 hours. Moisture on the underside means vapour is coming through the concrete from the ground; that is a source a dehumidifier will be fighting indefinitely, and an interior basement wall sealer or exterior work reduces it. Moisture on the top means the room air is humid enough to condense on the cold concrete — that is the dehumidifier's job, and the setting needs to come down.

Setting It Up, Step by Step

1. Fix liquid water first. Check gutters, downspouts and grading, and look for active seepage. A dehumidifier can control humidity; it cannot keep up with a wall that leaks.

2. Place the unit centrally, with drainage. At least a foot from walls, out of closets, and with a hose to a floor drain or a built-in pump if possible. A unit that stops every time the bucket fills will not hold any setting. If you are still choosing one, our guide to the best dehumidifiers for basements covers capacity ratings, pumps and low-temperature operation.

3. Start at 50% in auto mode. Close the windows, and the door at the top of the stairs.

4. Put a calibrated hygrometer in the dampest spot. Use the salt test first if it is new. Away from the dehumidifier, around chest height, or near the floor in a finished basement.

5. Compare after 48 hours. If the hygrometer reads 50% or a little below, leave it. If it reads above 52%, lower the setting by 5 points and check again in another two days. If it reads below 45% in summer, raise the setting by 5 points.

6. Re-check each season, and clean the filter every few weeks in the heavy season — a clogged filter cuts water removal and looks like a setting problem when it is not. Most basements settle at a dial setting of 45–50%; a few need 40% to hold 50% in the room. The hygrometer is the target, not the dial.

Frequently Asked Questions

What humidity should a basement dehumidifier be set at?

Around 50% for most basements through the humid season, or 45% in a finished basement or one with cold, uninsulated walls. Basement walls and floors are colder than the room air, so their surfaces run much damper than the room reading: at 68°F and 50%, a 58°F wall sits around 71% at its surface, while at 60% it is around 85%, well into mould territory. Confirm the setting with a separate hygrometer, because built-in sensors commonly read several points off.

Is 60% humidity too high for a basement?

Yes, if it is sustained. 60% is the upper limit the EPA gives for indoor air, but it is measured in the middle of the room. In a basement, cool walls, the slab and anything pressed against them sit at a considerably higher humidity — often 80% or more when the room reads 60% — which is enough for mould and mildew to grow on cardboard, wood and drywall. A day or two at 60% after heavy rain is not a crisis, but a basement that regularly reads 60% needs a lower setting, a larger unit or less water getting in.

Should I run my basement dehumidifier in winter?

In a cold climate, often not. Cold outdoor air holds very little water, and a heated basement frequently settles at 30–40% humidity on its own, so a dehumidifier set to 50% will sit idle anyway. Compressor units also work poorly in cool rooms and can frost their coils. If a separate hygrometer reads below 45% for a couple of weeks and there is no condensation on walls or pipes, switch it off, empty and dry the bucket, and clean the filter. If you do see winter condensation on cold walls or rim joists, run it at 40–45% and look for the indoor moisture source, such as a poorly vented dryer or an upstairs humidifier. In mild, humid climates, many basements need the unit year-round.

Should I set my dehumidifier to continuous or auto?

Auto, for normal use. Auto mode runs the compressor until the room reaches your target and then stops, which is what makes a dehumidifier sensible to leave on for months. Continuous mode runs the compressor regardless of the humidity reading and is best kept for specific jobs: drying out after a leak or flood, overriding a built-in sensor that reads badly, or when the unit is controlled by an external humidity controller. Continuous mode needs a drain hose or pump, since the unit stops when a bucket fills, and it uses full power around the clock, so switch back to auto once the job is done.

Why does my dehumidifier say 50% when my hygrometer says 60%?

Because the two sensors are measuring different air with different accuracy. The dehumidifier's sensor reads the air entering its intake, which can be affected by the unit's own warmth and only reflects one spot in the room. Built-in sensors commonly carry a tolerance of about five points and drift as they age. Check your hygrometer with the salt test — damp table salt sealed in a bag holds the air at 75% — and if it proves accurate, trust it. Lower the dehumidifier's setting until the hygrometer reads about 50%, even if the dial ends up at 40%.

Is it better to set a basement dehumidifier to 35% or 40%?

Usually neither, outside of winter. Below 50%, mould, mildew and dust mites are already held back, so going lower buys little protection while raising running costs disproportionately: dehumidifiers remove less water per kilowatt-hour in drier air, and moisture moves into a drier basement faster. A 40% setting makes sense in two cases — winter, when foundation walls are at their coldest and show condensation, or when the unit's own sensor reads low and the dial has to sit at 40% for a hygrometer to read 50%.

Should a basement dehumidifier run all the time?

It should be switched on all the time through the humid season, but the compressor should not run all the time. In auto mode at 45–50%, a correctly sized unit in a basement without leaks cycles on and off, often running a substantial part of the day in mid-summer but resting in drier weather. If the compressor never stops and the hygrometer never reaches the target, the unit is either too small for the space or water is entering faster than it can remove it — a lower setting will not help. Check sizing, and look for seepage and overflowing gutters.
