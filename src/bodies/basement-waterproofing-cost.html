How Much Does Basement Waterproofing Cost? (2026 Prices)

A gallon of sealer and a full exterior excavation are both sold as "waterproofing." One costs $50 and the other $30,000. Here is what each method costs, what it actually fixes, and how to stop paying for the wrong one.

Basement waterproofing costs anywhere from about $50 to more than $30,000, and the range is that wide because the word covers completely different jobs. Most homeowners with a damp or leaking basement end up paying $3,000–$12,000 for a professional interior drainage system, or a few hundred dollars for drainage fixes outside that solve the problem before any contractor is needed. Full exterior excavation with a new membrane is the top of the market at $10,000–$30,000+.

The number that matters most is not the price of any method but whether it matches the cause of the water. Condensation, surface seepage through porous block, a single cracked wall, a high water table and a bowing foundation each have a different fix at a different price, and the expensive mistake is buying a $9,000 system for a problem a $300 downspout extension would have solved — or a $300 coating for a problem that needed the $9,000 system.

Bowing wall repair $3,000–$12,000 per wall, more if the wall has to be rebuilt.

1. Gutters, Downspouts and Grading — $50 to $3,000

The cheapest fix is also the one that solves the most problems, which is why it comes first. A single downspout discharging at the foundation can put hundreds of gallons against one wall in a heavy storm, and soil that slopes toward the house sends everything that falls on the yard the same way.

Downspout extensions run $10–$30 each and should carry water at least six feet from the wall; buried drain lines to daylight or a dry well run $500–$2,000 professionally. Cleaning and repairing gutters is a weekend, and replacing them on a typical house runs roughly $1,000–$3,500. If clogging is why they overflow, gutter guards are often the cheaper answer than more cleaning. Regrading so soil falls about six inches over the first ten feet costs $50–$400 in topsoil DIY or $500–$3,000 with a crew and equipment, depending on how much soil has to move.

None of this is glamorous, and contractors who sell interior systems have little incentive to lead with it. But a basement that only leaks during storms, on one side, near a downspout, is very often a $300 problem.

2. Interior Sealer or Waterproof Paint — $300 to $8,000

Masonry waterproofers, crystalline sealers and cementitious coatings go on the inside face of the wall. Materials run roughly $0.30–$1.00 per square foot of wall, so a basement with 800–1,200 square feet of wall area costs $300–$1,200 to do yourself. Professionals charge roughly $2.50–$6.50 per square foot installed, or $2,000–$8,000, and much of that is surface preparation: removing efflorescence, old paint and loose mortar so the coating can bond or penetrate.

These products are genuinely useful on damp block and porous poured walls with light seepage. They are not designed to hold back water under pressure, and a coating applied over a wall that leaks at the floor joint will simply be bypassed. Our guide to the best basement wall sealers covers which chemistries suit which wall; hollow block walls have their own considerations, covered in our cinder block sealer comparison.

3. Crack Injection — $400 to $1,000 per Crack

On poured concrete walls, most leaks come through a handful of shrinkage or settlement cracks, and injecting them is the most cost-effective professional repair available. Polyurethane foam expands to fill the crack and seals it against water; epoxy injection bonds the two sides back together and is used where structural strength matters. A professional typically charges $400–$800 for an average 8-foot vertical crack, rising toward $1,000 or more for long, wide or hard-to-reach cracks or for epoxy.

DIY injection kits cost $60–$150 per crack and work well on straightforward cracks in bare walls. Injection is not suitable for block walls, where water travels through the cores and comes out somewhere other than where it went in. And a crack that is wider at the top than the bottom, offset, or accompanied by bowing is a structural signal, not a waterproofing one.

4. Interior Drain Tile and Sump — $4,000 to $15,000

This is the most commonly sold professional solution and, for basements with water entering at the base of the walls, often the right one. The crew breaks out a strip of floor slab around the perimeter, lays perforated pipe in a gravel bed beside the footing, routes it to a sump pit, re-pours the concrete, and usually fits a dimpled plastic membrane on the lower wall so seepage runs down behind it into the drain. On block walls, weep holes are drilled into the bottom course so water in the cores drains out.

Pricing runs about $35–$90 per linear foot, with full-perimeter systems on typical homes landing at $4,000–$15,000 including one sump pump. It does not stop water entering the wall; it collects water once it gets in and removes it before it reaches the floor. That is why it works so reliably against hydrostatic pressure, and why it pairs badly with an ignored gutter problem — the pump runs constantly and the walls stay wet.

5. Exterior Excavation and Membrane — $10,000 to $30,000+

The only method that keeps water out of the wall entirely. The soil is dug down to the footing, the wall cleaned and repaired, a rubberised or sheet membrane applied, a drainage board added, and a new footing drain laid and routed to daylight or a sump. It is the textbook fix and the one building codes describe for new construction.

It costs $80–$200 per linear foot on open, accessible sides, and considerably more where the dig runs under decks, patios, driveways, porches or mature landscaping, all of which must be removed and replaced. Whole-house exterior jobs commonly reach $15,000–$30,000, and deep basements in tight urban lots can exceed $40,000. It is justified for repeated serious flooding, structural repairs that require exterior access anyway, finished basements where interior work would mean demolishing everything, or when a single bad wall can be dug alone.

6. Wall Anchors and Bracing for Bowing Walls — $3,000 to $12,000 per Wall

A wall bowing inward from soil pressure needs structural repair before or alongside any waterproofing, and the price depends on how far it has moved. Carbon fibre straps, bonded to the wall at intervals of about four feet, run roughly $400–$1,000 each and suit walls with modest deflection, typically under about two inches. Steel I-beam braces run $500–$1,200 each and can be tightened over time. Wall anchors — plates inside connected by steel rods to anchors buried in the yard — run $600–$1,200 each and can gradually pull a wall back toward plumb.

A typical 30–40-foot wall needs six to ten of any of these, giving $3,000–$12,000 per wall. Walls that have moved too far, or block walls that have sheared along a mortar joint, need excavating and rebuilding at $15,000–$35,000 or more per wall. A structural engineer's assessment, at roughly $400–$1,000, is money well spent before signing for any of it, because the engineer is not selling the repair.

Per Linear Foot and Whole-House Ranges

Contractors price drainage systems by the linear foot of wall, not by floor area, so the useful number is your basement's perimeter. A simple rectangular basement of 1,000 square feet has a perimeter of roughly 130 feet; an L-shaped or jogged footprint has more. Measure it before you call anyone — it lets you check every quote against the per-foot range.

A sump pump is often bundled into a drainage system price, but it is also bought on its own — to replace a failed pump, add a battery backup, or install a pit in a basement that has never had one.

A new pit involves breaking the slab, digging a basin about two feet deep, setting a liner, running a discharge line out through the rim joist, and checking local rules — many municipalities prohibit connecting sump discharge to the sanitary sewer. The pump itself is a modest share of the cost; the labour is the concrete and the plumbing.

The upgrade most worth paying for is backup. Pumps fail most often during the storms that bring power cuts, and a pump that stops for six hours in a heavy rain undoes whatever the system cost. A battery backup or, where municipal water pressure allows, a water-powered backup pump is cheap insurance on any system. What Pushes the Price Up

Access

Exterior work is priced on what is in the way. Concrete driveways, patios, stoops, decks, air conditioning units, utility lines and mature planting along the foundation all have to be removed and replaced, and a narrow side yard may mean hand digging instead of a mini excavator. Inside, crews need a clear 3–4 feet along every wall; furnaces, water heaters, stairs and built-ins against the perimeter slow the work and add cost.

Soil

Expansive clay holds water and swells, increasing pressure on walls and making excavation heavier work. Rocky or bouldered soil slows digging and can require jackhammering. Sandy soil drains well but may need shoring on deep excavations. A high water table is the most expensive condition of all, because it means water arrives from below rather than from the side, and only a drainage and pump system will manage it.

Depth and Wall Type

A full 8-foot basement costs substantially more to excavate than a 4-foot partial or crawl space, and very deep foundations may need shoring for crew safety. Block walls need weep holes and often more drainage capacity than poured walls; stone and rubble foundations in older homes usually need specialist work and command a premium.

Finished Basement Demolition

Interior systems require access to the base of the wall. In a finished basement that means removing at least the bottom two to four feet of drywall, insulation and flooring along the affected walls, and putting it back afterward. Demolition alone adds roughly $1–$3 per square foot of affected area; rebuilding drywall, trim and flooring commonly adds $2,000–$8,000 or more to an otherwise ordinary job. Where finishes are extensive and expensive, exterior waterproofing can become competitive precisely because it leaves the inside untouched.

Mould, Permits and Engineering

Wet basements frequently have mould behind finishes, and remediation of a contained area runs $1,500–$5,000 before any waterproofing begins. Permits for drainage, structural or excavation work typically cost $50–$500. A structural engineer's report, needed for bowing walls and often useful in any dispute, runs $400–$1,000. Regional labour rates matter too: the same job can cost 30–40% more in high-cost metro areas than in rural ones.

💡 Diagnose before you price. Tape a foot-square piece of foil to the wall for 48 hours. Moisture on the outside of the foil is condensation — a basement dehumidifier is the fix, at a few hundred dollars. Moisture behind it is coming through the wall. Water appearing at the floor joint after storms points to drainage. Our guide on how to waterproof basement walls walks through the full diagnosis.

DIY or Professional?

DIY makes sense for the outside and the surface. Downspout extensions, gutter repair, regrading, foil tests, dehumidifiers, wall coatings and injecting a few cracks in a poured wall are all within reach of a competent homeowner, and together they cost under $1,500. They also solve a large share of damp-basement problems, which makes them the right first step regardless of what comes next.

DIY interior drain tile is possible but hard. Saw-cutting and jackhammering a perimeter strip of slab, hauling out a ton or more of broken concrete and soil, setting pipe to the right fall and re-pouring is heavy, dusty work over several weekends. Materials and tool rental run $1,500–$4,000 for a typical basement, saving perhaps 60–70% of a professional price. The risks are poor pipe fall, an undersized pump, and having no warranty if it fails.

Professional is the only sensible option for exterior excavation, which needs equipment, shoring and utility locates; for any structural repair to bowing or displaced walls; and for high-water-table situations where a mistake means repeated flooding. In those cases the cost of a professional is also buying a warranty that, in the better versions, follows the house.

The sensible sequence for most people: do the outside drainage work and the dehumidifier first, wait through a wet season, and only then price an interior or exterior system for whatever problem remains. That sequence alone saves many homeowners the cost of a system they never needed.

Getting Quotes — and the Red Flags

Get at least three quotes, and make at least one of them from a contractor who does more than one type of work — someone who installs both interior and exterior systems is less likely to prescribe whichever one they happen to sell. A good quote should state:

  • The diagnosis. Where the water is coming from and why, in writing.
  • Linear footage and method. How many feet of drain, at what depth, in what pipe and gravel, and whether a wall membrane is included.
  • Sump specification. Pump type and horsepower, pit size, whether a lid and backup are included, and where the discharge goes.
  • Exclusions. Who removes and replaces finishes, flooring, decks or landscaping, and who handles permits.
  • Warranty terms. What is covered, for how long, and whether it transfers.

Divide each quote by its linear footage before comparing them. Two quotes that differ by $5,000 often turn out to be for different lengths of drain, or one includes rebuilding finishes and the other does not.

Red Flags

  • Pressure to sign the same day, including "today only" discounts. Legitimate pricing does not expire in an hour.
  • Mould or structural scare tactics without evidence — no moisture readings, no measurements of wall movement.
  • A full-perimeter system for a one-wall problem, or any system proposed without asking about gutters and grading.
  • Large deposits beyond roughly 10–30%, or full payment up front.
  • Vague scope — "waterproof basement" rather than footage, depth and materials.
  • No licence or insurance documents, and no permit mentioned for structural or excavation work.
  • A "lifetime" warranty from a company a few years old, with terms they will not put in writing before you sign.

Warranties and Transferability

Waterproofing warranties vary more than the work does, and the details decide whether the warranty is worth anything.

What is covered. Most interior system warranties cover water coming through the area treated by the system — the walls and wall-floor joint where drain tile was installed. They usually exclude water through windows, plumbing leaks, floor cracks away from the perimeter, condensation, and failures caused by power cuts. Sump pumps typically carry a separate, shorter manufacturer warranty of a few years, not the lifetime system warranty. Read which of these applies to your specific job.

Lifetime of what. "Lifetime" may mean the life of the structure, the time you own the house, or the life of the company. Some warranties promise only to repair the system at no charge, not to pay for damaged finishes or belongings.

Transferability. A transferable warranty is a real selling point, because buyers' inspectors flag signs of past water and a documented, warrantied system answers the question. Many established contractors allow one transfer, often requiring notice within 30–90 days of closing and sometimes a fee or a re-inspection. Others are non-transferable entirely. Ask for the transfer terms in writing before signing; it is one of the few warranty details that affects resale value directly.

Maintenance conditions. Some warranties require periodic paid service visits or annual sump inspections to remain valid. Factor that into the real cost, and keep the receipts.

Is It Worth It?

Waterproofing rarely adds value in the way a new kitchen does; buyers do not pay a premium for a dry basement because they expect one. Its return is mostly avoided loss, which is larger than it first appears.

Damage avoided. A single flood in a finished basement can mean $5,000–$25,000 or more in drywall, flooring, carpet, furniture and mould remediation, and standard homeowner policies typically exclude groundwater seepage. Two such events cost more than almost any waterproofing system.

Usable space. A reliably dry basement can be finished, and finished below-grade space is often one of the cheaper ways to add living area per square foot. Finishing a basement that has not been waterproofed first is how people end up paying for the finishes twice.

Resale. Most states require sellers to disclose known water problems, and inspectors look for efflorescence, staining and musty air. An unresolved water issue gives buyers grounds to walk away or negotiate a price cut that frequently exceeds the cost of the fix. A completed system with a transferable warranty and paperwork removes that lever.

Where the return is best. Drainage and gutter work has the highest return of anything here, because it is cheap and often decisive. Crack injection is excellent value on poured walls. Interior drain tile pays back when water genuinely enters under pressure. Exterior excavation is the hardest to justify on return alone, unless a finished basement or structural repair tips the balance.

The Short Version

Spend the first $50–$400 outside: extend downspouts, clean or guard the gutters, and regrade any soil that slopes toward the house. Run a dehumidifier if the foil test shows condensation. Many basements need nothing more.

If water still comes through specific cracks in a poured wall, budget $400–$1,000 per crack for injection. If it comes in along the wall-floor joint or the floor stays wet after storms, budget $35–$90 per linear foot — roughly $4,000–$15,000 for most homes — for interior drain tile and a sump with battery backup.

Reserve $10,000–$30,000+ exterior excavation for severe or structural cases, and get an independent engineer's opinion before paying for bowing-wall repair. Whatever you buy, compare quotes by the linear foot, and get the warranty terms, including transfer, in writing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to waterproof a basement?

Most homeowners pay $3,000–$12,000 for professional basement waterproofing, but the full range runs from about $50 to over $30,000 depending on the method. Outside drainage fixes such as downspout extensions and regrading cost $50–$400 DIY or $500–$3,000 professionally. Interior wall coatings run $300–$1,200 DIY or $2,000–$8,000 installed. Crack injection costs $400–$1,000 per crack. An interior drain tile system with a sump pump costs $35–$90 per linear foot, or $4,000–$15,000 for a typical basement. Exterior excavation with a new membrane runs $80–$200 per linear foot, commonly $10,000–$30,000 or more for the whole house.

Is interior or exterior basement waterproofing better?

Exterior waterproofing is the more complete fix because it stops water before it enters the wall, but it costs two to three times as much and involves digging up everything around the foundation. Interior drain tile with a sump pump does not keep the wall dry; it collects water at the base of the wall and pumps it out before it reaches the floor. For most leaking basements that is enough, and it costs far less. Exterior work earns its price for finished basements where interior work means demolition, for serious structural repairs that need outside access anyway, or for a single bad wall that can be excavated on its own.

How much does basement waterproofing cost per linear foot?

Interior drain tile systems typically cost $35–$90 per linear foot installed, usually including one sump pit and pump on a full-perimeter job. Exterior excavation with membrane, drainage board and footing drain runs $80–$200 per linear foot on accessible walls, and more where driveways, decks or patios have to be removed. A simple 1,000-square-foot rectangular basement has a perimeter of around 130 feet, putting interior drainage at roughly $4,500–$11,500 and exterior at roughly $10,500–$26,000. Measuring your own perimeter before collecting quotes lets you compare them on the same basis.

How much does it cost to install a sump pump?

Replacing a pump in an existing pit costs $400–$1,200 professionally, or $150–$500 as a DIY swap. Installing a new pit and pump in a basement without one — cutting the slab, digging and lining the basin, and running a discharge line outside — typically costs $1,500–$3,500. Adding a battery backup pump runs $600–$1,800 installed, and a water-powered backup $600–$1,500 where water pressure allows. Backup is the most worthwhile upgrade, because pumps most often fail or lose power during the storms when they are needed most.

Can I waterproof my basement myself?

Much of it, yes. Extending downspouts, repairing gutters, regrading soil, running a dehumidifier, applying masonry waterproofer and injecting cracks in poured concrete walls are all realistic DIY jobs that together cost under $1,500 and solve a large share of damp-basement problems. Installing interior drain tile yourself is possible but heavy — saw-cutting and breaking out the slab perimeter, hauling debris and re-pouring — and saves 60–70% of a professional price at the cost of several weekends and no warranty. Exterior excavation and any repair of bowing or displaced walls should be left to professionals.

How much does it cost to fix a bowing basement wall?

Typically $3,000–$12,000 per wall for stabilisation. Carbon fibre straps cost roughly $400–$1,000 each and suit walls with modest movement. Steel I-beam braces run $500–$1,200 each, and wall anchors that can gradually pull a wall back toward plumb run $600–$1,200 each. A typical wall needs six to ten of them. Walls that have moved too far or sheared along a mortar joint need excavating and rebuilding, at $15,000–$35,000 or more per wall. A structural engineer's assessment at $400–$1,000 is worth getting first, because the engineer is not the one selling the repair.

Are basement waterproofing warranties transferable?

Often, but not always, and the terms matter. Many established contractors allow one transfer to a new owner, frequently requiring written notice within 30–90 days of closing and sometimes a fee or re-inspection. Others are non-transferable. Also check what the warranty covers: most interior system warranties cover water through the treated walls and wall-floor joint only, exclude windows, plumbing and condensation, and treat the sump pump under a separate, shorter manufacturer warranty. Get the transfer terms in writing before you sign, because a transferable warranty with paperwork is a genuine help at resale.

Does basement waterproofing increase home value?

Not much directly, since buyers expect a dry basement and do not pay a premium for one. The return comes from losses avoided and leverage removed. A single flood in a finished basement can cost $5,000–$25,000 or more, and groundwater seepage is typically excluded from standard homeowner policies. At sale, known water problems generally have to be disclosed, and buyers commonly negotiate a price reduction larger than the repair would have cost. A completed system with a transferable warranty removes that objection and makes it safe to finish the basement as living space.
