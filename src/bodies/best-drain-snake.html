Best Drain Snake — Match the Cable to the Pipe, Not the Price Tag

A hair clog in a bathroom sink, a toilet that will not flush and a backed-up floor drain need three different tools. Cable diameter, cable length and how the head reaches the clog decide whether a snake clears the drain or kinks in the trap — here is how to pick the right one.

The best drain snake is the one sized for the drain you are actually clearing. For most households that means two tools: a hand-crank drum auger with a 1/4-inch cable, 15 to 25 feet long, for sinks, tubs and showers, and a closet (toilet) auger with a protective sleeve for the toilet. Add a cheap plastic barbed strip for the hair clogs that cause most bathroom slow drains, and you can clear the large majority of household blockages without calling anyone.

What you should not do is buy one big machine and try to use it everywhere. A 3/8-inch cable will not turn through a 1-1/4-inch bathroom sink trap, and a 1/4-inch cable will coil uselessly inside a 4-inch main. Toilets need their own tool because a bare steel cable scratches porcelain permanently. Main-line clogs — several fixtures backing up at once — call for a powered drum machine with 3/8- to 1/2-inch cable, which most people should rent rather than buy.

⚡ Quick answer: For bathroom sinks and tubs, start with a plastic barbed strip ($5–$15) — most of those clogs are hair within a few inches of the drain. For anything deeper in a sink, tub, shower or laundry line, use a 1/4-inch, 25-foot drum auger ($25–$70 hand-crank, more for drill-assisted models like the RIDGID PowerSpin+). For toilets, use a closet auger such as the RIDGID K-3, never a regular drain snake. For a main line or floor drain, rent a powered drum machine with 3/8- to 1/2-inch cable, or call a plumber if roots or a broken pipe are possible.

The Four Kinds of Drain Snake

"Drain snake" covers everything from a $5 strip of plastic to a machine that weighs more than you do. They are not interchangeable. Each one is built for a particular pipe diameter and a particular distance from the opening, and using the wrong type is the main reason DIY snaking fails or damages something.

1. Plastic Barbed Strips

Thin, flexible plastic strips, usually around two feet long, with small backward-facing barbs along both edges. You push one down the drain past the stopper, twist, and pull it back with the hair and soap scum it hooks. That is the whole tool.

They work far better than they look, because the typical bathroom sink or tub clog is a mat of hair wrapped around the pop-up stopper linkage or caught just below the drain flange — within a foot of the opening. A barbed strip reaches it without taking anything apart. They do nothing for grease in a kitchen line, a toy in a toilet, or anything beyond the trap, and they are disposable: once the barbs are matted, you throw it away.

2. Hand-Crank Drum Augers

This is what most people mean by a drain snake: a coiled steel cable, usually 1/4 inch in diameter and 15 to 25 feet long, stored in a plastic or steel drum with a handle. You feed cable into the drain, lock it with a thumbscrew or chuck, and crank. The rotating spring head either bores through a soft clog or hooks it so you can pull it out.

A 1/4-inch cable is the right size for 1-1/4- to 2-inch lines — bathroom sinks, kitchen sinks, tubs, showers and laundry standpipes. Twenty-five feet is enough to reach from almost any fixture to the main stack in a typical house. Many drum augers have a hex or round shaft at the back that can be chucked into a cordless drill, which speeds up the spinning considerably; drill-assisted models with an automatic feed, like the RIDGID PowerSpin+, also feed and retract the cable for you.

3. Toilet (Closet) Augers

A short cable, typically 3 to 6 feet, inside a rigid tube with a curved end and a rubber or vinyl sleeve at the bend. The curve puts the cable straight into the toilet's built-in trap, and the sleeve keeps steel off the porcelain. You crank the handle at the top, push the cable through the trap, and pull back.

This is the only snake that belongs in a toilet. A regular drum auger's bare cable leaves grey scratch marks on the glazed bowl that do not come out, and it tends to curl up in the bowl's tight internal passages instead of following them. A closet auger only reaches the toilet's own trap and the start of the drain below it; if the clog is further down, the toilet has to come off the floor.

4. Drill-Powered and Electric Drum Machines

Powered snakes come in two sizes. Handheld cordless sink machines use a 1/4- or 5/16-inch cable and do the same jobs as a hand drum auger, faster and with less effort. Electric drum machines — the floor-standing units plumbers wheel in and rental stores stock — use 3/8- to 1/2-inch cable 50 to 100 feet long and are what you need for floor drains, 3-inch laundry and kitchen branch lines, and 4-inch main and building drains.

Heavier cable carries far more torque and resists kinking under load, which is exactly why it cannot negotiate a small sink trap. These machines can also hurt you: a cable spinning under load that catches can whip or wrap around a hand. Read the safety section before running one.

Matching Cable Size and Length to the Drain

Cable diameter is a trade-off between flexibility and strength. Too thick, and the cable cannot turn the tight bends of a trap. Too thin, and in a large pipe it flops around, loses its torque in loose coils, and can kink or knot itself rather than biting into the clog. Match the cable to the pipe:

Length. Clogs in a sink or tub are almost always within the first 10 feet: in the trap, the trap arm, or where the arm meets the vertical drain. A 25-foot cable covers that with plenty to spare and reaches the main stack in most houses. Laundry lines are worth more reach because lint and detergent build up further down the run. For a main line, measure roughly from the cleanout to where the line leaves the house and add the distance to the street or septic tank — that is why rental machines carry 75 to 100 feet.

Head type. Most small augers come with a spring or bulb head that hooks hair and soft debris. Larger machines take interchangeable cutters: a straight auger to retrieve, a spade or C-cutter to open a path through grease and sludge, and a toothed cutter for roots. If the snake goes all the way through but the drain stays slow, the cable has punched a hole in the clog rather than removing it — a larger cutter, or retrieving rather than boring, finishes the job.

Kitchen sinks with a disposal. Do not try to snake through a garbage disposal. Remove the trap or the trap arm below the sink and feed the cable into the wall pipe directly. Kitchen clogs are usually congealed grease further along the line, and that is where a drill-driven cable or a 5/16-inch cable earns its place.

How to Snake a Drain Properly

Snaking is mostly patience. The cable does the work; forcing it is how you kink cable, crack old pipe, or jam the head in a trap.

Sinks: Go Through the Trap, Not the Drain Opening

A bathroom sink drain has a pop-up stopper and a tight bend just below the bowl, and pushing a cable down from above often just jams it there. Put a bucket under the sink, unscrew the slip nuts on the P-trap and remove it. Clean the trap — it often holds the clog. If not, feed the cable straight into the trap arm that goes into the wall. The cable now has a straight shot at the drain line, and you avoid scratching the sink.

Tubs: Use the Overflow

The drain at the bottom of a tub leads through a tight shoe fitting and its stopper mechanism. Remove the two screws on the overflow plate (the round cover below the faucet), pull out the linkage, and feed the cable down the overflow pipe instead. It drops straight into the drain line below the stopper and reaches the trap without fighting the bend. For a shower, remove the strainer and feed straight down.

Feeding and Cranking

  1. Pull a foot or two of cable out of the drum and push it in until it stops. Keep the drum within a foot or so of the opening so exposed cable cannot whip or loop.
  2. Lock the cable and crank clockwise while pushing gently forward. The head will turn through a bend or start to bite into the clog.
  3. When you feel resistance, keep turning and let the head work. Do not lean on it. If the cable starts to twist up or stall, stop, back off a few inches and try again.
  4. Unlock, pull out more cable, lock and repeat until the resistance gives way or the cable runs freely.
  5. Pull the cable back while continuing to turn clockwise slowly — this keeps the head from unhooking what it caught. Wipe it with a rag as it comes out.
  6. Run hot water for a couple of minutes. If it drains slowly, go again; the cable may have only punched a hole through the clog.

Never turn the cable counterclockwise under load. It can unwind the spring, damage the cable, and on sectional machines unscrew the sections inside the pipe.

Toilets

Pull the auger cable fully back into the tube, set the curved end in the bottom of the bowl with the guard facing the porcelain, then crank while pushing the cable down. Once it is fully extended, crank it back while pulling up. Flush with the tank lid off and your hand ready to close the flapper if the bowl starts to rise. Two or three passes clears most toilet clogs.

💡 Do not force it. A cable that stops dead and will not turn is usually against a fitting, not a clog — a 90-degree elbow, a tee, or an old cast-iron joint. Pushing harder bends the cable back on itself and can kink it permanently. Back off, turn the cable, and let the head find the bend. If it will not go, the access point is wrong: try a different cleanout or remove the trap.

Safety: Gloves, Chemicals and Spinning Cable

Gloves and eye protection. A cable that has been in a drain is coated in sewage and grease, and it flicks droplets as it spins. Wear heavy rubber or leather gloves and safety glasses. With powered drum machines, follow the manufacturer's glove guidance — loose cloth gloves can catch on a rotating cable and pull a hand in, which is why plumbers use the specific mitts sold for the purpose.

Never snake after chemical drain cleaner. Caustic and acid drain openers sitting in a blocked line are still active, and a spinning cable will splash them onto your face and hands. If you or someone else has already poured a chemical in, flush it thoroughly if the drain is draining at all, or tell a plumber exactly what product was used. Repeated chemical use is also hard on older pipes and rubber seals, so mechanical clearing is the better first choice anyway.

Machines need a firm stance. Use the foot switch if the machine has one so you can stop it instantly. Keep exposed cable short, never let the cable twist into a loop, and stop the motor before you put your hand on a cable that is stalled under load — stored torque can whip it free.

Electrical. Floor drains and laundry rooms are wet; plug corded machines into a GFCI-protected outlet. If water is pooled around the drain, clear it first — a wet/dry shop vac is the fastest way to empty a tub or a backed-up floor drain so you can see what you are working on.

Cleanouts can be pressurised. If the main line is backed up, the pipe above the clog may be full of sewage. Loosen a cleanout cap slowly, with a bucket and old towels ready, and stand to the side. Never take a cleanout cap fully off a backed-up line quickly.

When It Is the Main Line — and When to Call a Plumber

A clog in a single fixture is that fixture's drain. A clog that affects several fixtures is lower down — in the main stack, the building drain, or the sewer line to the street or septic tank. The signs are distinctive:

  • Water comes up in the tub or shower when you flush a toilet.
  • The basement floor drain gurgles or backs up when the washing machine empties.
  • Several fixtures on the lowest floor drain slowly at the same time.
  • Sewage appears in the lowest drain in the house.

A main-line clog needs a powered drum machine with 3/8- to 1/2-inch cable, run from the main cleanout, usually a capped fitting near where the line leaves the house in the basement, crawl space or outside. Many homeowners handle this successfully with a rental machine. If water has backed up into the basement, our guide on how to fix a damp basement covers the drying out and what to check afterwards, and a basement dehumidifier gets the moisture down while it dries.

Call a plumber instead when:

  • The line clogs again within weeks of being cleared — this suggests roots, a sagging section (belly) or a broken pipe rather than a simple clog.
  • The cable returns with root fibres, soil, or fragments of clay or pipe.
  • The house has old clay, cast-iron or fibre (Orangeburg) sewer pipe, which a heavy cable can damage.
  • There is no accessible cleanout, or the only access is by pulling a toilet you are not comfortable resetting.
  • Sewage is backing up and you cannot stop water use in the house.

For recurring clogs, ask for a camera inspection. A plumber runs a waterproof camera down the line and shows you exactly what is there — roots at a joint, a collapsed section, a belly that holds water, or just grease — and where it is, measured in feet from the cleanout. Camera inspections typically cost a few hundred dollars and often save far more, because they tell you whether you need a repair or just regular cleaning. Clearing a single fixture professionally typically costs $150–$350; a main line costs more, and hydro-jetting more again.

Rent or Buy?

Buy the small tools. A plastic strip or Drain Weasel, a 1/4-inch drum auger and a closet auger together cost less than one plumber's visit and cover the large majority of household clogs. They are also tools you want on hand at 9 p.m. on a Sunday, not something you need to fetch.

Rent the big machine. A powered drum machine with 3/8- to 1/2-inch cable typically rents for $60–$120 for a half day from big-box and equipment rental stores, which usually includes a selection of cutter heads and gloves. Main-line clogs happen rarely in most houses, a machine you own sits for years, and the cable rusts if it is not cleaned and dried. Ask the rental desk for the cable size suited to your pipe and the cutters for your problem — roots and grease need different heads.

Buy the big machine only if you have repeat clogs you have already diagnosed — a floor drain that silts up every year, a laundry line that needs regular clearing, several rental properties. In those cases a machine like the RIDGID K-400 pays for itself within a few uses. If you are building out a battery platform for other jobs, cordless sink and toilet machines on the same packs add up; our Ryobi tools review and Milwaukee vs. DeWalt guide cover platform choice.

Looking After a Drain Snake

Cable rusts. After every use, wipe the cable as it comes out, run it out onto a drop cloth if it is wet, let it dry fully, and spray it lightly with a water-displacing lubricant before coiling it back in. A rusted cable kinks and snaps. Replace a cable that has a permanent kink — it will not feed straight and can break under load.

The Short Version

For most homes: a Drain Weasel or barbed strip for the bathroom, a 1/4-inch, 25-foot drum auger such as the RIDGID PowerSpin+ for sinks, tubs and laundry, and a closet auger such as the RIDGID K-3 for the toilets. That kit costs well under $150 and clears almost everything above the main line.

For toilets, use only a closet auger — manual for occasional use, a powered one like the Milwaukee M12 TRAPSNAKE if you clear toilets regularly.

For floor drains and main lines, rent a powered drum machine with 3/8- to 1/2-inch cable, run it from the cleanout, and call a plumber with a camera if the clog comes back or the cable returns with roots.

Above all, match the cable to the pipe and let the head do the work. Nearly every snaking failure comes from the wrong size cable or too much force.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best drain snake for home use?

A 1/4-inch drum auger about 25 feet long handles most household sinks, tubs, showers and laundry lines, and the RIDGID PowerSpin+ is a strong choice because it can be cranked by hand or driven by a drill with an automatic feed. Pair it with a closet auger for toilets — a regular snake scratches porcelain — and a cheap plastic barbed strip or Drain Weasel for bathroom hair clogs. Those three cover the large majority of clogs above the main line.

What size drain snake do I need?

Match the cable diameter to the pipe. Use 1/4-inch cable for 1-1/4- to 2-inch lines: bathroom and kitchen sinks, tubs, showers and most laundry standpipes. Use 5/16-inch for stubborn 1-1/2- to 2-inch kitchen and laundry lines, 3/8-inch for 2- to 3-inch floor drains and branch lines, and 3/8- to 1/2-inch or larger for 3- to 4-inch main lines. For length, 25 feet covers nearly any fixture clog; mains need 75 to 100 feet.

Can I use a regular drain snake on a toilet?

You should not. A regular drum auger's bare steel cable scratches the glazed porcelain, leaving grey marks that will not come out, and it tends to coil up inside the toilet's tight internal trap rather than following it. A closet auger has a rigid tube with a curved end and a protective sleeve so the cable enters the trap cleanly without touching the bowl. If the clog is past the toilet's reach, the toilet should come off the floor so the drain can be snaked from the flange.

How do you snake a bathtub drain?

Try a plastic barbed strip down the drain first, since tub clogs are usually hair close to the opening. If that fails, remove the two screws on the overflow plate below the faucet, lift out the stopper linkage and feed a 1/4-inch drum auger down the overflow pipe. That avoids the tight bend at the drain and puts the cable straight into the line above the trap. Crank clockwise, let the head work, withdraw slowly while turning, then flush with hot water.

Is it safe to snake a drain after using drain cleaner?

No. Caustic and acid drain cleaners stay active in standing water, and a spinning cable splashes them onto your hands and face. If chemical cleaner is already in a blocked drain, let it flush away if the drain is draining at all, or tell a plumber exactly which product was used before they work on it. Mechanical clearing with a snake is the better first step for a clog anyway, and wear gloves and eye protection whenever you snake a drain.

Should I rent or buy a drain snake for a main line?

Rent, in most cases. A powered drum machine with 3/8- to 1/2-inch cable typically rents for $60–$120 for a half day, often with cutters and gloves included, while buying one costs several hundred dollars to well over a thousand. Main-line clogs are infrequent in most houses, and stored cable rusts. Buying makes sense only if you have a diagnosed recurring problem, such as a floor drain that clogs every year, or several properties to maintain.

How do I know if the clog is in the main line?

A main-line clog affects several fixtures at once. Typical signs are water coming up in the tub or shower when a toilet is flushed, the basement floor drain gurgling or backing up when the washer empties, several fixtures on the lowest floor draining slowly together, or sewage in the lowest drain. A single slow fixture is almost always a clog in that fixture's own trap or branch line, which a household drum auger can reach.

When should I call a plumber instead of snaking?

Call when a line clogs again within weeks of clearing, when the cable comes back with roots, soil or pipe fragments, when the house has old clay, cast-iron or fibre sewer pipe, when there is no cleanout you can reach, or when sewage is backing up and you cannot stop water use. Ask for a camera inspection on recurring clogs: it shows whether you have roots, a broken or sagging pipe or simply grease, which decides whether you need a repair or regular cleaning.
