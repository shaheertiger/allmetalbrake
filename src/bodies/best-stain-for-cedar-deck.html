Best Stain for a Cedar Deck — Penetrating Oils That Work With the Wood

Cedar is soft, full of water-soluble tannins and turns silver within a season. The stains that suit it soak in, carry enough pigment to stop UV, and wear away instead of peeling.

The best stain for a cedar deck is a penetrating, oil-based semi-transparent stain in a cedar or redwood tone, applied thin over wood that has been cleaned and brightened. Cedar is soft, porous and loaded with water-soluble extractives, so it rewards a finish that soaks into the fibres and punishes one that sits on top as a film. Semi-transparent pigment is the minimum that meaningfully slows UV greying; anything clearer looks beautiful for one summer and then goes silver.

Of the widely sold options, Armstrong Clark, Penofin Red Label, Ready Seal and TWP are the oil-based lines that suit cedar best, with Defy Extreme the strongest water-based choice. Which one fits depends on whether the cedar is a deck, a fence or siding, how much sun it takes, and how often you are willing to recoat — which is what the rest of this guide sorts out.

⚡ The direct answer: For a cedar deck, use an oil-based penetrating semi-transparent stain — Armstrong Clark Wood Stain is the best all-rounder, Penofin Red Label the deepest-penetrating oil, Ready Seal Natural Cedar the easiest for fences. Clean, then brighten with oxalic acid to restore the tannin colour, sand smooth new boards with 60–80 grit, and apply thin coats that fully absorb. Expect to recoat a deck every 2–3 years and a fence every 3–5. Avoid solid stains and film-forming finishes on walking surfaces: on soft cedar they wear through and peel.

Why Cedar Is Different

Most deck-stain advice is written for pressure-treated pine, which is dense with resin, wet from treatment and fairly forgiving. Western red cedar behaves differently in four ways that matter for finishing.

It Is Rich in Tannins and Extractives

Cedar's colour and much of its rot resistance come from extractives — tannins and naturally occurring compounds such as thujaplicins that make the heartwood unappealing to decay fungi. Many of these extractives are water-soluble. When water moves through the board, or when a water-based coating wets the surface, they migrate outward and leave brown or reddish streaks known as extractive bleed. The same tannins react with iron, so carbon-steel nails, steel-wool residue or even a steel brush leave blue-black stains. Stainless steel fasteners are the standard on cedar for that reason.

It Is Soft

Western red cedar is one of the lightest commercial softwoods, roughly two-thirds the density of southern yellow pine. That is why it is stable, light to work and slow to check, and also why it dents under furniture legs and abrades under foot traffic. A film finish on a soft substrate gets crushed and scuffed until it breaks, then water gets under it. A penetrating finish has no film to break.

Only the Heartwood Is Durable

The naturally rot-resistant part is the heartwood — the reddish-brown core. Pale sapwood, common at the edges of lower-grade fence pickets and some deck boards, has little natural durability. Stain protects both, but the sapwood is where the finish matters most, and its lighter colour is why mixed-grade cedar can look patchy under a transparent product.

New Cedar Can Resist Absorption — and Old Cedar Turns Grey Fast

Smooth-planed new cedar often has a compressed, slightly burnished surface from the planer knives, commonly called mill glaze. Whether it is true glaze or simply tight surface fibres, the practical effect is the same: the board absorbs stain poorly and the finish sits on top. At the other extreme, unfinished cedar exposed to sun begins to change colour within weeks, as ultraviolet light breaks down lignin in the surface fibres. Rain washes the degraded material away and mildew colonises what remains, so within six to twelve months bare cedar is silver-grey. The grey layer is shallow and can be cleaned off, but stain applied over it will not bond well.

Oil, Water-Based or Film-Forming?

Oil-Based Penetrating Stains

These carry pigment into the wood on oils and resins that cure inside the surface fibres rather than on top of them. On cedar they have three advantages. They do not mobilise tannins the way water does. They penetrate the soft, open earlywood readily. And when they fail, they fade and thin rather than peel, so maintenance is a clean and a fresh coat. Their downsides are slower drying, solvent cleanup, stronger odour, and restrictions on sale in some low-VOC jurisdictions.

Water-Based Stains

Modern water-based stains have improved considerably and some, like Defy, are designed to penetrate. They dry in hours, clean up with water and are low in VOCs. On cedar, though, the water carrier can draw extractives to the surface, and many water-based products contain acrylic resins that build a thin film if applied heavily. A thin film on soft cedar under foot traffic is exactly the condition that leads to peeling. Use them thin, over well-brightened wood.

Film-Forming Finishes and Solid Stains

Solid stains, deck paints and varnish-type coatings sit on the surface. They hide the wood, block UV almost entirely and can last a long time on vertical surfaces. On a cedar deck they are a poor choice: the soft wood moves and dents beneath the film, traffic wears it through in paths, water gets in behind and the coating lifts. Once a cedar deck has been coated with a film, going back to a penetrating stain means stripping it completely. If you need maximum water repellency without colour, a clear deck sealant is a different product with a different job, and on cedar it should still be a penetrating type.

💡 Water-based solid stain on cedar siding? It can work, but cedar's tannins will bleed through a water-based topcoat as brown blotches unless you prime first with a stain-blocking primer formulated for tannin-rich woods. Oil or alkyd primers are the traditional answer. This is a siding issue; on decks, avoid solids altogether.

Transparency vs UV Life

Pigment is what protects wood from ultraviolet light. Oils and resins keep water out; pigment physically blocks the UV that breaks down lignin and turns cedar grey. The more pigment a product carries, the longer it protects — and the more of the grain it hides. That is the central trade-off on cedar, which people usually stain precisely because they like the grain.

Preparing Cedar Properly

Preparation decides more about how a cedar finish looks and lasts than the brand on the can. The sequence is the same whether the wood is new, grey or previously stained.

1. Clean

Use a sodium percarbonate (oxygen bleach) deck cleaner to lift grey fibres, dirt and mildew. If there is old stain to remove, a sodium hydroxide-based stripper does that job instead. Avoid chlorine bleach: it whitens the surface by attacking the wood itself, leaving cedar looking washed out and fuzzy, and it does little to remove grey fibres. Scrub with a stiff synthetic brush, never a steel one.

2. Brighten with Oxalic Acid

This is the step people skip and the one that matters most on cedar. Percarbonate cleaners and strippers are alkaline, and alkalinity darkens cedar's tannins, often leaving the wood a muddy brown after cleaning. A wood brightener based on oxalic acid neutralises that alkalinity, restores the reddish-tan colour of the tannins, and removes the black iron-tannin stains from fasteners. The difference is often dramatic — the wood comes back close to its fresh-cut colour — and it gives the stain a lighter, more even base so the colour you choose is the colour you get.

3. Rinse Gently

Cedar is soft enough that a pressure washer at full power will furrow the earlywood and raise a fuzzy surface. If you use one, keep it to a few hundred psi — roughly 500–800 — with a wide fan tip held well back, moving with the grain. A garden hose and a scrub brush are slower and entirely adequate.

4. Sand Where Needed

After drying, any raised fibres or fuzz should be knocked down with 60–80 grit. The same coarse sanding opens up smooth, planed new boards. Do not go finer than about 100 grit on exterior cedar: a polished surface absorbs less stain, which is the opposite of what you want.

5. Let It Dry — and Test New Wood

Give cleaned cedar at least 48 hours of dry weather before staining. For brand-new cedar, the advice used to be to let it weather for several months; the more precise approach is a water test. Sprinkle water on the boards. If it soaks in within a minute or so, the wood is ready. If it beads, the surface is still too tight — clean, brighten and sand it, or wait a few weeks and test again. Leaving new cedar bare for a whole season is counterproductive: by then the surface has greyed and needs cleaning anyway.

💡 Mixed decks: Plenty of cedar decks sit on a pressure-treated frame, or have redwood or PT stair stringers. Treated pine dries more slowly and often needs longer before it accepts stain. If the same deck has both, the water test on each species decides when each part is ready. Our redwood sealer guide covers redwood, which shares cedar's tannin chemistry and prep.

Applying It Without Problems

Most cedar stain failures are application failures: too much product, applied in the wrong conditions.

Conditions. Stain between roughly 50°F and 90°F, out of direct midday sun, with no rain due for 24–48 hours. In hot sun the surface flashes off before the oil can penetrate, leaving lap marks and an uneven finish. Morning or late-afternoon shade is ideal.

Thin is the rule. Cedar takes what it can absorb. Anything left on the surface after 20–30 minutes is not protecting the wood; it is a sticky layer that collects dirt, encourages mildew and, with water-based products, can peel. Wipe or brush out puddles and excess before it sets.

Back-brushing. Spraying or rolling is fast, but the stain should be worked into the wood with a brush or pad afterwards — back-brushing. It pushes product into checks, end grain and the gaps between boards, and evens out colour. On rough-sawn fence pickets it is the difference between a uniform result and a blotchy one.

Wet-on-wet coats. Several oil stains suited to cedar, including Armstrong Clark and TWP, specify two thin coats wet-on-wet: the second goes on while the first is still wet, typically within 20–45 minutes, so both soak in together. Waiting a day and applying a second coat over a cured first coat, by contrast, often leaves the second sitting on top. Follow the label; one-coat products such as Ready Seal are designed differently.

Work board by board. Finish the full length of two or three boards at a time, ending at a natural break, so you never overlap wet stain onto dry. Do the rails and vertical surfaces before the deck floor, and coat the end grain generously, since it drinks the most.

Decks vs Fences vs Siding

Decks are the hardest environment a cedar finish faces. The boards are horizontal, so they take UV head-on, hold water after rain, and take foot traffic, grit and furniture. Only penetrating stains belong here, semi-transparent or semi-solid for anything in full sun. Expect the traffic paths and stair treads to wear first; touching up those areas between full recoats is normal.

Fences are vertical, so rain runs off and the sun strikes at an angle. Finishes last noticeably longer than on decks. Most cedar fencing is rough-sawn, which absorbs much more stain per square foot and holds it longer than smooth boards. Ready Seal, sprayed and back-brushed, is the efficient answer for long runs. Remember that fences have two sides; if you only stain the street side, the bare side still moves moisture and greys.

Siding and trim have no foot traffic, so the penetrating-only rule relaxes. Translucent finishes like Cetol SRD, semi-solids, and solid stains over a proper tannin-blocking primer are all legitimate. Siding under eaves can go many years between coats; the south and west walls will always need attention first. For general exterior woodwork beyond cedar, see our exterior wood sealer guide.

Colours That Suit Cedar

Cedar and natural-cedar tones are the safe choice for a reason: they are designed to match and enrich the wood's own colour, they even out the difference between dark heartwood and pale sapwood, and wear shows less because the wood underneath is a similar shade.

Redwood tones push cedar redder and suit western red cedar's natural reddish heartwood. On cedar with a lot of pale sapwood they can look uneven at first.

Warm honey and amber tones, such as Cabot's, make cedar look golden. They can turn orange on pale boards, so test on your actual wood.

Greys and driftwood toners are a good choice if you like weathered cedar but want it even and protected. A grey semi-transparent gives the silver look without the blotchy mildew and fuzzy surface of genuinely weathered wood.

Very dark browns hide variation and last well thanks to heavy pigment, but on a sunny deck they get hot underfoot and wear paths show as pale streaks. On cedar, they also hide what most people bought the wood for.

Always test on a spare board or an inconspicuous area, prepared the same way as the deck, and judge the colour once it is dry — oil stains look darker wet and some, like Ready Seal, keep shifting for a couple of weeks.

Maintenance Cycles

The great advantage of a penetrating finish is that maintenance is simple. When water stops beading and the colour has faded, usually first on stair treads and sunny boards, clean with a percarbonate cleaner, brighten, let it dry, and apply one light coat of the same product. No stripping is needed. For a semi-transparent oil on a sunny cedar deck, that cycle typically falls every two to three years; for fences, every three to five.

Do not recoat on a schedule; recoat on condition. Over-maintaining with a penetrating oil can build up enough product that the surface stays tacky and darkens. A light annual wash with a mild deck cleaner, without brightener or stain, extends the time between coats by removing the dirt and mildew that accelerate breakdown.

Switching products is where maintenance gets expensive. Most penetrating stains will not soak into wood still holding a different product, so changing brands, or going from a solid back to a semi-transparent, usually means a full strip first. Choose carefully the first time and stick with it.

The Short Version

Use an oil-based penetrating semi-transparent stain on a cedar deck. Armstrong Clark is the best all-rounder; Penofin Red Label suits fine cedar you want to show off; Ready Seal is the easiest for fences; TWP for damp, shaded sites; Defy if you need water-based.

Prepare properly: clean, brighten with oxalic acid, rinse gently, sand smooth boards with 60–80 grit, and water-test new wood before staining. Apply thin, back-brush, and follow the label's coat method.

Avoid clear finishes if you want to keep cedar's colour, and avoid solid stains and film finishes on any surface you walk on. Recoat when the wood tells you to, with the same product, after a clean — typically every two to three years on a deck.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best stain for a cedar deck?

An oil-based penetrating semi-transparent stain in a cedar or natural tone. Cedar is soft and rich in water-soluble tannins, so it suits a finish that soaks into the fibres rather than one that forms a film on top. Armstrong Clark Wood Stain is the most balanced choice, Penofin Red Label offers the deepest penetration, Ready Seal Natural Cedar is the easiest to apply over large areas, TWP 1500 Series suits damp and shaded sites, and Defy Extreme is the strongest water-based option. Semi-transparent pigment is the minimum that meaningfully slows greying; clear finishes let cedar go silver within a season on a sunny deck.

Is oil-based or water-based stain better for cedar?

Oil-based is generally better for cedar. Oils penetrate the soft wood readily, do not draw tannins to the surface the way a water carrier can, and fade gradually rather than peeling, which makes recoating a simple clean and a fresh coat. Water-based stains dry faster, clean up with water and are low in VOCs, and a penetrating water-based product like Defy Extreme can work well — but it must be applied thin over thoroughly cleaned and brightened wood, because heavy coats form a film that tends to peel on soft cedar under foot traffic.

Should I let new cedar weather before staining?

Not for a whole season. Smooth new cedar can resist absorption because of a tight, planed surface often called mill glaze, but leaving it bare for months just lets it grey, which then has to be cleaned off. Test instead: sprinkle water on the boards. If it soaks in within a minute or so, stain. If it beads, clean the wood with a deck cleaner, brighten with oxalic acid and sand with 60–80 grit, or wait a few weeks and test again. Rough-sawn cedar usually accepts stain immediately.

Why does my cedar look dark or blotchy after cleaning?

Most deck cleaners and strippers are alkaline, and alkalinity darkens cedar's tannins, often leaving the wood muddy brown. Black streaks or spots near nails are iron-tannin stains from steel fasteners or tools. Both are fixed with a wood brightener based on oxalic acid, applied after cleaning and before rinsing off. It neutralises the cleaner, restores the reddish-tan colour and removes iron stains. Skipping this step is the most common reason a cedar deck stains darker and less evenly than expected.

Can I use solid stain on a cedar deck?

It is not recommended. A solid stain behaves like paint, forming a film on the surface. Cedar is soft, so foot traffic and furniture dent and abrade the wood beneath the film until it cracks; water then gets behind it and it peels, usually in traffic paths first. Going back to a penetrating stain afterwards requires a complete strip. Solid stain is a reasonable choice on cedar siding and fences, ideally over a tannin-blocking primer, but on a walking surface a semi-transparent or semi-solid penetrating oil is far easier to live with.

How often should a cedar deck be restained?

Typically every two to three years for a semi-transparent oil stain on a sunny deck, and every three to five years for a cedar fence or siding. Clear and toner finishes need attention every one to two years. Rather than following a calendar, recoat when water stops beading and the colour has visibly faded on the sunniest boards and stair treads. Clean and brighten, let the wood dry for 48 hours, and apply one light coat of the same product — no stripping is needed with a penetrating stain.

Can I pressure wash a cedar deck before staining?

Carefully. Cedar is soft, and a pressure washer at full power gouges the softer earlywood and raises a fuzzy surface that absorbs stain unevenly. If you use one, keep the pressure low — roughly 500–800 psi — with a wide fan tip held well back from the surface, moving along the grain. Let the cleaner do the work. A garden hose and a stiff synthetic brush are entirely adequate. If fibres are raised anyway, sand with 60–80 grit once dry.

What colour stain looks best on cedar?

Cedar-tone and natural-cedar semi-transparents are the most reliable because they match the wood's own colour, even out pale sapwood and dark heartwood, and show wear less. Redwood tones suit western red cedar's reddish heartwood. Honey and amber oils give a golden look but can turn orange on pale boards. Grey toners give an even weathered look with protection. Very dark browns last well but get hot underfoot and hide the grain. Test on a prepared spare board and judge it dry.
