How to Caulk Exterior Windows — Where, What and How

A good bead around a window keeps water out of the wall. A bead in the wrong place keeps water in. Here is which joints to seal, which to leave open, and how to lay a bead that lasts a decade.

To caulk an exterior window properly, cut out all the old caulk, clean and fully dry the joint, push foam backer rod into any gap wider than about 1/4 inch, then run a continuous bead of exterior-rated sealant along the head and both sides of the trim, tool it smooth within a few minutes, and keep it dry until it skins over. What you do not seal matters as much: weep holes, the weep slots in the bottom of vinyl and aluminium frames, and the bottom flange of a nail-fin window are drainage paths, and closing them traps water inside the wall.

The work itself is an afternoon for a handful of windows and costs very little — a tube of good sealant, a roll of backer rod and a few hand tools. Where people go wrong is not technique so much as placement and timing: sealing the wrong joint, caulking over damp wood, or painting a bead that has not cured. This guide walks through each decision in order.

⚡ The short version: Seal the top and sides of the exterior trim where it meets the siding, and the joint between trim and window frame. Never seal weep holes, frame weep slots, the bottom nailing flange, or the gap under a metal drip cap. Use a paintable siliconised acrylic or hybrid sealant on painted trim, backer rod in gaps over 1/4 inch, and caulk between roughly 40°F and 90°F with at least 24 hours of dry weather ahead.

Where to Caulk — and Where Not To

A window is not meant to be a sealed box. Modern window installation relies on two lines of defence: the exterior caulk and trim shed the bulk of the rain, and behind them, flashing tape and the house wrap catch whatever gets past and drain it down and out. Caulk is the first line, not the only one. The rule that follows from this is simple — seal the joints that face up and sideways into the weather, and leave open the joints at the bottom where water is supposed to leave.

Seal These Joints

  • Trim to siding, head and both sides. The gap where the outer edge of the brick mould or flat casing meets the siding, brick or stucco. This is the main job and the joint most likely to have failed.
  • Trim to window frame. The inside edge of the casing where it meets the vinyl, aluminium, fibreglass or wood frame — again head and sides.
  • Mitre and butt joints in the trim itself. Open corner joints in wood casing let water straight into end grain, which is where rot begins.
  • Where a sill horn or nose meets the siding on its ends — the vertical joints where the sill meets the wall on the left and right.

Never Seal These

  • Weep holes and weep slots. Vinyl and aluminium windows have small slots or flapped openings on the outside face of the bottom frame. Water that gets into the sash track drains out through them. Caulk them shut and the track fills, overflows inward, and rots the interior sill.
  • The bottom nailing flange of a flanged (new-construction) window. The installer deliberately leaves the sill flange unsealed so that water behind the window can escape over the flashing below. If you can see the bottom fin, or the joint where the frame bottom meets the siding, leave it open.
  • Under a metal drip cap or Z-flashing. Many siding manufacturers specify a small gap — commonly around 1/4 inch — between the flashing above the head trim and the siding sitting on it. That gap is a drain; caulking it directs water behind the flashing rather than off it.
  • Brick veneer weep holes near the window, usually open head joints in the mortar just above a steel lintel. Same principle at a larger scale.

A note on that last row: on older single-glazed wood windows, the seal between the glass and the sash is glazing putty or glazing compound, not caulk. Running a caulk bead over cracked putty traps moisture against the wood. If the putty is failing, re-glaze rather than cover it.

Choosing the Caulk

We cover the chemistries in depth in our exterior caulk guide, so this is the short version for windows specifically. Two questions decide it: will the joint be painted, and how much does it move?

  • Painted wood or composite trim: a premium siliconised acrylic with a stated movement rating, or a hybrid (MS polymer) sealant. Both take paint and handle seasonal movement. This covers most windows.
  • Unpainted vinyl or aluminium frames that will never be painted: 100% silicone or a hybrid. Silicone sticks well to vinyl, glass and metal and outlasts everything else in UV — but nothing will ever paint over it.
  • Trim to brick, stone or stucco: polyurethane or hybrid. Masonry is a demanding substrate and wood-to-masonry joints move a lot.

Avoid plain, cheap acrylic latex ("painter's caulk") outside. It is designed for interior trim joints that barely move, and outdoors it typically cracks within a season or two. Buy by the movement rating and the label's exterior suitability, not the price. If the window is white vinyl and the house has painted trim, it is fine to use one paintable product for everything — matching the frame colour matters less than getting the right chemistry on the painted surfaces.

Tools and Materials

  • Caulk gun — a smooth-rod, dripless gun with a thrust ratio of at least 10:1. Thick polyurethanes and cold cartridges need more force; a 12:1 or higher gun saves your hand on those.
  • Sealant — plan on roughly one 10.1 oz cartridge per one or two average windows. A cartridge holds about 18 cubic inches, which lays down approximately 24 feet of a 1/4 × 1/4 inch bead, and far less in wide joints.
  • Closed-cell foam backer rod in a diameter about 25% wider than your widest gap.
  • Removal tools — utility knife with fresh blades, a 5-in-1 painter's tool, and a hooked caulk removal tool.
  • Cleaning — stiff nylon brush, bucket of water with a little detergent, a mildew cleaner or dilute bleach for dark staining, and isopropyl alcohol with lint-free rags.
  • Tooling — a plastic caulk-finishing tool or a gloved finger, plus a spray bottle of water (acrylics) or soapy water (silicone).
  • Optional — painter's tape for crisp lines on visible joints, an awl or screwdriver to probe for rot, and a ladder stabiliser for upper floors.

Budget roughly $30–$80 for a typical house's worth of windows using a mid-to-premium sealant, plus $15–$30 if you need to buy a decent gun.

Temperature and Rain Windows

Weather is where most otherwise good caulking jobs fail, because adhesion and cure both depend on it.

Temperature. Most exterior sealants list a minimum application temperature of about 40°F, and that is the surface temperature, not the air. A shaded north-side window at 8 a.m. in October can sit well below the forecast high. Some products are formulated for colder application, but the cartridge will say so explicitly. At the hot end, avoid sealing trim in direct midday sun above roughly 90°F: the surface skins too fast to tool, and the joint is at its narrowest, so the bead spends the winter stretched. The best conditions are a dry, mild day between about 50°F and 80°F.

Rain. Water-based acrylics are vulnerable until they skin and begin to cure. Standard products generally want several hours to a full day dry before rain; products sold as "rain-ready" or "water-ready" shorten that to as little as 30 minutes to a few hours. Silicone and hybrids shrug off rain much sooner, usually once they have skinned. The safe rule is to start only when the forecast gives you 24 dry hours, and longer in cool, humid weather when cure slows.

Moisture in the substrate. Equally important and easier to forget: the wood must be dry before you start. After rain or washing, allow at least a day of dry weather — two in cool or shaded spots — before sealing bare or weathered wood.

Step by Step

1. Inspect Before You Remove Anything

Walk the house and look at each window from the outside. Note split beads, caulk pulled away from one side, open mitres, peeling paint at the corners of the casing and dark staining below the sill. Push an awl into the bottom of the casings and the sill nose: if it sinks in easily, the wood is rotten and needs repair before sealing, because caulk over rot just hides it. Small soft spots can be consolidated and filled with a two-part exterior epoxy wood filler; larger ones need a section of trim replaced. Interior spackle and painter's putty are not exterior fillers — our spackle vs. putty guide explains what each is actually for.

Also check the inside. Water stains on the interior sill, the drywall below the window or the ceiling of the room beneath an upper-floor window tell you which windows are actually leaking and deserve the most attention.

2. Remove the Old Caulk Completely

New sealant does not bond reliably to old sealant, and caulking over a failed bead just buries the failure. Score along both edges of the old bead with a utility knife, then pull it out with a hooked removal tool or the point of a 5-in-1. Most aged acrylic comes out in strips once both sides are cut.

Stubborn acrylic softens with gentle heat from a hair dryer or a heat gun on its lowest setting — but keep heat well away from vinyl frames, which can distort, and from glass, which can crack from uneven heating. Old silicone leaves an invisible film that prevents almost anything bonding to it, including new silicone poorly. After cutting out the bulk, scrape the faces and wipe with a silicone remover product or mineral spirits, then isopropyl alcohol.

⚠️ Older homes and lead paint. Window trim in homes built before 1978 may carry lead paint under later coats, and scraping caulk out of painted joints disturbs it. Test with an EPA-recognised lead test kit before you start. If it is positive, mist the area with water while scraping, lay plastic sheeting below to catch debris, never dry-sand or use a heat gun at high temperature, and clean up with a HEPA vacuum and wet wipes.

3. Clean and Dry the Joint

Brush out dust, loose paint and old sealant fragments. Wash mildew-stained areas with a mildew cleaner or a solution of roughly one part household bleach to three parts water, rinse, and let it dry. On vinyl, aluminium and previously siliconed surfaces, a final wipe with isopropyl alcohol removes oils and residue and markedly improves adhesion.

Loose or chalking paint on the joint faces needs to come off — the sealant only bonds as well as whatever it is stuck to. Scrape back to sound paint and spot-prime bare wood with an exterior primer, letting it dry per the label before sealing. Then wait. A damp joint is the single most common cause of a bead that peels away from one side within the first year.

4. Fill Deep or Wide Gaps With Backer Rod

Any joint wider than about 1/4 inch needs backer rod before sealant. The rod does two things. It sets the depth of the bead, so you are not pumping half a tube into a cavity, and it stops the sealant bonding to the back of the joint. A bead stuck to three surfaces cannot stretch when the joint opens; it tears. A bead stuck to two sides only stretches like a rubber band.

Choose a rod about 25% wider than the gap so it holds by compression, and push it in evenly with a blunt tool — a plastic putty knife or the back of a spoon — to a depth that leaves room for the bead. The target shape is roughly twice as wide as it is deep, with a depth of at least 1/4 inch and generally no more than 1/2 inch. A 1/2-inch gap, for example, gets a bead about 1/4 inch deep.

Do not puncture or stretch closed-cell rod as you install it. A punctured cell releases gas into the curing sealant and leaves bubbles or blisters in the bead. Use scissors rather than tearing it to length.

5. Tape the Edges (Optional)

On joints that will be highly visible — a front-facing window or anything with dark trim against light siding — run painter's tape along both sides of the joint, leaving exactly the bead width exposed. It costs ten minutes and produces a dead-straight line. Pull the tape immediately after tooling, before the sealant skins, pulling away from the joint at a low angle.

6. Cut the Nozzle

Cut the tip at about a 45-degree angle, starting with an opening slightly smaller than the joint — you can always cut more off, but not put it back. For a 1/4-inch joint, an opening of around 3/16 inch is about right. Puncture the inner foil seal with the gun's built-in poker wire or a long nail; a nozzle that is cut but not punctured simply builds pressure until the cartridge bottom blows out.

7. Run the Bead

Hold the gun at about 45 degrees to the joint with the angled face of the nozzle against the work. Push the bead ahead of the nozzle rather than dragging it behind: pushing forces sealant into the joint and against both faces, while pulling tends to lay it on the surface and bridge over voids. Squeeze steadily and move at a constant speed, watching the sealant fill the joint just ahead of the tip rather than watching the gun.

Work in runs as long as you can manage — the full height of one side casing, then the head — so there are as few stops and restarts in the bead as possible. Each restart is a weak point. At the end of a run, release the gun's pressure with the thumb catch and pull away; a dripless gun stops the flow, a cheap one keeps oozing.

Do the head first, then the sides, working top to bottom so that anything you drip lands on unfinished areas rather than finished beads. Stop the side beads at the bottom of the casing; do not wrap the bead across the bottom.

8. Tool It Within the Skin Time

Tooling is not cosmetic. Pressing the bead pushes sealant firmly against both joint faces, closes air pockets, and leaves a slightly concave surface that is thinner in the middle — the profile that stretches best. Do it within the skin time on the cartridge, typically 5–15 minutes for acrylics and often less for silicone in warm weather. After a skin forms, tooling tears it.

For acrylics, mist the bead lightly with water and draw a gloved finger or plastic tool along it in one smooth pass. For silicone and hybrids, use a tool dipped in soapy water. Wipe the tool frequently. Remove excess from the siding immediately with a damp rag for water-based products, or mineral spirits for polyurethane — once cured, polyurethane on siding is a scraping job.

9. Protect It While It Skins

Keep sprinklers off and do not pressure wash the house for at least a week. If a shower arrives earlier than forecast on a fresh acrylic bead, inspect it after it dries: washed-out or milky beads should be cut out and redone rather than painted over.

Painting and Cure Times

Only paint a sealant labelled paintable — siliconised acrylics, polyurethanes and most hybrids. Pure silicone cannot be painted, and paint applied over it beads up and peels. Even "paintable" is a spectrum: some acrylics accept paint within 30 minutes, while polyurethanes may need a day or more, and painting too early traps solvent or water, which wrinkles or cracks the paint film over the bead.

These are typical ranges; the cartridge always wins. Cure slows considerably in cool or humid weather, and deep beads take longer than thin ones because they cure from the surface inward. Use a quality exterior acrylic paint over the bead, and carry the paint about 1/16 inch onto the siding or frame so the paint film seals the bead's edge. Leaving a bare strip of sealant at each edge is common and shortens its life, because UV attacks the exposed edge first.

If a leak has already stained the interior — a brown ring on the drywall below the window, or a stain on the ceiling of the room under an upper-floor window — fix the outside first and wait a few dry weeks to be sure it has stopped. Then seal the stain with a stain-blocking primer before repainting, or it bleeds straight back through; our ceiling paint guide covers which primers and paints hide water stains.

Common Mistakes

Sealing the drainage paths. The most damaging mistake, and the easiest to make with good intentions. A full rectangle of caulk around a flanged window — including the bottom — turns a small leak behind the trim into a reservoir. Head and sides, never the bottom.

Caulking over old caulk. It looks fine for a summer. The new bead is bonded to a failed bead rather than to the trim, and it lets go with it.

Using caulk as filler. A 1-inch gap filled solid with sealant is expensive, slow to cure, and fails in the middle as it moves. Backer rod first, or repair the trim so the gap is a joint again.

Caulking damp wood. The bead may look perfect and peel off one side in a few months. Wait for a dry day after a dry day.

Huge, smeared beads. An oversized nozzle and heavy tooling leave sealant spread an inch onto the siding. The thin feathered edges crack and peel, and they look poor. Cut the nozzle smaller and use tape on visible joints.

Silicone on painted trim. It will never take paint, and its residue contaminates the wood so even the next caulk job struggles to bond. Use a paintable product anywhere paint is involved now or might be later.

Treating caulk as the cure for a leak. If water is coming in around a window despite intact caulk, the problem is usually missing or failed flashing, a bad window seal, or water arriving from above. Overflowing gutters that dump sheets of water directly onto window heads are a frequent cause — keeping gutters clear often does more for a leaking upper window than any amount of sealant.

How Often to Recaulk

Inspect exterior window caulk every year, ideally in spring after the freeze-thaw season, and plan on recaulking:

  • Every 5–10 years for good siliconised acrylics on painted trim — usually timed with repainting.
  • Every 10–20 years for hybrids, polyurethanes and silicone, if the joint was prepared and sized properly.
  • Every 2–3 years for cheap acrylic latex, which is one of the better arguments for not using it outside.

South- and west-facing windows take the most UV and heat cycling and fail first, so start your inspection there. Look for hairline splits along the middle of the bead, gaps along either edge, hardening (press with a fingernail — sound sealant gives), and mildew. You do not need to redo a whole window because one section has failed: cut out the failed length back to sound material, clean the end of the remaining bead, and overlap the new bead onto it by about half an inch. A full redo is due once failures are appearing on several sides.

If you are repainting the trim anyway, recaulk at the same time, after scraping and priming and before the finish coats. Bare wood trim that you prefer to keep unpainted needs its own protection regime — a penetrating finish from our exterior wood sealer guide — and a non-staining sealant chosen to suit it.

Ladder Safety

The sealant is the easy part; for most people, the ladder is the actual risk. Falls from ladders send many thousands of people to emergency rooms in the US every year, and a large share happen during routine home maintenance at modest heights.

  • Set the angle with the 4-to-1 rule. For every 4 feet of height to where the ladder rests, the base goes 1 foot out from the wall. A 16-foot contact point means a base 4 feet out.
  • Never rest the ladder on the window or glass. Use a ladder stabiliser (standoff) that spans the window and bears on the wall either side. It also puts you at a comfortable distance from the trim instead of pinned against it.
  • Firm, level footing. Use levelling feet or a purpose-made leveller on slopes, not bricks or boards. On soft soil, set the feet on a wide board and stake the base.
  • Keep your belt buckle between the rails. Reach only as far as you can while keeping your body centred; move the ladder rather than leaning. Caulking a whole casing usually means two or three ladder positions.
  • Three points of contact while climbing, with tools in a pouch or hung on the ladder with a hook, not in your hand.
  • Respect the rating. A Type I (250 lb) or Type IA (300 lb) duty rating covers you plus tools; check the label.
  • On a stepladder, do not stand on the top cap or the step just below it.
  • Stay at least 10 feet from overhead power lines, especially with aluminium ladders, and do not work in wind.

Second-storey windows over a slope, a deck or a lower roof are a reasonable place to stop and hire help. A handyman or painter will typically recaulk a set of upper windows in a few hours, and the cost is small compared with the alternative. For first-floor windows reachable from a stepladder, this is one of the most rewarding small jobs on a house.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I caulk the bottom of an exterior window?

Generally no. Caulk the head and both sides of the trim where it meets the siding, and the trim-to-frame joint on the head and sides, but leave the bottom open. On flanged (nail-fin) windows the bottom flange is deliberately left unsealed so water that gets behind the window can drain out over the flashing below, and vinyl and aluminium frames have weep slots along the bottom that must stay clear. Sealing the bottom traps water inside the wall, where it rots the sill and framing.

What is the best caulk for exterior windows?

For painted wood or composite trim, a premium siliconised acrylic with a stated movement rating or a hybrid MS-polymer sealant — both take paint and tolerate seasonal movement. For unpainted vinyl or aluminium frames that will never be painted, 100% silicone or a hybrid gives the longest life. Where trim meets brick, stone or stucco, polyurethane or a hybrid bonds best to masonry. Avoid cheap acrylic latex painter's caulk outdoors; it cracks within a season or two.

Do I have to remove old caulk before recaulking windows?

Yes. New sealant bonds poorly to old sealant, so caulking over a failed bead means the new one fails with it, usually within a year. Score both edges with a utility knife, pull the old bead out with a hooked removal tool or 5-in-1, and scrape the joint faces clean. Old silicone residue blocks adhesion, so wipe those joints with a silicone remover and then isopropyl alcohol.

How cold is too cold to caulk outside?

Most exterior sealants list a minimum application temperature around 40°F, measured at the surface rather than the air, and the surface needs to stay above that while the bead skins. Shaded walls and early mornings run colder than the forecast. The ideal is a dry, mild day between roughly 50°F and 80°F.

How long before caulk can get rained on?

It depends on the chemistry. Standard water-based acrylics generally need several hours to a full day before rain; products labelled rain-ready or water-ready shorten that to as little as 30 minutes to a few hours. Silicone and hybrid sealants tolerate rain much sooner, typically once they have skinned. The practical rule is to start only with at least 24 dry hours forecast, and more in cool, humid weather.

When can I paint over new exterior caulk?

Only over a product labelled paintable, and only after the time on the cartridge. Many siliconised acrylics accept paint within 30 minutes to two hours, hybrids often the same day, and polyurethanes commonly need 24 hours or more. Painting too early wrinkles or cracks the paint over the bead. Pure silicone cannot be painted at all.

Do I need backer rod for window caulking?

For any gap wider than about 1/4 inch, yes. Backer rod sets the bead depth so you are not filling a cavity with sealant, and it stops the bead bonding to the back of the joint. A bead stuck to three surfaces cannot stretch and tears when the joint opens; one stuck only to the two sides flexes freely. Use closed-cell rod about 25% wider than the gap and aim for a bead roughly twice as wide as it is deep.

How often should exterior windows be recaulked?

Inspect every year, ideally each spring. Good siliconised acrylics on painted trim typically last 5–10 years and are usually redone with each repaint. Hybrids, polyurethanes and silicone can last 10–20 years in a properly prepared and sized joint. Cheap acrylic latex may need redoing every two to three years outside. South- and west-facing windows fail first. Isolated failures can be patched; failures on several sides mean the whole window is due.
