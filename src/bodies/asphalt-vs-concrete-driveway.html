Asphalt vs. Concrete Driveway — The 2026 Cost Comparison

Asphalt costs less to install. Concrete costs less to own. Here are the real per-square-foot numbers, the maintenance nobody quotes, and the climate and soil factors that decide it.

A driveway is one of the few home improvements where the two mainstream options are genuinely close on merit, and where the right answer flips depending on your climate, your budget horizon, and how much maintenance you are willing to do. Asphalt is cheaper to install and easier to repair. Concrete lasts longer and needs less attention. Neither is universally correct, and the cost comparison that dominates the decision is more complicated than the per-square-foot numbers suggest.

This guide covers what each surface actually costs to install in 2026, what it costs to maintain and eventually remove, how each performs in heat and in freeze-thaw climates, and how to work out which one is cheaper for your situation rather than in the abstract.

⚡ Quick Answer: Asphalt is cheaper up front — roughly $7–$13 per square foot installed versus $8–$18 for plain concrete — and it is the better choice in cold, freeze-thaw climates and on sloped or shifting ground. Concrete is cheaper over 30 years because it lasts 30–40 years against asphalt's 15–20 and needs far less maintenance. Choose asphalt for lower entry cost, cold winters, and easy repairs. Choose concrete for longevity, hot climates, and appearance.

Asphalt Driveway Cost — What You Are Paying For

At $7–$13 per square foot installed, a typical asphalt driveway breaks down roughly as follows:

  • Excavation and grading — $1.50–$3.00/sq ft. Removing topsoil, cutting to grade, and establishing drainage fall.
  • Aggregate base — $1.50–$3.00/sq ft. Six to eight inches of compacted crushed stone. This layer determines how long the driveway lasts and is the first thing a cheap bid trims.
  • Asphalt material and placement — $3.50–$6.00/sq ft. Typically 2 inches of compacted surface course over a 2–3 inch binder course for residential work.
  • Compaction, edging, and cleanup — $0.50–$1.00/sq ft.

Asphalt driveway cost per square foot is heavily exposed to crude oil prices, because asphalt binder is a petroleum product. Quotes swing by 15–25% between years on material cost alone, and paving contractors price seasonally — late summer and early fall, when plants are running and crews are booked but not desperate, tends to produce the most honest bids. The cheapest quotes generally appear at the very end of the season, with the trade-off that a late-season pour in a cold climate risks compaction problems.

The most important line in an asphalt quote is the base depth and the compacted asphalt thickness. A contractor quoting 1.5 inches of asphalt over 4 inches of base will always beat one quoting 3 inches over 8 inches, and the driveway will fail years earlier. For residential use, ask for a minimum of 3 inches total compacted asphalt over 6 inches of compacted aggregate; 4 inches over 8 in areas with heavy frost or soft soil.

Concrete Driveway Cost — What You Are Paying For

Plain broom-finished concrete at $8–$18 per square foot breaks down as:

  • Excavation and grading — $1.50–$3.00/sq ft.
  • Base and forming — $1.50–$3.00/sq ft. Four to six inches of compacted aggregate, plus form setting.
  • Reinforcement — $0.50–$1.50/sq ft. Wire mesh or, better, rebar on chairs. Fiber-reinforced mixes add a smaller premium.
  • Concrete and placement — $4.00–$8.00/sq ft. Four inches for cars, 5–6 inches if trucks or RVs will park on it.
  • Finishing, jointing, and curing — $1.00–$2.50/sq ft.

Concrete driveway cost per square foot is driven by ready-mix prices, which have risen steadily and are less volatile than asphalt but rarely fall. Labor is a larger share of a concrete job than an asphalt job because finishing is skilled work performed under time pressure — a concrete crew has one window to get the surface right, and a poor finish cannot be fixed afterward the way a rough asphalt surface can be overlaid.

Reinforcement and jointing are the two specifications worth scrutinizing. Concrete cracks; that is not a defect, it is a material property. The job of control joints is to decide where it cracks, and the job of reinforcement is to hold the cracked pieces in alignment. Joints should be cut at intervals no greater than 2.5 to 3 times the slab thickness in feet — for a 4-inch slab, that is 10 to 12 feet — and cut within 6 to 18 hours of the pour.

Cost to Stain a Concrete Driveway

Staining existing concrete runs $3–$9 per square foot professionally, depending on whether it is a single-color acid stain or a multi-color decorative treatment with a scored pattern. DIY acid staining costs $0.50–$1.50 per square foot in materials but is unforgiving — the reaction depends on the concrete's own chemistry and cannot be undone. It also requires sealing afterward, and any driveway stain needs resealing every 2–3 years to hold its color under UV and tire traffic.

Black Concrete Driveways

Charcoal and black driveways have become popular, and there are two routes: integral pigment added to the mix (permanent, uniform, adds $1–$3 per square foot) or a topical stain or dye applied afterward (cheaper, but fades and requires recoating). A word of caution that applies to both — dark concrete absorbs substantially more solar heat than light concrete, which increases surface temperature, accelerates the drying of the surface during curing, and makes barefoot use unpleasant in summer. In hot climates it also increases thermal movement at the joints.

Which One Suits Your Climate?

Climate is the single most decisive factor, and it is the one that most cost-focused comparisons skip.

Cold Climates and Freeze-Thaw — Advantage: Asphalt

Asphalt is a flexible pavement. It moves with the ground rather than resisting it, which means frost heave lifts and lowers it without cracking it apart. It is also dark, so it absorbs solar heat and sheds snow and ice faster, and it tolerates road salt and de-icing chemicals essentially indefinitely.

Concrete in a freeze-thaw climate is more demanding. It must be air-entrained (tiny air bubbles deliberately introduced in the mix give freezing water somewhere to expand into), it must be properly jointed, and it is genuinely vulnerable to de-icing salts — chlorides drive surface scaling, and calcium and magnesium chloride are worse than sodium chloride. A concrete driveway in Minnesota salted every winter will show surface deterioration long before one in Georgia.

Hot Climates — Advantage: Concrete

Asphalt softens as it heats. On a 100°F day, surface temperatures on a black driveway can exceed 140°F, at which point the binder becomes plastic enough that a motorcycle kickstand, a trailer jack, or a hot tire on a turned wheel will leave a permanent depression. Sustained heat also accelerates oxidation, which is what turns asphalt grey and brittle. Concrete is dimensionally stable at any temperature a driveway will see, stays cooler underfoot, and does not deform under point loads.

Slopes and Unstable Soil — Advantage: Asphalt

On expansive clay, on fill, or on a slope where some movement is inevitable, asphalt's flexibility is a real asset — it deforms and can be overlaid. Concrete on moving ground cracks, and a cracked slab is far harder to make good than a rutted asphalt surface.

Maintenance — The Cost Nobody Quotes

This is where the two surfaces genuinely diverge, and where the up-front comparison misleads.

Asphalt needs sealcoating every 3–5 years. The sealer protects the binder from UV oxidation and from fuel and oil, both of which dissolve it. Skipping it does not produce a sudden failure; it produces a driveway that greys, ravels at the surface, and reaches the end of its life at 12 years instead of 20. At $0.15–$0.30 per square foot, sealcoating a 600 sq ft driveway costs $90–$180 in materials for DIY, or $250–$450 professionally. Over 20 years that is four to six applications — call it $1,200–$2,500 professionally.

Asphalt also needs crack filling as cracks appear, which is inexpensive and genuinely effective if done early. A crack that has been open long enough to let water into the base is a different and more expensive problem.

Concrete is close to maintenance-free by comparison. Sealing is optional in mild climates and advisable in salted ones, at 5–10 year intervals. Joints should be resealed when the filler degrades. Cracks that appear away from control joints can be filled but not invisibly repaired — this is concrete's real weakness, that damage is permanent and visible in a way asphalt damage is not.

💰 30-Year Cost Comparison, 600 sq ft driveway: Asphalt — $6,000 install + ~$1,800 sealcoating + one full replacement at year 18 ($6,500) = roughly $14,300. Concrete — $7,800 install + ~$800 occasional sealing + no replacement = roughly $8,600. Concrete wins decisively on a long horizon, provided the climate does not punish it.

Repairs and Appearance Over Time

Asphalt repairs well. Cracks are filled with hot or cold pour crack filler, potholes are cut out and patched, and a worn surface can be overlaid with a fresh 1.5–2 inch lift for roughly half the cost of a full replacement — reusing the existing base entirely. After a sealcoat, an old asphalt driveway looks close to new.

Concrete repairs badly. A patch will not match the surrounding slab in color or texture, and it never will — new concrete cures lighter and weathers differently. Slabs that have settled can be lifted with polyurethane foam injection (mudjacking's modern replacement) at $5–$25 per square foot, which is often worth doing. But a cracked, spalled concrete driveway generally has to be lived with or replaced. There is no equivalent of the resurfacing overlay.

On appearance: concrete offers far more design range — broom finish, exposed aggregate, stamped patterns, integral color, scored geometry. Asphalt offers black, and later, grey.

Cost to Remove an Existing Driveway

Removal is a real line item and it differs meaningfully between the two.

Cost to remove an asphalt driveway: $1–$3 per square foot. Asphalt breaks up easily, is light relative to concrete, and is fully recyclable — reclaimed asphalt pavement is a valuable feedstock, so disposal is cheap and sometimes free. A 600 sq ft asphalt driveway typically costs $600–$1,800 to remove and haul.

Cost to remove a concrete driveway: $2–$6 per square foot. Concrete is heavier, requires breaking with a hydraulic hammer, and reinforced slabs are slower still because the rebar or mesh has to be cut. Disposal fees are higher by weight. A 600 sq ft concrete driveway typically costs $1,200–$3,600 to remove.

One option worth asking about: if the existing surface is structurally sound, you may be able to pave over it rather than remove it. Asphalt overlays on sound asphalt are routine. Asphalt over sound concrete is possible but risks reflective cracking at the slab joints. Concrete over asphalt is generally not advisable.

Which Is Cheaper — and Which Should You Choose?

Is concrete or asphalt cheaper? Asphalt is cheaper to install, typically by 15–30% for comparable quality. Concrete is cheaper to own, because it lasts roughly twice as long and demands much less maintenance. The crossover point in a temperate climate sits somewhere around year 15 to 18 — before that, asphalt has cost you less; after that, concrete has.

That makes the honest decision rule about time horizon and climate rather than price:

Choose asphalt if: you are in a freeze-thaw climate; your driveway is on a slope or on soil that moves; you need the lowest possible entry cost; you may sell within a decade; or you want a surface that can be resurfaced rather than replaced.

Choose concrete if: you are in a hot climate; you intend to stay long enough for the longevity to pay back; you want a surface you can largely ignore; appearance and design options matter; or heavy vehicles will park on it regularly.

Choose pavers if: you want the longest life and the best repairability and the budget accommodates it.

🔍 Before You Sign: Get three itemized bids and compare the base depth and surface thickness, not the total. Ask what compaction equipment will be used and how many lifts the base goes down in. Ask about drainage — where water goes matters more than what the surface is made of. A well-built asphalt driveway outlasts a badly built concrete one by a decade.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is asphalt or concrete cheaper for a driveway?

Asphalt is cheaper to install — roughly $7–$13 per square foot against $8–$18 for plain concrete, so a 600-square-foot driveway runs about $4,200–$7,800 in asphalt versus $4,800–$10,800 in concrete. Concrete is cheaper to own. It lasts 30–40 years against asphalt's 15–20 and needs far less maintenance, while asphalt requires sealcoating every 3–5 years at $250–$450 a time professionally. Over a 30-year horizon on a 600 sq ft driveway, asphalt totals roughly $14,300 including one replacement, and concrete roughly $8,600. The crossover falls around year 15–18. If you may sell within a decade, asphalt is the better financial choice; if you are staying, concrete is.

How much does a concrete driveway cost?

Plain broom-finished concrete runs $8–$18 per square foot installed in 2026, which puts a two-car driveway of about 600 square feet at $4,800–$10,800 and a three-car driveway of 900 square feet at $7,200–$16,200. Stamped or decorative concrete adds substantially, running $12–$28 per square foot. The spread within those ranges comes from site conditions more than anything else — excavation of an existing surface, added base depth for poor drainage, difficult truck access, steep grades, and expansive clay subgrade each add 20–40%. Within the quote itself, watch the slab thickness (4 inches for cars, 5–6 for trucks or an RV), the reinforcement, and the control joint spacing.

How much does an asphalt driveway cost?

Asphalt runs $7–$13 per square foot installed, so a 600-square-foot two-car driveway typically costs $4,200–$7,800 and a 1,200-square-foot long driveway $8,400–$15,600. Because asphalt binder is a petroleum product, material costs swing 15–25% between years with crude prices, and contractors price seasonally. The specification that matters most in a quote is depth: ask for a minimum of 3 inches of compacted asphalt over 6 inches of compacted aggregate base, or 4 over 8 in heavy-frost regions or soft soil. A bid quoting 1.5 inches over 4 inches will always be cheaper and will fail years sooner.

Which lasts longer, asphalt or concrete?

Concrete, by roughly double. A properly installed concrete driveway lasts 30–40 years; asphalt lasts 15–20. But that comparison assumes a climate that suits each material. In a hard freeze-thaw region where de-icing salts are used every winter, concrete's life shortens meaningfully because chlorides drive surface scaling — calcium and magnesium chloride more aggressively than sodium chloride. In a hot climate, asphalt's life shortens because heat softens the binder and accelerates oxidation. Installation quality also outweighs material choice: a well-built asphalt driveway on a proper 6–8 inch compacted base will outlast a concrete slab poured thin on unprepared ground.

Can I put asphalt over an existing concrete driveway?

It is possible but not ideal. The main problem is reflective cracking — the joints and cracks in the concrete slab below telegraph up through the new asphalt within a few years, because the two materials move differently and the asphalt has nothing to hold it together across a moving joint. Techniques exist to mitigate it, including a thicker overlay and a geotextile interlayer, but they add cost and do not eliminate the issue. Asphalt over sound existing asphalt is a different matter entirely and is routine: a fresh 1.5–2 inch lift over a stable old surface costs roughly half a full replacement and reuses the existing base. Concrete over asphalt is generally not advisable — the flexible layer beneath will not support a rigid slab.

How much does it cost to remove a driveway?

Removing asphalt costs $1–$3 per square foot, so a 600-square-foot driveway runs $600–$1,800. Asphalt breaks up easily, is relatively light, and is fully recyclable as reclaimed asphalt pavement, which keeps disposal costs low. Removing concrete costs $2–$6 per square foot, or $1,200–$3,600 for the same area. Concrete is heavier, needs a hydraulic hammer to break, and reinforced slabs take longer because the rebar or mesh has to be cut through. Disposal is charged by weight, so concrete costs more there too. Before budgeting for removal, ask whether the existing surface is sound enough to pave over — it often is, and that avoids the cost entirely.

Does asphalt or concrete handle cold winters better?

Asphalt, clearly. It is a flexible pavement that moves with frost heave rather than resisting it, so ground movement lifts and lowers it without breaking it apart. Its dark surface absorbs solar heat and clears snow and ice faster. And it is essentially immune to road salt and de-icing chemicals. Concrete in a freeze-thaw climate needs to be air-entrained — deliberate microscopic air bubbles in the mix that give freezing water room to expand — properly jointed, and ideally sealed, and it remains vulnerable to chloride-driven surface scaling from de-icers. Concrete works in cold climates when it is specified and installed correctly, but the margin for error is much narrower than with asphalt.

How thick should a driveway be?

For asphalt, aim for a minimum of 3 inches of compacted asphalt over 6 inches of compacted aggregate base for cars, and 4 inches over 8 inches where frost is deep, soil is soft, or heavy vehicles will use it. For concrete, 4 inches of slab over 4–6 inches of compacted base handles passenger cars; go to 5–6 inches of slab if trucks, an RV, or a boat trailer will park on it regularly. In both cases the base depth matters at least as much as the surface thickness, and it is the first thing trimmed in a low bid. Ask any contractor to state base depth, number of compaction lifts, and finished surface thickness in writing before you sign.
