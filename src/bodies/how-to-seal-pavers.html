How to Seal Pavers — A Step-by-Step DIY Guide

Sealing pavers is mostly waiting, cleaning and drying, with about an hour of actual spraying at the end. Get the timing and the film thickness right and the job is forgiving. Get either wrong and you get haze, peeling or a slippery patio.

To seal pavers, you wait until new pavers have finished pushing out efflorescence, clean the surface thoroughly, refill the joints with sand and let them cure, then spray a thin coat of sealer with a low-pressure pump sprayer and back-roll it, adding a second thin coat if the product calls for one. The spraying itself takes an hour or two on a typical patio. Everything that decides whether the result looks good for three years or turns white in three days happens before that.

Most paver sealing failures trace back to two causes: moisture trapped under the sealer, and sealer applied too thick. Both are avoidable with patience and a light hand, and this guide walks through each step in the order it needs to happen.

⚡ The whole job in one line: wait 60–90 days on new pavers (longer if they are still blooming white), clean and remove efflorescence, re-sand the joints and let polymeric sand cure, wait for 24–48 dry hours and a forecast of 50–90°F with no rain for 24 hours, then apply two thin coats rather than one thick one. Expect foot traffic after about 24 hours and vehicles after 48–72.

When Pavers Can Be Sealed

Concrete pavers contain soluble salts, mostly calcium hydroxide, that migrate to the surface as water moves through the unit and evaporates. On the surface they react with carbon dioxide and leave a chalky white bloom called efflorescence. It is not a defect and it is not permanent, but it is the main reason you cannot seal new pavers straight away.

If you seal over active efflorescence, the salts keep coming up and get trapped under the sealer film, where they show as a cloudy white layer that no cleaner can reach. The only fix at that point is stripping the sealer. So the rule is to let the pavers finish blooming first.

The common guidance from paver manufacturers and sealer makers is to wait at least 60–90 days after installation. That is a minimum, not a guarantee. Pavers installed in late autumn, or in a wet climate, may keep blooming through the following spring, and some installations show efflorescence on and off for most of the first year. The practical test is simple: clean a section, let it dry for a few days, and look. If fresh white haze reappears, the pavers are not ready.

A few points refine the timing:

  • Natural stone and clay brick pavers do not effloresce the way concrete does, so the wait is mostly about the joint sand and the surface drying. A couple of weeks is usually enough.
  • Some breathable, water-based penetrating sealers are labelled for use on newer pavers because they let vapour and salts pass. Even then, sealing over visible efflorescence locks in the look you have that day.
  • Older pavers that have been down for years can be sealed whenever they are clean and dry. The question there is how often, which we cover in our guide to how often to seal pavers.

Step 1 — Clean and Remove Efflorescence

Sealer locks in whatever is on the surface, including dirt, stains, moss and salt. Cleaning is the step most worth overdoing.

Treat Stains First

Spot-treat oil and grease with a paver degreaser, following the dwell time on the label, and scrub before rinsing. Deep, old oil stains often need a poultice-type cleaner left to dry and draw the oil out, and sometimes two rounds. Rust stains need a dedicated rust remover. Organic staining from leaves and algae usually comes out with a general paver cleaner or a diluted oxygen bleach solution. Do this before the general wash, because pressure washing a fresh oil stain tends to spread it.

Pressure Wash

Wash the whole area with a 25° or 40° fan tip held 8–12 inches from the surface, working at a low angle so you are pushing dirt off rather than drilling into the paver. A rotary surface cleaner attachment is faster and much more even on large areas, and it is the easiest way to avoid the striped look of uneven wand cleaning. Never use a zero-degree tip; it etches concrete pavers and exposes the aggregate in lines.

Expect to blast some joint sand out, especially on older installations. That is fine, because you will re-sand in the next step, and removing the old, dirty sand and any weeds growing in it is part of the point.

Remove Efflorescence

If white haze is present after the pavers have dried, treat it with an efflorescence remover made for concrete pavers. These are buffered acid products, typically based on phosphoric, sulfamic or a weak hydrochloric blend, formulated to dissolve the salt deposits without burning the cement paste and fading the colour the way straight muriatic acid can.

  1. Test a spot in an inconspicuous area first, especially on coloured or tumbled pavers.
  2. Pre-wet the pavers with plain water so the acid works on the surface instead of soaking in.
  3. Apply at the labelled dilution, scrub with a stiff nylon brush, and do not let it dry on the surface.
  4. Rinse thoroughly, then rinse again. Acid residue left behind can itself cause haze and interfere with sealer bonding.

After removal, wait a few days and check again. If the efflorescence returns, the pavers are still releasing salts and you should wait longer before sealing rather than repeat the acid treatment.

💡 Protect plants and metal. Wet down nearby plantings before using acid-based or bleach cleaners and rinse them afterwards. Keep efflorescence remover away from metal railings, aluminium thresholds and car paint, and cover drains if your local rules require it.

Step 2 — Re-Sand the Joints

Joints should be full before sealing. Sand that sits a quarter-inch below the top of the paver leaves a channel for water, invites weeds and ants, and lets the pavers rock and shift. Full joints also lock the units together so the sealed surface behaves as one.

Polymeric Sand or Regular Joint Sand?

For most patios and walkways, polymeric sand is the default. It contains binders that harden when wetted, which resists washout and weed growth far better than plain sand. Our guide on how to use polymeric sand covers the installation in detail, and the best polymeric sand for pavers compares the main products by joint width and finish.

The alternative is plain jointing sand plus a joint-stabilizing sealer. These sealers are thin enough to flow down into the joints and bind ordinary sand as they cure, so the sealer does the job the polymer would. It is a sound approach and avoids polymeric sand's haze risk, but it only works with regular sand. Do not rely on a joint-stabilizing sealer to fix polymeric sand that failed to set.

The Short Version of the Re-Sand

  1. Make sure the pavers and joints are completely dry. Damp joints make polymeric sand stick to the paver faces and activate early.
  2. Sweep the sand diagonally across the joints until they are full.
  3. Run a plate compactor with a protective pad over the area to settle the sand, then top up and repeat until the sand stays about 1/8 inch below the paver edge, or below the chamfer on bevelled pavers.
  4. Blow every grain of loose sand off the paver surfaces with a leaf blower. Residue left on top is what causes polymeric haze.
  5. Activate with a fine shower setting, following the manufacturer's wetting sequence exactly.

How Long to Wait Before Sealing

Polymeric sand must be fully cured and fully dry before sealer goes on. Manufacturers' waiting periods vary, so read the bag, but plan on at least 48 hours in warm, dry weather, and closer to a week when it is cool or humid. Sealing over damp polymeric joints traps moisture, which is a direct route to clouding in the joints and along the paver edges. If you pressure-washed hard enough to empty the joints, this cure period is unavoidable, so build it into the schedule.

Step 3 — Pick the Weather Window

Sealer cares about three things: temperature, moisture in the pavers, and what the next 24 hours bring.

A simple way to check moisture is the same plastic-sheet test used on concrete slabs: tape a square of clear plastic to a paver for a few hours in the sun. Condensation underneath means the pavers are still wet inside. Shaded areas, low spots and the base of downspouts stay damp longest, so test there rather than in the sunniest corner.

The best window in most of the country is late spring or early autumn: a stretch of dry days, mild temperatures and no heavy dew. Midsummer works if you seal early in the morning before the pavers heat up. Do not seal when overnight temperatures are forecast to drop below freezing.

Step 4 — Choose the Right Sealer

Paver sealers differ in two ways that matter: how they look, and what carries them.

Natural Look vs Wet Look

Natural-look sealers are mostly penetrating products, typically silane/siloxane blends or low-solids water-based acrylics, that soak into the paver and leave little or no film on top. The pavers look much as they did before sealing, perhaps slightly richer in colour. They are less slippery, rarely peel, and are the forgiving choice for a first-time DIY job. The trade-off is that they do less to deepen colour and do not add any gloss. Our roundup of the best paver sealers for a natural look compares the main options.

Wet-look sealers are film-forming acrylics or acrylic-urethane blends that leave a coating on the surface and make the pavers look permanently damp, with deeper colour and a satin to gloss sheen. They are what most people picture when they think of sealed pavers. They also bring the classic film problems: they can blush, peel and become slick when wet, and they need more care in application. See the best paver sealers for a wet look for products in this category.

For a first DIY job, a water-based sealer is the safer choice. It is easier to apply evenly, cleans up with water, and is less likely to haze if conditions are not perfect. The best water-based paver sealers include both natural and enhancing finishes, so choosing water-based does not lock you into a flat look.

Solvent-based acrylics give the deepest wet look and have one real advantage: because each new coat re-dissolves the one beneath, problems like blush and scuffs can often be fixed without stripping. They are also less forgiving of damp pavers and humid days, and they cannot go over most water-based sealers.

Joint-Stabilizing Sealers

As covered above, these are thin, penetrating sealers designed to flood the joints and bind regular sand. Choose one if you are re-sanding with plain jointing sand, if you want to avoid polymeric sand altogether, or if you are resealing an older patio whose joints are sound but loose on top. Skip it if the joints are polymeric; a standard sealer is the better match there.

💡 Check what is already on the pavers. If the patio was sealed before and you do not know with what, test a small area first. Water-based over solvent, or solvent over water-based, frequently fails to bond or wrinkles the old coat. A drop of xylene on the surface is a rough guide: if the old coating softens and turns tacky, it is likely a solvent acrylic.

Step 5 — Apply the First Coat

The aim is a thin, even coat that soaks in or levels without pooling. More sealer does not mean more protection; past a point, it means haze and peeling.

Before You Start

  • Blow the surface clean one last time to remove dust, leaves and loose sand.
  • Mask siding, garage door bottoms, house walls and anything else within overspray range. Cover plants with sheet you remove as soon as you finish.
  • Stir, do not shake, the sealer, and strain it if the label suggests.
  • Plan your exit. Start at the far corner and work towards a step, gate or the lawn so you never walk on wet sealer.
  • Test a small area to confirm the look, coverage rate and how quickly it soaks in.

Spray, Then Back-Roll

Use a low-pressure pump sprayer with a fan tip and pump it only enough to get an even, wide fan, not a misty jet. Spray a section of roughly 30–50 square feet, enough that you can roll it before the edges start drying, then immediately back-roll with a 3/8" nap roller to spread the sealer, push it into the paver texture and lift out any puddles.

Work in this rhythm across the area, overlapping each sprayed section slightly to keep a wet edge. On patterned pavers, roll along the long dimension of the pattern rather than across it; it hides small lap marks better. Some water-based penetrating sealers are designed for spray-only application with no back-rolling, so follow the label where it differs.

Avoid Puddling

Pooled sealer in low spots, in the chamfers along paver edges and in the joints is the start of nearly every white or peeling patch. As you roll, watch the edges and joints and pull excess out with a nearly dry roller. Joints should be wetted, not flooded, unless you are deliberately using a joint-stabilizing sealer whose instructions call for it.

How Much to Apply

Coverage depends on the sealer and how porous the pavers are. As a rough guide, a first coat on concrete pavers often covers somewhere around 100–150 square feet per gallon, and a second coat goes further because the surface has less to absorb. Very porous or tumbled pavers drink more. Use the label's figure, measure your area, and pour out the right amount for each section rather than spraying until it looks done; that habit alone prevents most over-application.

💡 Thin is right. A good first coat looks barely wet within a few minutes as it soaks in. If it still looks glossy and pooled after five minutes, you put down too much. Roll it out before it starts to skin.

Step 6 — The Second Coat

Most film-forming and enhancing sealers are designed for two thin coats. The first one mostly soaks in and seals the pores; the second builds the even colour and sheen. Many natural-look penetrating sealers are single-coat products, and adding more can leave an unwanted film, so check before reaching for the sprayer again.

Timing depends entirely on chemistry and conditions:

  • Water-based film-forming sealers usually want the first coat dry to the touch, typically one to four hours in warm weather.
  • Some water-based penetrating sealers are applied wet-on-wet: the second coat goes on within minutes, while the first is still damp, so both soak in together.
  • Solvent-based acrylics generally need the first coat dry, often a few hours, sometimes overnight in cool weather.

Apply the second coat at right angles to the first if you can, still spraying and back-rolling, and still thinly. Remember the second coat covers more area per gallon than the first, so reduce the amount per section accordingly.

This is also the point to add an anti-slip additive if the area is a pool deck, steps or a sloped walk and you are using a gloss sealer. Fine polymer or aluminium oxide grit is stirred into the second coat and kept in suspension as you spray. It changes the texture slightly underfoot, which is the point.

Common Failures and Fixes

White Haze or Blush

Cause: moisture trapped under or in the sealer film. The usual triggers are sealing pavers that were not fully dry, sealing over uncured polymeric sand, rain or heavy dew before the sealer cured, or a coat thick enough that it skinned over before its own water or solvent escaped. Active efflorescence under the film looks similar.

Fix: on solvent-based acrylics, haze can often be cleared by re-wetting the area with xylene or a light coat of fresh solvent sealer, which re-dissolves the film and lets the trapped moisture escape. Sometimes warm, dry weather alone clears light blush over a week or two. On water-based sealers, haze generally does not reverse; the fix is a sealer stripper, rinse, dry fully and reapply thinly. Efflorescence trapped under a film also needs stripping.

Slippery When Wet

Cause: gloss film-forming sealers fill the paver's texture and leave a smooth surface, especially when applied heavily or in too many coats over the years.

Fix: next time, use fewer, thinner coats and add an anti-slip grit to the final coat. On an existing slick surface, a fresh thin coat with grit mixed in can help, provided it is compatible with what is there. For pool decks and steps, a natural-look penetrating sealer avoids the problem at the source.

Peeling or Flaking

Cause: film built too thick, often from resealing too often; sealer applied over dirt, efflorescence or a chalky surface; moisture pushing up from below; or an incompatible new coat over an old one.

Fix: loose film has to come off. Strip with a product matched to the sealer type, pressure wash, let everything dry, and start again with thin coats. Sanding or scraping individual patches rarely blends well on a film-forming finish. The best prevention is not resealing until the old coat has visibly worn; our guide to resealing intervals explains how to tell.

Lap Marks, Streaks and Bubbles

Cause: sealing in direct hot sun, working too large a section and losing the wet edge, or rolling too fast and whipping air into the film.

Fix: on solvent sealers, a thin additional coat usually melts lap marks and bubbles in. On water-based, a light second coat in better conditions often evens things out; severe streaking may need stripping. Next time, seal in the cooler part of the day and keep sections small.

Sealer on the Wrong Surfaces

Overspray on siding, glass, plants or cars is easiest to remove while fresh. Water-based sealer comes off wet with soap and water. Solvent sealer needs the appropriate solvent, used carefully, and may damage some plastics and car paint. Masking is cheaper than clean-up.

The Short Version

Wait 60–90 days on new concrete pavers and until no new efflorescence appears. Clean thoroughly, treat stains first, and use a buffered efflorescence remover rather than straight acid. Re-sand the joints and let polymeric sand cure and dry completely, at least 48 hours and longer in cool weather.

Pick a dry, mild window: pavers dry for 24–48 hours, 50–90°F, and no rain for the next 24. Choose water-based natural-look if you want the safest first job, solvent wet-look if you want maximum gloss and accept more care, and a joint-stabilizing sealer only with regular sand.

Spray and back-roll thin coats, pull out every puddle, and add the second coat when the label says. Then stay off it: a day for feet, two to three for cars. Almost every haze, peel or slick-surface complaint traces back to moisture or thickness, and both are under your control.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should I wait to seal new pavers?

At least 60–90 days for concrete pavers, and longer if they are still producing efflorescence, the white chalky bloom of salts that rises out of new concrete. Sealing over active efflorescence traps it under the film, where it shows as a permanent cloudy haze that only stripping can remove. Pavers laid in autumn or in wet climates can bloom well into the following year. The reliable test is to clean a section, let it dry for a few days and look: if white haze reappears, keep waiting. Natural stone and clay pavers do not effloresce the same way and can usually be sealed within a couple of weeks once the joints are cured and dry.

Should I use a sprayer or a roller to seal pavers?

Both. Spray the sealer with a low-pressure pump sprayer to get it down quickly and evenly, then immediately back-roll with a 3/8" nap roller to work it into the texture and pull out puddles. Spraying alone tends to leave pooling in low spots and paver chamfers, while rolling alone is slow and puts down too much. Use a solvent-resistant sprayer and roller cover if the sealer is solvent-based. Some water-based penetrating sealers are designed for spray-only application, so follow the label where it says otherwise.

Do I need to re-sand pavers before sealing?

If the joints are low, washed out or full of weeds, yes. Joints should be filled to about 1/8 inch below the paver surface before sealing, both to lock the pavers together and to stop water and weeds getting in. Pressure washing usually removes some sand anyway. Polymeric sand is the usual choice, and it must be fully cured and dry before sealing, which typically means at least 48 hours in warm, dry weather and up to a week in cool or humid conditions. The alternative is regular jointing sand followed by a joint-stabilizing sealer that binds the sand as it cures.

Why did my pavers turn white after sealing?

Almost always moisture. White haze, called blush, appears when water is trapped under or within the sealer film: pavers that were not fully dry, polymeric sand that had not cured, rain or dew before the sealer set, or a coat applied so thick it skinned over before it could release its own moisture. Efflorescence sealed under the film looks similar. On solvent-based acrylic sealers, re-wetting the area with xylene or a thin coat of fresh solvent sealer often clears it by letting the moisture escape. On water-based sealers, haze usually needs a stripper and a fresh, thin reapplication once everything is dry.

How many coats of sealer do pavers need?

Most film-forming and colour-enhancing sealers are designed for two thin coats: the first soaks in and seals the pores, the second builds even colour and sheen. Many natural-look penetrating sealers are single-coat products, and some water-based types are applied wet-on-wet, with the second coat going on within minutes of the first. Two thin coats always beat one heavy one. A thick coat is the main cause of haze, peeling and a slippery surface, so follow the label's coverage rate and measure out sealer per section rather than spraying until it looks wet.

How long after sealing pavers can I walk or drive on them?

Light foot traffic after roughly 4–24 hours, depending on the product and the weather. Furniture can go back after 24–48 hours, placed rather than dragged. Vehicles typically need 48–72 hours, and longer in cool weather, because hot tyres on soft film-forming sealer can mark it or lift patches. Full cure takes around a week, so avoid harsh cleaners, grills and standing water until then. Turn off any sprinkler zones that hit the pavers for at least 24–48 hours after the final coat.

Can I seal pavers in hot weather or direct sun?

You can, but not in the middle of the day. Most sealers want air and surface temperatures between about 50°F and 90°F. Pavers in full summer sun can be far hotter than the air, which makes sealer flash off before it levels, leaving lap marks, streaks and bubbles. Seal in the early morning or late afternoon when the surface is cool, or wait for a cloudy, dry day. Avoid evenings before a heavy dew, especially with solvent-based sealers, which are prone to blushing in humid conditions.

Is a water-based or solvent-based paver sealer better for DIY?

Water-based is the safer first choice. It is low-odour, cleans up with soap and water, tolerates slight moisture better and is less likely to blush. It comes in natural and enhanced finishes. Solvent-based acrylic gives the deepest wet look and gloss and has one real advantage: new coats re-dissolve old ones, so haze and scuffs are often fixable without stripping. It is less forgiving of damp pavers and humid days, smells strongly, needs a respirator, and is restricted in some states because of VOC rules. Whichever you choose, do not put one type over the other without testing compatibility first.
