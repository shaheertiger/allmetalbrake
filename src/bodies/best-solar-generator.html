Most solar generator shopping goes wrong in the same place: people compare batteries and treat the panels as an accessory. But a solar generator is a two-part system. The power station stores energy and turns it into household current; the panels decide how much of that energy comes back each day. A 4 kWh battery fed by a single 200 W folding panel is, for all practical purposes, a 4 kWh battery you can only refill from the wall. A 1 kWh battery with 400 W of well-aimed panels, by contrast, can be emptied every evening and full again by mid-afternoon, day after day, for as long as the weather holds.

So this guide works the other way round from our main portable power station guide. It starts with the charge side: how many watts a panel actually delivers, how many sun hours you can count on, what the station's charge controller will accept, and how to wire panels so they stay inside its voltage window on a frosty morning. Then it pairs seven widely sold power stations from EcoFlow, Jackery, Anker SOLIX and Bluetti with the amount of solar each one needs to be useful, from a 10-pound camping kit to a wheeled home-backup system feeding off a ground-mounted array.

☀️ The short answer: Pair a power station with roughly one watt of panel for every three watt-hours of battery you want to refill in a day, and never exceed the station's solar input voltage or wattage. For most households, that means a 1 kWh LFP station such as the EcoFlow Delta 3 Plus or Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 with 300–400 W of panels. For camping, an EcoFlow River 3 Plus with a single 100–200 W panel is plenty. For real home backup, an EcoFlow Delta Pro 3 or Anker SOLIX F3800 with 1,200–2,000 W of rigid panels on a ground mount is what turns a big battery into indefinite power.

Sun-Hours Math: What a Panel Really Delivers

A panel's rated wattage is measured under standard test conditions: strong, perpendicular light on a cool cell. Real panels in a real yard almost never see that. Heat, dust, imperfect aim, wiring losses and the charge controller all take a share, and portable panels typically deliver about 60–75% of their rating at the best part of the day.

The second number is peak sun hours: the day's sunlight expressed as the equivalent number of hours at full strength. A clear summer day across much of the U.S. gives four to five; winter in northern states can fall to two or three; the desert Southwest in summer runs higher. Multiply the two and you get the rule this guide uses throughout:

💡 Daily harvest ≈ panel watts × 0.6–0.75 × peak sun hours. At four to five sun hours, that works out to roughly 2.5–3.5 Wh per rated panel watt per day. A 200 W panel gives about 500–700 Wh; 400 W gives about 1.0–1.4 kWh; 1,600 W of rigid panels gives about 4–5.6 kWh.
Panel wattagePoor day (~2 sun hrs)Typical day (~4 sun hrs)Great day (~5 sun hrs)
100 W~120–150 Wh~240–300 Wh~300–375 Wh
200 W~240–300 Wh~480–600 Wh~600–750 Wh
400 W~480–600 Wh~960–1,200 Wh~1,200–1,500 Wh
800 W~1.0–1.2 kWh~1.9–2.4 kWh~2.4–3.0 kWh
1,600 W~1.9–2.4 kWh~3.8–4.8 kWh~4.8–6.0 kWh

Notice the left-hand column. A heavily overcast or short winter day can cut harvest in half or worse, and a stormy day that knocks the power out is rarely a good solar day. That is why we size arrays with a margin, and why every serious solar generator plan keeps wall charging, car charging or a gas generator as the fallback for a run of grey days.

Two practical caveats. First, a full refill from empty is not always the goal. If you only drain a 1 kWh station to about half each night, 200 W of panels keeps pace. Size the array to what you actually use per day, not to the battery label. Second, the station's solar input limit caps the useful array size. Panel wattage above that limit is not lost entirely, because it helps on hazy days and in the early morning, but at noon the charge controller will clip the extra. A modest oversize of 10–30% is common and sensible; doubling it is wasted money.

MPPT Input Limits and Voltage Windows

Every station on this list uses an MPPT (maximum power point tracking) charge controller. It continually adjusts the load on the panels to pull the most power from them as sunlight changes, which is noticeably more efficient than the older PWM controllers on cheap kits. But an MPPT input has three limits printed on its spec sheet, and all three matter.

Maximum input power (watts). The most the controller will turn into charge. Panels beyond this are clipped at midday.

Voltage window (volts). A minimum below which the controller will not start charging, and a maximum that must never be exceeded. Exceeding the maximum, even briefly, can damage the controller, and manufacturers generally treat over-voltage damage as misuse.

Maximum current (amps). Wiring panels in parallel adds their currents. If the total exceeds the input's current rating, the controller limits it and the extra panel adds little.

Large stations such as the Delta Pro 3 and SOLIX F3800 split their solar input across two separate ports, each with its own window. That lets you build two smaller strings rather than one long one, which is easier to keep within voltage limits and less vulnerable to shade. Read each port's limits individually; they are not always identical.

Series vs. Parallel Wiring

Once you have more than one panel, you have to decide how to connect them. In series, positive goes to negative and the voltages add while the current stays the same. In parallel, using a branch connector, positives join positives and the currents add while the voltage stays the same. Either way the total wattage is the same; what changes is how that power reaches the charge controller.

For most portable setups the rule is simple: use series when the combined voltage stays comfortably inside the window, including cold-weather headroom; otherwise use parallel, or a mix of both. Large stations with two inputs often work best with one series string per input. And avoid mixing very different panels in the same string; the weakest panel sets the pace for the rest.

Voc and Cold-Weather Voltage

The voltage number that matters for safety is Voc, open-circuit voltage: what a panel produces with nothing connected. It is always higher than the voltage under load, and it is printed on the panel's label. Crucially, Voc rises as the panel gets colder. Silicon panels typically gain somewhere around 0.3% of their voltage for every degree Celsius below the 25°C (77°F) test temperature.

Here is an illustrative example with round numbers. Suppose a station's input accepts up to 60 V, and you have panels rated at 22 V Voc. Two in series gives 44 V, which looks fine. Three in series gives 66 V, which is already over. Now consider a clear January morning at -10°C (14°F), 35 degrees below the test temperature. At 0.3% per degree, Voc climbs roughly 10%, so the two-panel string reads about 48–49 V. Still inside the window. But if your panels were rated at 27 V Voc, two in series would be 54 V on paper and close to 60 V on that cold morning, right at the limit, with no margin at all.

💡 Leave 15–20% headroom. Add up the Voc of the panels in a series string and keep the total at least 15–20% below the station's maximum input voltage if you will use the system in freezing weather. Cold, bright mornings after a snowfall, when the panels are cold and the light is reflected, are exactly when voltage peaks.

Cold also matters on the battery side. LFP cells should not be charged below freezing, and most stations block charging when the battery is too cold. Keep the station indoors or in an insulated space in winter and run the panel cable through a door seal or window gap, rather than leaving the whole system outside.

Portable vs. Rigid Panels

The panel type you choose should follow what the system is for, not what the bundle includes.

Folding panels are the right tool when the system moves: they pack flat, set up in minutes and plug straight into the station with the right adapter. Their drawbacks are price per watt, fragility in wind, and the fact that someone has to set them out and re-aim them. Rigid panels cost far less per watt, survive hail and snow, and can stay aimed permanently on a simple ground rack. For a 4 kWh home-backup station, a few rigid panels on a ground mount near the house will almost always outperform a pile of folding ones for the same money.

The catch with rigid panels is voltage. A full-size residential panel has a much higher Voc than a folding one, so two in series may already be near a mid-size station's input limit. Large stations with high-voltage inputs are designed for this; carry-size stations often are not. Check the numbers before you buy.

Angle, Tilt and Shading

Face south (in the Northern Hemisphere) and avoid anything that throws shadow between mid-morning and mid-afternoon. That window is where most of the day's energy arrives.

Tilt for the season. A reasonable rule is to tilt the panel from horizontal by roughly your latitude, then add about 15 degrees in winter when the sun is low and subtract about 15 degrees in summer when it is high. Folding panels with built-in kickstands usually offer only a few fixed angles, so pick the closest one. Lying a panel flat on the ground can cost a large share of its output in winter.

Re-aim if you can. With portable panels at a campsite, turning them to follow the sun two or three times a day noticeably increases harvest. A permanent ground mount set to a good fixed angle gives up a little peak output in exchange for never needing attention.

Treat shade as the enemy. A shadow across even a small part of one panel can cut its output sharply, because the cells in each section are wired in series. Bypass diodes limit the damage, but a branch shadow, a vent pipe or a neighbor's fence across the bottom row still costs more than its size suggests. If one panel in a series string will be shaded part of the day, consider wiring that panel in parallel, or moving it.

Keep panels cool and clean. Panels lose output as they heat up, so leave an air gap behind folding panels rather than laying them on hot pavement, and wipe off dust, pollen and bird droppings. Small habits add up to a meaningful difference over a week-long outage.

Bundle or Buy Separately?

Every major brand sells its power stations as "solar generator" bundles with its own panels. The appeal is real: matched connectors, a panel that is guaranteed to sit inside the station's input window, one warranty contact, and sometimes a discount over buying the parts individually.

The drawbacks are just as real. Bundles are often sized for a photo rather than a refill, with a single panel paired to a battery that needs two or three. Brand-name folding panels also tend to cost more per watt than comparable third-party panels. And for home backup, a bundle of folding panels is usually the wrong kind of panel altogether.

Our rule of thumb: buy a bundle for camping and RV systems where folding panels are what you want anyway, and check that the panel wattage roughly matches the battery using the table above. Buy separately for home backup, pairing the station with rigid panels, a ground mount and the right adapter cables, all checked against its input voltage, current and wattage. Home-Backup Solar Sizing: Worked Examples

The process is the same for every house. Add up the energy your essential loads use in a day, divide by 0.85 for inverter losses, then size the battery to cover the night and the array to put the day's use back. The runtime rule from our main guide still applies: hours ≈ Wh × 0.85 ÷ watts.

Example 1: Apartment essentials on a 1 kWh station

Refrigerator at about 1.2 kWh a day, router and modem at 20 W (about 480 Wh), LED lamps (150 Wh) and phone and laptop charging (100 Wh). That is about 1,930 Wh a day, or roughly 2,270 Wh drawn from the battery once inverter losses are included. A 1 kWh station alone lasts around ten to eleven hours. With 400 W of folding panels on a balcony or in a sunny window putting back about 1.0–1.4 kWh on a clear day, you can cover the daytime load as it happens and still put some charge back. Running the fridge in cycles rather than continuously closes the rest of the gap. This is a short-outage plan that stretches to several days in good weather.

Example 2: Suburban house, essentials on a 4 kWh system

Refrigerator (1.5 kWh), chest freezer (0.8 kWh), internet (0.5 kWh), lights (0.3 kWh), charging (0.1 kWh), and a gas furnace's blower running about 150 W for four hours a day in winter (0.6 kWh). Total about 3.8 kWh a day, or about 4.5 kWh from the battery. A Delta Pro 3 or SOLIX F3800 covers most of a full day on its own. To keep up indefinitely, the array must put back about 4.5 kWh a day: at roughly 3 Wh per rated watt, that is about 1,500 W of panels in summer, and closer to 2,000 W or more for the shorter days of winter. Four or five rigid panels on a ground mount, split into two strings across the station's two inputs, is the realistic shape of this system.

Example 3: Rural house with a 240 V well pump

Add a 240 V well pump to Example 2. Its daily energy may be modest if water use is careful, but its starting surge is large, which is why the 6,000 W, 120/240 V SOLIX F3800 or the Delta Pro 3's 240 V output matter here. The solar sizing barely changes; the inverter choice does. Schedule laundry and showers for the sunny middle of the day, when panels are carrying part of the load directly, and the battery saves its energy for the night.

Example 4: Medical device backup

A CPAP at 40 W for eight hours a night uses about 320 Wh, or about 375 Wh from the battery. A 1 kWh station covers two to three nights on its own, and even a single 200 W panel replaces one night's use on most clear days. For anyone who depends on a device, this is the cheapest and most reliable solar generator setup there is. Confirm the station's UPS switchover time against the device maker's guidance.

Most households wanting outage backup plus camping: EcoFlow Delta 3 Plus, Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 or Bluetti Elite 100 V2 with 300–400 W of panels. Buy the Jackery as a two-panel bundle, the Bluetti with third-party panels, and the EcoFlow if you might expand later.

RVs and longer outages in sunny regions: Jackery Explorer 2000 v2 with 400–600 W of folding panels.

Whole-home essentials and multi-day outages: EcoFlow Delta Pro 3 or Anker SOLIX F3800 with 1,200–2,000 W of rigid panels on a ground mount, connected to the house through a transfer switch by a licensed electrician. Choose the F3800 if a 240 V well pump or heavy motor loads are on the list.

Phones and lights only: you may not need a solar generator at all. A good power bank or a small station is enough, and our portable chargers for power outages guide covers those.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a solar generator?

A solar generator is a portable power station paired with solar panels. The power station contains a lithium battery, an inverter that produces household AC power, and an MPPT charge controller that turns panel output into stored energy. The panels are the fuel source. Nothing is burned, so a solar generator is silent and safe to use indoors, while the panels sit outside in the sun. The term is mostly marketing; the same stations can be charged from a wall outlet or a car socket. What makes one a good solar generator is how well its battery size, solar input limits and panel wattage are matched.

How many solar panels do I need for a solar generator?

Divide the battery capacity in watt-hours by about three to get the panel wattage that refills it on a typical sunny day, or by 2.5 for some margin. A 286 Wh camping unit needs about 100 W of panels, a 1 kWh station about 350–400 W, a 2 kWh station about 700–800 W, and a 4 kWh home-backup system about 1,300–1,650 W. If you only use part of the battery each day, you need proportionally less. Always stay within the station's maximum solar input wattage, voltage and current, because panel capacity above those limits is clipped at midday.

How long does it take to charge a solar generator with solar panels?

It depends on the panel wattage, the weather and the battery size. Portable panels deliver roughly 60–75% of their rating in good sun, and a clear day offers around four to five peak sun hours. So a 200 W panel puts back about 500–700 Wh a day, enough to refill a 286 Wh unit by midday or about half of a 1 kWh station. With 400 W, a 1 kWh station refills in one sunny day. Cloudy and winter days can cut harvest in half or more, so plan for slower charging when the weather turns.

Can I use any solar panel with my power station?

Usually yes, as long as three things fit. The panel's open-circuit voltage, or the total for panels wired in series, must stay below the station's maximum input voltage with headroom for cold weather. The current, or the total for panels in parallel, must not exceed the input's current rating. And you need the right connector or adapter, since brands use different plugs. Panel wattage above the station's input limit will not harm it but will be clipped. Check the station manual for its exact input range before connecting third-party panels.

Should I wire my panels in series or parallel?

Series adds voltage, parallel adds current, and total wattage is the same either way. Series is usually better when it fits inside the station's voltage window, because higher voltage reaches the charge controller's start threshold earlier in the morning and loses less power over long cables. Parallel is safer when series would exceed the maximum input voltage, and it copes better when one panel is partly shaded. Large stations with two solar inputs often work best with one short series string on each. Whichever you choose, keep the cold-weather voltage below the station's limit.

Can a solar generator power a house?

A large one can power the essential circuits of a house, and with enough panels it can keep doing so through a long outage. Systems such as the EcoFlow Delta Pro 3 and Anker SOLIX F3800 store roughly 4 kWh, output 4,000–6,000 W at 120/240 V, and accept large arrays. Connected through a transfer switch by an electrician and fed by 1,500–2,000 W of rigid panels, they can run a fridge, freezer, furnace blower, lights and internet indefinitely in good weather. Central air, electric heat and electric ranges use too much energy for a solar generator to sustain.

Do solar generators work in winter or on cloudy days?

Yes, but at reduced output. Heavy overcast can cut panel output to a fraction of its clear-day level, and winter days in northern states may offer only two or three peak sun hours, with the sun low in the sky. Cold itself does not hurt panels; they are slightly more efficient when cool, and their voltage rises, which is why you need voltage headroom. The battery is the sensitive part, since LFP cells should not charge below freezing. Keep the station indoors, tilt panels steeper for the low winter sun, clear snow off them, and size the array for winter if that is when outages hit.

Is a solar generator better than a gas generator?

Each wins in different situations. A solar generator is silent, produces no exhaust, can run indoors, needs no fuel storage and costs almost nothing to run once bought. It is ideal for apartments, medical devices, camping and short or sunny outages. A gas generator delivers more power for longer at a far lower upfront cost and keeps running through a week of storms as long as you have fuel. Many prepared households use both: the solar generator for quiet nights and daily essentials, the gas generator to recharge it during a long, cloudy outage.
