What Is Efflorescence on Basement Walls? Causes and Fixes

The white, chalky crust on a basement wall is salt left behind by water passing through the masonry. The salt is harmless. The water carrying it is the thing to deal with — and cleaning the wall without stopping that only buys a few months.

Efflorescence is the white, powdery or crusty deposit that appears on concrete, cinder block and brick basement walls. It is made of water-soluble salts — mostly calcium, sodium and potassium compounds — that dissolve in water inside the masonry, travel with it to the surface, and are left behind when that water evaporates into the basement air. The deposit itself is harmless to breathe and does not damage the wall in small amounts.

What it tells you matters more than what it is. Efflorescence only forms where water is moving through the wall, so a fresh bloom is a moisture map. You can brush it off in five minutes, but it will return until the water is reduced — usually by fixing gutters, downspouts and grading outside, and sometimes by sealing the wall from inside. The real risks are indirect: salts that crystallize behind paint push it off, and salts that crystallize just beneath the surface can flake the face of the block over time.

⚡ The quick answer: White, crystalline, crunchy and on bare masonry = efflorescence. It dissolves or darkens when sprayed with water, and older hardened deposits fizz with vinegar; mold does neither and is usually fuzzy, smudgy or colored. Remove it by dry brushing first, then a diluted vinegar or efflorescence cleaner if needed, rinse and let it dry. Stop it by cutting the water: gutters, downspouts extended 4–10 ft, soil sloping 6 in. over the first 10 ft, then an interior penetrating or crystalline sealer. Never paint over it with ordinary paint.

What Efflorescence Actually Is

Concrete, concrete block, mortar and brick all contain soluble salts. Cement produces calcium hydroxide as it cures, and sand, aggregate, mixing water and the soil outside the wall contribute sodium, potassium and magnesium sulfates and carbonates. While the wall is dry, those salts stay put. When water soaks into the masonry, it dissolves them and carries them along through the network of tiny capillary pores that every cementitious material has.

On a basement wall, the water usually enters from the soil side, where the wall is wet, and travels toward the room side, where the air is drier. When it reaches the interior face, it evaporates — and the salt it was carrying cannot evaporate with it. It crystallizes on the surface as a white film, a fuzzy-looking bloom of fine needles, or a hard crust, depending on which salts are involved and how long the process has been running.

It needs three things: soluble salts, water to move them, and a surface where that water evaporates. The salts are always there and the surface is your wall, so water is the only one you can realistically control.

Primary and Secondary Efflorescence

Primary efflorescence appears on new masonry in its first months, driven by the water used in mixing the concrete or mortar as it dries out. It typically fades after the first year or so as construction moisture leaves, and brushing it off periodically is often all it needs.

Secondary efflorescence appears on older walls because water from outside is getting in — rain, snowmelt, a saturated backfill, or a high water table. It keeps returning after cleaning and signals a moisture problem worth fixing. On a house more than a couple of years old, assume secondary.

Why It Changes Over Time

Fresh efflorescence is mostly soluble salt and wipes away easily. Left on the wall, its calcium hydroxide reacts with carbon dioxide in the air to form calcium carbonate — essentially limestone — which is no longer water-soluble and bonds as a hard scale. That is why old deposits need a mild acid to remove, and why early cleanup is far less work.

Hollow concrete block is the most prone to heavy efflorescence. The cores fill with water when the outside soil is saturated, giving the block a reservoir that keeps feeding moisture to the interior face for days after rain. Poured concrete walls are denser and tend to show efflorescence mainly at cracks, cold joints and form-tie holes rather than across the whole surface.

Timing tells you something too. Deposits that grow after heavy rain or spring thaw point to surface water — the cheapest kind to fix. Deposits present year-round suggest groundwater, which is a bigger job. If you also see damp patches or smell mustiness, start with the broader diagnosis in our guide on how to fix a damp basement.

Is It a Problem?

The salt itself, no. Efflorescence is not toxic, does not release spores, and is not a structural defect. A light dusting on an unfinished basement wall can be brushed off now and then indefinitely without consequence.

It becomes a problem in four ways.

It is evidence of water movement. A wall passing enough water to deposit visible salt is a damp wall, and a damp basement raises humidity, feeds mold on framing, drywall paper and stored boxes, and is unfit for finishing. Efflorescence often appears before any visible wetness.

It pushes off paint and coatings. When water carrying salt reaches the back of a paint film or a sealer, it cannot evaporate through it as freely, so the salt crystallizes right there, between the masonry and the coating. Salt crystals grow with real force. The result is the classic sequence on a painted block wall: bubbles, then flaking, then white powder behind every flake — the most common reason basement wall paint fails.

Subflorescence can damage the surface. When the evaporation point sits just beneath the face of the masonry rather than on it — common behind coatings, or where a wall dries very quickly — salts crystallize inside the pores. Repeated wetting and drying cycles grow those crystals until they break off the surface layer. This is spalling: a block or brick face that turns sandy or pops off in thin flakes. It takes years, but it is real material loss.

It can point to a more serious issue. Efflorescence along a horizontal crack, a stair-step crack in a block wall, or a wall that is visibly bowing inward is a sign of soil pressure as well as water. The efflorescence is not the concern there — the crack and the movement are, and they warrant a structural assessment before any cosmetic fix.

White mold on a basement wall exists and looks similar from across the room. Up close, the two are easy to tell apart.

The quickest checks: mist a patch with a spray bottle — fresh efflorescence darkens and seems to vanish within a minute or two, then returns as it dries. On anything that did not dissolve, put a few drops of white vinegar; fizzing means calcium carbonate, which is hardened efflorescence. Mold does neither, and smears when pinched where efflorescence feels gritty.

You can have both: dust, cobwebs or old paint on damp masonry can feed mold alongside efflorescence. If anything you find is fuzzy, colored or smells musty, treat it as mold — our guide to getting rid of mold in a basement covers cleanup and protection — and treat the moisture behind both as the same problem.

How to Remove Efflorescence

Work from the gentlest method upward and stop as soon as the wall is clean. Most efflorescence comes off with step one alone.

Step 1: Dry Brush and Vacuum

Scrub the dry wall with a stiff nylon or natural-bristle masonry brush, then vacuum the powder off the wall and floor with a shop vacuum. Wear a dust mask.

Do this dry. Wetting the wall first redissolves the salts and carries them back into the pores, where they reappear days later — you have just supplied the water efflorescence needs. Avoid steel wire brushes on brick or decorative block, since broken bristles rust and stain; brass bristles are safer if nylon is not enough.

Light blooms usually need nothing more. Note how quickly it returns — weeks means a lot of water is moving, months means relatively little.

Step 2: Diluted Vinegar or a Mild Cleaner

For deposits that will not brush off, apply white vinegar mixed roughly 1:1 with water, let it sit a few minutes, and scrub. Vinegar dissolves light carbonate scale slowly but safely. Rinse with a damp sponge — no more water than needed — and let the wall dry.

Commercial efflorescence cleaners are the next step up: buffered acid products, often sulfamic- or phosphoric-based, that dissolve mineral deposits with less risk of etching than straight acid. Follow the label dilution exactly.

Step 3: Diluted Muriatic Acid (Last Resort)

For thick crusts that survive both, diluted muriatic (hydrochloric) acid works — and is where most damage happens, so test an inconspicuous area first.

  • Dilute properly. Typical dilutions run from 1 part acid to 10–20 parts water. Start weak. Always add acid to water, never water to acid, which can spatter violently.
  • Pre-wet the wall with plain water just before applying. Saturated masonry keeps the acid at the surface instead of drawing it deep into the pores, which reduces etching and staining.
  • Short dwell time. Apply, let it work for a few minutes while it fizzes, scrub, and do not let it dry on the wall.
  • Rinse thoroughly with clean water, then neutralize with a solution of about one cup of baking soda per gallon of water, and rinse again.

Acid can lighten or etch the surface, especially colored mortar, leaving the cleaned patch looking different from the rest of the wall.

Safety for Acid Cleaning

An enclosed basement makes acid work more hazardous than outdoors. Wear splash goggles, rubber or nitrile gloves and long sleeves, and run a fan pushing air out a window. Muriatic fumes corrode metal, so keep acid away from the furnace, water heater, electrical panel and tools. Never mix acid cleaners with bleach or any chlorine product — the combination releases chlorine gas. Keep children and pets out until the area is rinsed and aired.

How to Stop Efflorescence Coming Back

Cleaning removes the symptom. Stopping it means reducing the water reaching the wall, and the order matters: outside first, because it is cheaper and because interior treatments work far better on a wall that is no longer being soaked from behind.

1. Gutters and Downspouts

Roof water dumped against the foundation is the most common source of basement wall moisture. A thousand square feet of roof sheds roughly 600 gallons in an inch of rain, and a clogged gutter or short downspout puts much of it into the backfill against your wall. Clean the gutters and extend every downspout to discharge at least 4 feet out — 10 feet on clay or poorly draining lots. Extensions cost $10–$30 each, the best-value fix in this guide.

2. Grading

Soil should fall away from the house — the residential code benchmark is 6 inches of drop within the first 10 feet. Backfill settles over decades until it slopes toward the wall. Regrade with compacted, clay-rich fill rather than mulch or loose topsoil, which let water soak straight down. Keep soil and mulch at least 6 inches below siding, and make sure window wells drain.

3. Seal Cracks and Form-Tie Holes

Where efflorescence follows a specific crack or tie-rod hole, seal that defect directly. Hairline to moderate cracks in poured walls are usually sealed with a polyurethane or epoxy crack-injection kit; small holes and joints in block walls take hydraulic cement, which expands as it sets and can be applied even against slight seepage. Clean the efflorescence off first so the repair bonds to sound masonry.

4. Interior Penetrating Sealers

Once outside water is under control, a penetrating sealer on the interior face reduces how much moisture passes through the wall. The type matters here.

Silicate densifiers (sodium, potassium or lithium silicate) soak into concrete and react chemically with the calcium hydroxide inside the pores to form a hard mineral gel that partially blocks them. Because they consume the very lime that forms most efflorescence and leave no surface film to peel, they are a particularly good match for this problem. They work best on poured concrete and on block with reasonable density.

Silane and siloxane water repellents line the pores with a water-repellent layer that still passes vapor. They excel above grade against rain, but on a basement wall with water pushing in from the soil they can shift the evaporation point just beneath the surface — the condition that causes subflorescence. Below grade, prefer silicates or crystalline products.

We compare specific products by type in our best basement wall sealer guide, and the block-specific options in cinder block sealer reviews. Every one of them needs bare, clean, efflorescence-free masonry to penetrate — a sealer applied over salt bonds to the salt.

5. Crystalline Waterproofing

Crystalline coatings are cement-based slurries with reactive chemicals that migrate into damp concrete and grow insoluble crystals inside the capillary pores. They suit efflorescence-prone basements because they work from the interior side against outside water pressure, and the crystal growth reactivates when new moisture arrives, sealing very fine cracks over time.

They are more involved than a roll-on sealer — the wall must be clean, pre-dampened and kept moist while the coating cures — and work best on poured concrete; on hollow block, results depend on block density and mortar condition.

6. Dehumidification — for the Room, Not the Wall

A dehumidifier lowers room humidity, protecting stored items and framing from mold. Around 45–50% relative humidity is the standard target — see our guide to what humidity to set a basement dehumidifier.

What it does not do is stop water moving through the wall. Drier air speeds evaporation from the wall face, so efflorescence may even appear a little faster at first — a reason to treat dehumidification as one part of the fix, not the fix. Our best basement dehumidifier guide covers sizing by square footage and dampness.

7. Drainage, If It Keeps Coming Back

If the outside work is done, the wall has been sealed, and heavy efflorescence still returns year-round, the water source is likely groundwater or saturated soil that surface fixes cannot reach. That is the point for an interior drain-tile system with a sump pump, or exterior excavation and membrane waterproofing. Both are professional jobs with professional prices, laid out in our basement waterproofing cost breakdown. What Not to Do

Do not paint over efflorescence with ordinary paint. Ordinary latex or oil paint on a wall still passing water traps salt behind the film. Within months it blisters and peels, and stripping failed paint is far more work than the original cleanup. For a painted finish, clean the wall, fix the water source, let it dry, and use a masonry waterproofing paint or breathable coating made for below-grade walls. Our guide to the best paint for cinder block explains which formulations tolerate damp masonry and which do not. Even the best of them will fail if the wall is wet enough to keep blooming heavily.

Do not pressure wash or hose the wall. Large amounts of water drive salts back into the masonry and feed the next round of efflorescence.

Do not use undiluted muriatic acid, or any acid on a wall you have not test-patched. Strong acid etches the surface, dissolves mortar, and can leave permanent discoloration — and in a closed basement the fumes are a genuine hazard.

Do not cover it up. Framing and drywalling over a wall that is still blooming traps moisture against studs and drywall, where mold grows unseen. Finish only after the wall stays clean through a full wet season.

Do not ignore the pattern. If efflorescence follows a horizontal or stair-step crack, or the wall shows any inward bowing, have it looked at by a structural engineer or foundation specialist before spending money on cleaning or sealing.

The Short Version

Efflorescence is salt left behind by water evaporating from your basement wall. It is not mold, not toxic, and not structural damage in itself. Confirm it with a spray of water or a drop of vinegar.

Brush it off dry. Use diluted vinegar or an efflorescence cleaner for stubborn deposits, and diluted muriatic acid only as a careful last resort.

Then cut the water: gutters, downspouts, grading, cracks. Once the wall stays drier, a silicate or crystalline interior sealer reduces what is left. Do not paint over it with ordinary paint, and if heavy deposits keep returning after all that, call a drainage contractor.

Frequently Asked Questions

What causes white powder on basement walls?

Almost always efflorescence: soluble salts from the concrete, mortar, block or surrounding soil that dissolve in water moving through the wall and are left behind when it evaporates. On new masonry it comes from construction moisture and fades within a year or so; on an older wall it means outside water — usually roof runoff or poor grading — is soaking the wall.

Is efflorescence harmful to your health?

No. It is mineral salt, not toxic, and releases no spores; a dust mask while brushing it off is simply sensible. The indirect concern is that the same damp conditions let mold grow on nearby wood, drywall and stored cardboard, so a musty smell alongside efflorescence is worth investigating.

How do I tell efflorescence from mold?

Efflorescence is white, crystalline and crunchy, sits on bare masonry, and crumbles to grit when pinched. Fresh deposits dissolve when sprayed with water; hardened ones fizz with vinegar. Mold is fuzzy or smudgy, often colored, smells musty, grows mainly on organic material, and reacts to neither test.

Does vinegar remove efflorescence?

Yes, for light to moderate deposits. Dry-brush first, then apply white vinegar diluted about 1:1 with water, wait a few minutes, scrub with a nylon brush, rinse lightly and let the wall dry. It is slower than commercial cleaners or muriatic acid but far less likely to etch or discolor the masonry.

Will efflorescence go away on its own?

Primary efflorescence on new concrete or block usually fades over the first year as construction moisture leaves. Secondary efflorescence on an older wall does not, because outside water keeps feeding it; it returns after every cleaning until gutters, grading, cracks and sealing reduce that water.

Can I paint over efflorescence?

Not with ordinary paint, and never without removing it first. Paint bonds to the salt rather than the wall, and new salt crystallizing behind the film pushes it off. Clean the wall, fix the water source, let it dry, then use a masonry waterproofing paint or breathable coating made for below-grade walls.

Does a dehumidifier stop efflorescence?

No. It lowers room humidity, which protects against mold and is worth doing at around 45–50% relative humidity, but it does not reduce water moving through the wall from outside. Drier air can even speed evaporation at the wall face. Fix the wall moisture at its source.

Is efflorescence a sign of foundation problems?

It signals a moisture problem, not by itself a structural one, and is usually fixed with inexpensive exterior drainage work. The exception is efflorescence following a horizontal or stair-step crack, or on a wall bowing inward — those warrant a structural engineer or foundation specialist before any cleaning or sealing.
