How to Strip Deck Stain: Step-by-Step DIY Guide

Stripping is a chemistry job first and a muscle job second. Pick the right stripper for the finish you have, keep it wet for its full dwell time, rinse gently, neutralize — and most of the old stain comes off without ever touching a sander.

To strip deck stain, wet down the surrounding plants and siding, roll or spray a sodium hydroxide deck stripper onto a dry deck in the shade, keep it wet for 10–20 minutes, scrub with a stiff nylon brush, and rinse with a garden hose or a pressure washer set to 500–1,200 PSI with a 40° fan tip held 8–12 inches off the wood. Follow immediately with an oxalic acid brightener to neutralize the caustic and restore the wood's color, rinse again, and let the deck dry for two to three days before you apply new stain.

That sequence removes most semi-transparent and transparent stains in one pass and loosens failing film stains enough to come off with a second. It does not reliably remove sound solid stain or paint — those need a heavier approach, covered further down. The rest of this guide explains each step, the numbers behind it, and the mistakes that turn a one-weekend job into furred, blotchy wood.

⚡ The process at a glance: Protect plants and siding → apply caustic stripper to dry wood in shade → 10–20 min dwell, never let it dry → scrub with the grain → rinse at 500–1,200 PSI (cedar and redwood at the low end), 40° tip, 8–12 in away → oxalic acid brightener while still wet → rinse → spot-sand at 60–80 grit → dry 48–72 hours (moisture below ~15%) → restain. Budget one day of work plus two to three days of drying.

When You Actually Need to Strip

Stripping is the most aggressive thing you can do to a deck short of replacing boards, so it is worth being sure you need it. Plenty of decks only need a cleaner and a fresh coat of the same product. Stripping is warranted in five situations.

Switching stain types or brands. Oil-based stain over water-based, water-based over oil, or one brand's formula over a different one that is not listed as compatible — these combinations often fail to bond, because the new stain cannot penetrate wood whose pores are already filled with something else. Most manufacturers say plainly on the label that existing finishes must be removed unless you are recoating with the same product. If you are moving to a new stain from our deck stain comparison, assume you need to strip unless its instructions say otherwise.

Peeling or flaking. If the old finish is lifting in patches, anything you put on top bonds only as well as the loose layer underneath. New stain over peeling stain peels again, usually within a season. Our guide to why deck stain peels covers the causes; the cure, in almost every case, is to take it back to bare wood.

Going from solid to semi-transparent, or dark to light. You can always go darker or more opaque over an existing finish. You cannot go lighter or more transparent without removing what is there, because a translucent stain shows everything underneath it, including the old color.

Heavy buildup. Penetrating stains are designed to soak in. After three or four maintenance coats on boards that were not fully weathered, the excess sits on the surface as a film, which then wears unevenly in traffic lanes and peels at the edges. Stripping resets the wood so the next coat can soak in properly.

Blotchy, uneven weathering. When the steps and traffic lanes are bare gray and the protected areas under furniture still hold dark stain, a new coat will show both. Stripping evens the base.

Two Quick Tests Before You Commit

The water test. Sprinkle water on the boards in several places. If it soaks in within a minute or so, the existing stain has largely worn away and a thorough cleaning may be enough. If it beads and sits, there is enough finish left that a new, different stain will struggle to penetrate.

The tape test. Press a strip of duct tape firmly onto a board, then rip it off. If flakes of stain come away on the tape, the finish has lost adhesion and must come off regardless of what you plan to put back on.

Choosing a Stripper

Deck strippers fall into three chemical families, and the most common mistake is using a cleaner when the job needs a stripper. Products labeled "deck cleaner" and "deck stripper" are not interchangeable, even when they come from the same brand and sit on the same shelf.

Sodium Hydroxide (Caustic) Strippers

This is the workhorse for most decks. Sodium hydroxide is strongly alkaline and attacks the oils and resins in the stain, softening the binder so the pigment releases from the wood. It works on both oil-based and water-based penetrating stains and on most semi-transparent and semi-solid products, especially once they are already failing. It typically comes as a liquid concentrate to dilute, or ready to use, and covers somewhere around 100–250 square feet per gallon depending on dilution and how thirsty the wood is.

The trade-offs are real. Caustic strippers darken wood — sometimes alarmingly, particularly on cedar and redwood, which can turn orange-brown or nearly black where tannins react. They raise and soften the surface fibers, which is why aggressive rinsing afterwards causes furring. And they leave the wood at a high pH, which interferes with new stain penetration and adhesion. All three are reasons the brightener step is not optional.

Oxygen (Percarbonate) Cleaners

Sodium percarbonate is a powder that releases hydrogen peroxide when dissolved in water. It is excellent at lifting gray, UV-damaged surface fibers, dirt and mildew, and it is far gentler on plants and on the wood itself than lye. On a deck where the old penetrating stain has mostly weathered away, an oxygen cleaner may be all you need — see our picks for the best deck cleaner for that scenario.

What it will not do is strip intact stain. If the water test shows beading, a percarbonate cleaner will brighten the deck and leave the finish in place. It is the right second product on a deck that only needs refreshing, not a substitute for a stripper.

Solvent Strippers

These are the gel and paste strippers sold for furniture and paint removal, reformulated in recent years around benzyl alcohol, NMP and ester blends since methylene chloride was withdrawn from consumer sale in the US. They are slower than the old formulas — dwell times of 30 minutes to several hours are common — and expensive per square foot, but their thick consistency clings to vertical surfaces and works through paint and solid stain that a caustic stripper only softens.

On a deck they are best kept for railings, spindles, posts and small areas of solid stain or paint. Using them across a 300-square-foot floor costs a lot, takes a long time and produces a large volume of sludge to dispose of.

Tools and Materials

  • Stripper — a caustic deck stain stripper for most jobs; a solvent gel for railings or painted spots. Buy about 20% more than the coverage math suggests; weathered wood soaks it up.
  • Oxalic acid brightener — liquid or powder, enough to cover the same area.
  • Pump sprayer — a plastic garden sprayer with a chemical-resistant seal, or a medium-nap roller and tray. Keep one sprayer for caustic and a separate one for acid.
  • Stiff nylon deck brush on an extension pole, plus a hand brush for edges and between balusters. Never wire brushes — steel leaves fragments that rust and stain the wood black, especially after oxalic acid.
  • Pressure washer with a 25° and 40° tip, or a garden hose with a strong nozzle. A pressure washer surface cleaner attachment rinses floor boards more evenly than a wand.
  • Plastic sheeting and breathable drop cloths, painter's tape.
  • Chemical splash goggles, nitrile or neoprene gloves, long sleeves, rubber boots.
  • Moisture meter — a basic pin-type meter is enough to confirm the deck is dry before restaining.
  • Random-orbit sander or floor buffer with 60–80 grit, for spot work afterwards.
💡 Pick the right day. Strip on an overcast day, or work the deck in sections that are in shade, with air temperatures roughly 50–85°F and no rain forecast for the following two or three days. Direct sun and wind dry the stripper before it finishes working, and a stripper that dries on the wood stops stripping and leaves a residue that is harder to rinse.

Step 1: Protect Plants, Siding and the House

Caustic stripper will scorch foliage, darken aluminum, etch glass and stain siding, and the rinse water carries it wherever it runs. Ten minutes of preparation saves a lot of regret.

Clear the deck completely. Furniture, planters, grills and rugs all come off. Sweep, then blow out the gaps between boards so debris does not trap stripper.

Soak the plants. Thoroughly water every bed, shrub and patch of lawn around and below the deck before you start. A plant whose leaves and soil are already saturated absorbs far less chemical from overspray and runoff. Then cover low plants with breathable cotton or canvas drop cloths. If you must use plastic, take it off as soon as you finish each section — clear plastic in sun can cook plants underneath faster than the stripper would harm them. Rewet the plants again after the final rinse.

Protect the house. Tape plastic over the bottom of siding where it meets the deck, over doors and windows that open onto it, and over light fixtures, outlet covers and any metal hardware. Aluminum thresholds, brass kickplates and painted trim are all vulnerable. Keep a hose running nearby so you can immediately rinse any splash on siding or glass; caustic overspray that is allowed to dry on painted surfaces can dull or discolor them.

Wet the deck boards themselves only if the label says so. Most caustic strippers go on dry wood so they soak into the finish, but a few instruct you to pre-wet. Follow the product.

Step 2: Apply the Stripper and Let It Dwell

Work in manageable sections of roughly 50–100 square feet — about what you can apply, scrub and rinse before any part dries. On a typical deck that means a run of three or four boards across the full length, so you never leave a lap line mid-board.

Apply generously with the pump sprayer or roller. You want the surface visibly wet, not misted. Do the railings and vertical surfaces first and work from the top down, so drips land on wood you have not yet treated rather than streaking a finished section. Then do the floor, starting at the side farthest from your exit.

Now leave it. Most caustic strippers specify a dwell time of 10–20 minutes, and that time is when the chemistry does the work. You should see the old stain change color, wrinkle or start to look muddy as it softens. Within the dwell period:

  • Never let it dry. Re-mist with more stripper, or lightly with water if the label allows, whenever any area begins to look dull or tacky. A dried patch stops working and is visibly lighter or darker than its neighbors after rinsing.
  • Do not exceed the label's maximum. Long dwell times on caustic products can over-soften the wood surface, deepen discoloration and increase fiber damage without removing more stain.
  • Test before you scrub. Rub a small spot with the brush. If the old color lifts easily and the wood underneath looks bare, it is ready. If it still resists after the full dwell, apply a second coat over the first rather than leaving one coat on longer.

Step 3: Scrub With the Grain

Once the stripper has dwelled, scrub every board with the stiff nylon brush, working along the grain, never across it. Cross-grain scrubbing roughs up the softened fibers and leaves visible scratches once the new stain goes on. The scrubbing is what actually releases the loosened stain from the wood's surface pores — skipping it and relying on the pressure washer to do that work is the main cause of furred, damaged decks.

Pay extra attention to the board ends, nail and screw heads, and the edges of boards where stain pools. Use the hand brush on stair treads, risers and between spindles. A brownish, sudsy slurry is normal; that is the old stain coming off.

If a section has started to dry by the time you get to it, re-wet it with stripper before scrubbing. Scrubbing dried stripper accomplishes little.

Step 4: Rinse at Low Pressure

A pressure washer is a rinsing tool here, not a stripping tool. The stripper and the brush have already loosened the finish; the water only needs to carry it away. Using high pressure to blast stain off is how decks end up with furry, splintered, grooved boards that look worse after stain than they did before.

Most consumer electric pressure washers are rated at 1,600–2,300 PSI and gas units at 2,700–3,300 PSI, so on a softwood deck you will usually be running below the machine's capacity. If your washer has no pressure adjustment, use the widest tip and stand farther back: pressure at the surface falls off quickly with distance. Start a test pass on a board that is out of sight, at 18 inches, and move in until the slurry rinses away without the wood surface changing texture.

Technique matters as much as pressure. Keep the wand moving and sweep along the length of the board, with the grain, overlapping each pass slightly. Feather in and out at the end of a stroke rather than stopping on the wood, which leaves a gouge. Rinse from the house side outward so you do not push slurry back onto finished areas, and finish each section by rinsing the gaps between boards.

On cedar and redwood in particular, a garden hose with a firm spray nozzle is often enough once the stripper and scrubbing have done their work, and it eliminates the risk entirely. Our guide to the best stain for a cedar deck explains why these soft, oily woods deserve the gentlest treatment at every step.

Step 5: Neutralize and Brighten With Oxalic Acid

A caustic stripper leaves the wood strongly alkaline, darkened and slightly gray-brown. An oxalic acid brightener does three things at once: it neutralizes the residual sodium hydroxide, it removes the tannin and iron stains the stripper caused or exposed, and it returns the wood toward its natural color. Skipping it is the most common reason a new stain looks blotchy, dries slowly or fails early.

Timing: apply the brightener immediately after the stripper rinse, while the wood is still wet. Do not let the deck dry between the two steps.

Application: mix powdered brightener with warm water per the label, or use a ready-to-use liquid. Apply with a clean sprayer — never one that still has caustic residue in it — until the wood is evenly wet. Most brighteners specify a dwell time of roughly 10–15 minutes, keeping the surface wet throughout. You should see the darkening fade and the wood lighten noticeably, sometimes within a minute or two.

Rinse: lightly scrub any areas that remain dark, then rinse thoroughly with low pressure or a hose. Oxalic acid that is left on the wood can crystallize as a white haze once it dries.

Citric-acid brighteners are a milder alternative and are fine after an oxygen cleaner. After a caustic stripper, oxalic acid does a better job on the dark tannin and iron discoloration. Oxalic acid is also the specific fix for black stains around old steel fasteners, which it removes where nothing else will.

💡 Don't mix the two chemicals. Strong alkalis and acids react with heat when combined. The stripper should be rinsed off before the brightener goes on, and the two must never share a sprayer or bucket. Keep them labeled and separate.

Step 6: Sand Stubborn Areas

After brightening and drying, walk the deck in good light. A well-stripped deck has an even, lightly raised texture with no obvious color left except in the grain. You will often find three kinds of problem spots.

Leftover stain. Usually on board edges, ends, under railings and in the areas that were most protected from the sun. If it is extensive, a second stripper pass is faster than sanding. If it is a few patches, sand them.

Raised fibers or "fuzz." Caustic strippers and rinsing raise the grain. A light pass with a random-orbit sander or a floor buffer fitted with a sanding screen knocks it down.

Splinters and rough board ends. Sand these smooth, especially on stair treads and handrails where hands and bare feet go.

Use 60–80 grit on deck boards. Going finer — 120 grit and above — can burnish the surface and close the pores, so penetrating stain cannot soak in evenly. That is one of the less obvious causes of a new stain coat peeling. Avoid drum sanders unless you have used one before; they gouge softwood deck boards instantly when the drum pauses. Sweep and blow off all dust before staining, and wipe railings with a dry cloth.

Sanding is for dry wood only. Sanding damp, freshly stripped boards clogs paper and tears fibers rather than cutting them.

Step 7: Let It Dry Before Restaining

Stripping, rinsing and brightening put a lot of water into the wood. Stain applied to damp boards cannot penetrate, sits on the surface, and is likely to peel or blotch. Allow at least 48–72 hours of dry weather after the final rinse; in cool, humid or shaded conditions it can take longer.

Rather than guessing, measure. Push a pin-type moisture meter into several boards, including some in the shadiest part of the deck. Most stain manufacturers want readings below roughly 12–15% before application, and some water-based formulas tolerate slightly higher. A water drop test is a rough check: if water sprinkled on the boards soaks in quickly and the wood looks uniformly light, it is usually ready.

Once it is dry, move on to restaining within a week or two if you can. Bare wood begins to gray from UV exposure within weeks, and that thin layer of degraded fibers weakens the bond of the new stain. If more than a few weeks pass, a light percarbonate cleaning before staining will help. Removing Solid Stain and Paint

Solid stain behaves like thin paint: it forms a film on the surface rather than soaking in. Where it is peeling, a caustic stripper and scrubbing will remove the loose material, but where it is still firmly bonded, it often takes multiple stripper passes and still leaves pigment in the grain. Paint is harder again. You have four realistic options.

1. Heavy-duty stripper, several passes. Some caustic strippers are marketed specifically for solid stains, with a thicker consistency and longer dwell. Expect two or three applications, and expect pigment to remain in the grain on softwoods. It works best when the coating is already failing.

2. Solvent gel on railings and details. Apply thickly, cover with plastic if the label suggests it to slow evaporation, wait the full dwell, then scrape with a plastic scraper and scrub. This is slow but precise and the right approach for spindles and posts.

3. Sanding. On deck floors, sanding with a floor buffer or orbital sander at 36–60 grit removes solid stain completely but takes a long time and eats into the wood. It is practical on a small deck, exhausting on a large one. Change discs often — film coatings clog paper fast.

4. Flip or replace boards, or stay opaque. On a deck held down with screws, flipping deck boards exposes a clean underside. Badly worn boards are often cheaper to replace than to strip. And if the goal is simply a fresh look, recoating with another solid stain or a deck resurfacer over a cleaned, scuff-sanded surface is far easier than stripping to bare wood, provided the existing coating is still bonded.

Lead paint warning: if the home was built before 1978 and the deck or railings have been painted, test for lead before sanding, scraping or power washing. Inexpensive test kits are sold at hardware stores. Lead paint should be handled by a certified contractor or under lead-safe work practices, not sanded or blasted into the yard.

Safety: Eyes, Skin and Lungs

Deck strippers are among the most hazardous products a homeowner uses, and the eyes are the first priority. Sodium hydroxide causes severe chemical burns to the eye within seconds, and alkaline burns are particularly damaging because the chemical keeps penetrating tissue after contact.

  • Eyes: wear sealed chemical splash goggles, not open-sided safety glasses. Overspray, brush flick-back and rinse spray all reach the face. Keep a hose with clean water running nearby. If anything gets in an eye, flush continuously with water for at least 15 minutes, holding the eyelid open, and seek medical attention.
  • Skin: nitrile or neoprene gloves (not thin latex), long sleeves, long trousers and rubber boots. Caustic on skin feels slippery and may not burn immediately, which is why people leave it too long. Rinse with plenty of water as soon as you notice it.
  • Lungs: spraying creates a fine mist. Stand upwind, use a coarse spray rather than a fine one, and wear a respirator rated for the product if the label calls for it — solvent strippers in particular should be used with good ventilation and organic-vapor protection.
  • Slips: a deck coated in stripper slurry is extremely slippery. Work backwards toward your exit, wear boots with grip, and take care on stairs.
  • Children and pets: keep them indoors and away until everything is rinsed and dry. Dogs walking through stripper then licking their paws is a common call to animal poison lines.
  • Oxalic acid: it is toxic if swallowed and irritating to skin and eyes. The same goggles and gloves apply.

Cleanup and Disposal

Rinse water from a stripping job carries diluted caustic, acid and the old stain itself. Do not direct it into a street drain or storm sewer: those run untreated to streams and lakes in most areas, and many municipalities prohibit it. Instead, direct runoff onto a lawn or gravel area away from vegetable gardens and wells, where soil can absorb and buffer it, or contain it with plastic sheeting under the deck if regulations in your area require it. Check with your local public works or environmental office if you are unsure.

Leftover concentrate should stay in its original, labeled container. Do not pour it down a household drain. Take it to a household hazardous waste collection site or event. Solvent strippers in particular must be handled this way.

Scrapings and sludge from solvent stripping should be allowed to dry out completely in a well-ventilated area, away from ignition sources, then bagged and disposed of according to local rules — usually household hazardous waste.

Rags soaked with any oil-based stain residue can spontaneously combust as they dry. Lay them flat outdoors to dry, or store them in a sealed metal container filled with water, before disposal.

Finally, rinse the sprayers, brushes and buckets thoroughly, and give the surrounding plants one more generous watering.

The Short Version

For most decks with a semi-transparent or transparent finish, a caustic stripper, a stiff brush, a gentle rinse and an oxalic acid brightener will take the wood back to bare in a single day. The order matters more than the brand: keep the stripper wet for its full dwell, let the brush do the work, use the pressure washer only to rinse, and never skip the brightener.

Expect to spend one day on the job and to wait two to three dry days afterwards, verified with a moisture meter, before restaining. Use a percarbonate cleaner instead of a stripper only when the old finish is already mostly gone. For solid stain or paint that is still firmly attached, weigh stripping against sanding, flipping boards or recoating opaque — and test for lead first if the house predates 1978.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best way to strip deck stain?

For most semi-transparent and transparent stains, a sodium hydroxide deck stripper is the most effective method. Protect plants and siding, apply the stripper to dry wood in shade, keep it wet for the label's dwell time (typically 10–20 minutes), scrub along the grain with a stiff nylon brush, and rinse at low pressure — 500–1,200 PSI on softwoods with a 40° tip held 8–12 inches away. Then apply an oxalic acid brightener while the wood is still wet to neutralize the caustic and restore color, rinse again, and allow two to three days to dry before restaining. Solid stain and paint usually require multiple passes, solvent gels or sanding.

Can I strip a deck with just a pressure washer?

Not without damaging it. Removing intact stain with water alone requires pressure high enough to tear the surface fibers of softwood decking, leaving it furry, splintered and grooved — and the damaged surface then absorbs new stain unevenly. Let a chemical stripper and a scrub brush release the stain, and use the pressure washer only to rinse it away, at 500–800 PSI on cedar and redwood or up to about 1,200 PSI on pressure-treated pine. If your washer has no pressure control, use a 40° tip and stand farther back, or rinse with a garden hose instead.

Do I need to use a brightener after stripping a deck?

Yes, after any caustic stripper. Sodium hydroxide leaves the wood strongly alkaline and often darkened, especially on cedar and redwood, where tannins react and can turn the wood dark brown. An oxalic acid brightener neutralizes the residue, removes tannin and iron stains, and returns the wood toward its natural color. Without it, the new stain may penetrate poorly, dry unevenly or look blotchy. Apply it immediately after rinsing off the stripper, while the wood is still wet, leave it 10–15 minutes and rinse thoroughly. After a mild oxygen cleaner it is optional but still recommended.

How long should I wait to stain after stripping a deck?

At least 48–72 hours of dry weather after the final rinse, and longer in cool, humid or shady conditions. The reliable check is a moisture meter: most stain manufacturers want readings below roughly 12–15%. A quick field check is the sprinkle test — if water soaks in immediately and the wood looks evenly light, it is usually dry enough. Do not wait too long, either. Bare wood starts graying from UV within a few weeks, and if more than two or three weeks pass, a light cleaning before staining helps the new coat bond properly.

What is the difference between a deck cleaner and a deck stripper?

A cleaner removes dirt, mildew and gray, weathered wood fibers; a stripper removes the stain itself. Most deck cleaners are based on sodium percarbonate (oxygen bleach) and are relatively gentle. Strippers are usually based on sodium hydroxide, which breaks down the oils and resins in the stain's binder. If the old stain is mostly worn away and water soaks into the wood, a cleaner may be enough before recoating. If water beads, or you are changing stain types or removing peeling finish, you need a stripper. The two are not interchangeable, even when sold side by side.

Will deck stripper kill my plants?

It can scorch foliage and harm plants if they receive direct overspray or concentrated runoff, but damage is usually preventable. Water all plants, beds and lawn around and below the deck thoroughly before you start, so leaves and soil are saturated and absorb less chemical. Cover low plants with breathable drop cloths rather than plastic in direct sun, which can overheat them. Rinse any overspray off foliage immediately, and water everything generously again after the final rinse to dilute any residue. Oxygen-based cleaners are considerably gentler on surrounding plants than caustic strippers.

How do I remove solid stain from a deck?

Solid stain forms a surface film, so it is much harder to strip than semi-transparent stain. Where it is peeling, a heavy-duty caustic stripper applied in two or three passes, with thorough scrubbing, removes most of it, though pigment often remains in the grain. Solvent gel strippers work well on railings and spindles. On deck floors, sanding at 36–60 grit removes it completely but is slow. Often the more practical options are flipping or replacing the worst boards, or recoating with another solid stain over a cleaned and scuff-sanded surface. Test for lead before sanding any painted deck on a pre-1978 home.

What safety gear do I need to strip a deck?

Sealed chemical splash goggles are the most important item, because sodium hydroxide can cause severe eye injury within seconds and ordinary safety glasses leave gaps for overspray and splash. Add nitrile or neoprene gloves, long sleeves and trousers, and rubber boots, plus a respirator suitable for the product if the label calls for one, particularly with solvent strippers. Keep a hose with clean water running nearby: if stripper reaches an eye, flush continuously for at least 15 minutes and get medical help. Keep children and pets off the deck until it is fully rinsed and dry.
