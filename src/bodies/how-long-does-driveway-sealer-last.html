How Long Does Driveway Sealer Last?

Two to three years for a typical asphalt sealcoat, five to ten for a penetrating concrete sealer, one to three for an acrylic film — and almost never the number printed on the pail. Here is what really sets the lifespan, and how to stretch it.

A typical asphalt driveway sealcoat lasts two to three years before it is worn through in the tyre tracks. A premium polymer-modified or professionally applied commercial-grade sealcoat can reach three to five years on a lightly used, partly shaded driveway. Concrete is a different story: a penetrating silane or siloxane sealer keeps working for five to ten years, while an acrylic film sealer on concrete lasts one to three.

Those figures assume two thin coats on a clean, dry, crack-filled surface, applied in warm weather. Change any of that and the number drops — sometimes to a single season. And the "5-year", "7-year" or "10-year" rating printed on many retail pails is not a lifespan at all. It is a product tier, closer to a grade than a measurement, and it describes a best case few driveways ever see.

⚡ The direct answer: Asphalt sealcoat — 2–3 years typical; 3–5 years for premium or pro-grade product on a sheltered, lightly used driveway; as little as 1 year for a thin budget coat. Concrete, penetrating silane/siloxane — 5–10 years. Concrete, acrylic film — 1–3 years. Pavers — 3–5 years. Label ratings like "10-year" are marketing tiers, not service life. The biggest life-shorteners are turning tyres, full sun, snowploughs, a single thin coat, poor cleaning and sealing in cold weather.

Lifespan by Sealer Type

The single most useful thing to know about your sealer is what kind it is, because chemistry sets the ceiling and conditions decide how close you get to it. The "short end" column is a driveway in full sun with daily turning traffic and a snowplough in winter; the "long end" is a shaded single-car driveway with straight-in, straight-out use.

How a Sealcoat Actually Wears Out

"Lasts three years" makes it sound as though the sealer is intact on day 1,094 and gone on day 1,096. That is not how it works. A sealcoat is a sacrificial layer: it takes the UV, rain and tyre abrasion so the asphalt binder underneath does not, and it is being consumed from the day it cures. What changes over its life is where it is still present.

The first few weeks. A fresh coat is soft for longer than most people expect. It is dry to walk on within hours and usually drivable after 24–48 hours, but full cure takes weeks in mild weather. Power steering a parked car — turning the wheels while stationary — can tear a scuff into the surface during this period, and it is the most common cause of early marks that never fully disappear.

Year one. The surface dulls from jet black to deep charcoal. On a well-applied job, little else happens apart from wear starting where cars turn.

Year two. The tyre tracks begin to lighten. The aggregate stones in those tracks start to show their own grey-brown colour where the film has worn off their tops. The edges, the apron by the garage and the strip by the lawn still look dark.

Year three. On a typical driveway, the traffic lanes are now clearly grey and water soaks into them rather than beading. The untrafficked areas may still be protected. This is the end of the sealer's useful life on the part of the driveway that matters, even though half the surface still looks sealed.

That uneven pattern is the most important thing to understand about sealer lifespan. A driveway does not have one number — the turnaround might get eighteen months while the edges get five years. That is why sealing only the worn lanes in some years is a perfectly sound approach, and why recoating the whole driveway on a fixed schedule builds up a thick, crack-prone film along the edges.

Why a "10-Year" Pail Doesn't Last 10 Years

Many sealer lines are sold in tiers labelled with a number of years. It is natural to read "10-year" as "lasts ten years". It almost never means that.

It is a grade, not a measurement. The number differentiates products within one brand's range: the higher tier typically carries more solids, more filler, more polymer or a rubberised additive, and costs more. That makes it genuinely better than the bottom tier. It does not make it a measured service life under real driveway conditions, and different brands' "years" are not comparable with one another.

It assumes ideal conditions. Read the small print and the claim is usually tied to two coats, at a specified coverage rate, over a clean, primed, crack-filled surface, applied within the right temperature window. Most driveways get one coat stretched further than the label says, over a surface that was hosed rather than scrubbed.

It does not account for traffic. Sealer life is overwhelmingly a function of how many tyres cross the surface and how they turn. A label cannot know whether you have one car or four, a straight driveway or a turnaround.

Any warranty is narrow. Where a product carries a warranty at all, it typically covers replacement product or a refund of the purchase price — not labour — and requires proof that the label directions were followed exactly. It is not a promise that your driveway will look sealed in year eight.

A sensible way to read the tiers: assume a premium pail will give you roughly the top of the realistic range — three to five years on a well-prepared, moderately used driveway — and a budget pail the bottom, one to two. That gap is real and usually worth paying for. The number on the lid is not. Our asphalt driveway sealer reviews compare the main products by solids content, filler and coverage, which tell you far more than the years printed on the front.

💡 Check the coverage rate, not the years. A pail that claims to cover 400–500 square feet per coat is depositing a thinner film than one that claims 250–300. Thin film is the single biggest reason a sealcoat wears through early. When two products are priced similarly, the one with the lower coverage claim per pail is usually putting more material on the driveway.

What Shortens Sealer Life

Most early sealer failures trace back to one or two of the factors below. Some you cannot change, like sun exposure; most you can.

Traffic Volume

Every tyre crossing the driveway removes a little sealer. One car is light use; three or four cars plus a trailer is several times the wear, and shows up as tracks worn grey in eighteen months to two years.

Turning Tyres

A rolling tyre is relatively gentle; a tyre being turned while barely moving scrubs the surface like sandpaper. Turnarounds, the bend where the driveway meets the garage, and the spot where you swing in from the street wear two or three times faster than straight runs. Turning the steering wheel while stationary — dry steering — is the worst of all, especially in hot weather when the sealer is softer.

Sun and Heat

UV breaks down the asphalt in an emulsion sealer just as it oxidises the pavement, and heat softens the film so tyres scuff it more easily. A south-facing driveway in a hot climate sits at the short end of every range; a shaded one at the long end.

Snowploughs, Shovels and Snowblowers

A plough blade or a steel-edged shovel dragged across the surface strips sealer in a single pass, and snowblower scraper bars do the same in a narrower strip. In snow country, winter clearing can remove more sealer than a full year of summer traffic. Raising the plough shoes or snowblower skids slightly, and using shovels with a plastic edge, makes a real difference.

De-Icing Salt

On asphalt, rock salt does little chemical harm to the sealcoat itself; the damage comes from the extra freeze-thaw cycling meltwater causes and the grit tracked over the surface. On concrete it is far more serious. Salt brine soaks into unprotected concrete and accelerates scaling, and calcium and magnesium chloride products can attack the cement paste directly. A worn concrete sealer in a salted climate can lose years of effective life in a single hard winter.

A Single Thin Coat

The most common shortcut and the most costly. One coat spread to save a pail leaves a film that is too thin to survive two years of traffic, and it misses the low spots and texture of the surface that a second coat fills. Two thin coats, the second applied after the first has dried, routinely outlast one heavy coat and one light one — and roughly double the life of a single thin coat.

Poor Preparation

Sealer applied over dust, loose grit, moss, oil or tree sap bonds to the dirt instead of the pavement. It looks fine for a few weeks and then wears or flakes off far faster than it should. Oil spots are the classic example: sealer over untreated oil never bonds, and those patches are the first to lift. Unfilled cracks let water under the coat at every edge, undermining it from below.

Sealing in Cold or Damp Weather

Asphalt-emulsion sealers cure by water evaporation and need both air and surface temperatures of at least 50°F, rising through the day and not dropping near freezing overnight, along with a dry forecast for 24–48 hours. Sealer applied on a cool autumn afternoon may never cure properly: it stays soft, tracks onto shoes and tyres, and wears away within a season. How to Make Sealer Last Longer

Every one of these is cheap compared with resealing a year early, and together they are the difference between the bottom and the top of the ranges in the table above.

Fill cracks first. Clean out and fill every crack wider than about an eighth of an inch with a dedicated crack filler before sealing, and let it cure per the label. Sealer alone does not bridge cracks; it simply skins over them and splits as soon as the pavement moves. Our asphalt crack filler guide covers the products for narrow and wide cracks.

Clean properly, not just a quick hose-down. Sweep off loose material, trim overhanging grass, scrub with a driveway cleaner and stiff brush, rinse, and let it dry completely.

Treat and prime oil spots. Degrease stains and apply an oil-spot primer before sealing. These are the patches that fail first on almost every driveway, and a few dollars of primer prevents it.

Apply two thin coats. Use the label's coverage rate as a ceiling, not a target, and apply the second coat after the first is dry to the touch and no longer tacky — commonly four to eight hours in warm weather. Pull the squeegee in one consistent direction and avoid puddles, which cure slowly and crack.

Cut in the edges by hand. Brush the sealer along the garage slab, the street apron and the lawn edge first, then squeegee the field. Edges that are missed or starved of material wear and ravel first, and water gets under the sealer from the side. Our step-by-step guide on how to seal an asphalt driveway walks through the order of work.

Give it time to cure. Keep cars off for at least 24–48 hours and avoid dry steering for the first few weeks, particularly on hot afternoons. Park slightly differently from day to day if you can, so the wear is spread rather than concentrated in two narrow tracks.

Touch up the traffic lanes. When the tyre tracks wear grey before the rest of the driveway, seal just those lanes and the turnaround. That restores protection where it is gone without building up film where it is not, and it can add a year or two before a full recoat is needed.

Adjust winter clearing. Raise plough and snowblower skids slightly and use plastic-edged shovels.

Signs the Sealer Is Worn Out

The calendar only tells you when sealer might be gone. These tell you it is — and, just as importantly, whether you are looking at normal wear or a failure that needs a different fix.

Water soaks in instead of beading. Splash a cup of water on the tyre tracks. On protected asphalt or sealed concrete it sits on top or beads for a while; on a worn surface it darkens the pavement and disappears within a minute. This is the most reliable test for every sealer type, and the only reliable test for penetrating concrete sealers, which do not change the look of the surface.

Grey tyre tracks against dark edges. On asphalt, a clear contrast between worn grey lanes and still-dark edges means the sealcoat has done its job and is finished where it matters.

Exposed aggregate. When the tops of the stones in the traffic lanes show their natural colour, the film over them is gone.

Loss of colour or sheen on concrete. An acrylic film that once darkened the concrete or gave it a satin sheen will fade back to raw concrete as it wears, usually in the traffic lanes first.

Worn vs. Failed

Wear is gradual and follows the traffic. Failure looks different: sealer peeling off in sheets or flakes, patches lifting across untrafficked areas, a fine crazed network of cracks in the film itself, or a white, cloudy haze under a concrete sealer. Those point to a bond or build-up problem — over-sealing, sealing over dirt or damp, or incompatible products — rather than a sealer that has simply reached the end of its life. Resealing over a failing coat makes it worse; the loose material has to come off first. Our guide to driveway sealer peeling covers the causes and the fix.

Concrete Sealers: Why the Range Is So Wide

"Five to ten years" and "one to three years" are both honest answers for concrete, because the two families of sealer work in completely different ways.

Penetrating silane and siloxane sealers soak into the concrete and react inside the pores, leaving a water-repellent zone a few millimetres deep with nothing on the surface. Tyres have nothing to wear off, so protection lasts five to ten years, with high-solids silanes at the longer end — but there is no visual cue when it is gone, so use the water test.

Acrylic film sealers sit on top of the concrete, which is what gives them the darker "wet look" or satin finish. That film takes UV and tyre abrasion just as an asphalt sealcoat does, and wears in one to three years — solvent-based acrylics toward the longer end, water-based toward the shorter. Choose acrylic for the look, not the longevity.

Concrete sealer lifespan has enough nuance — including how densifiers, epoxies and urethanes behave and why most belong in garages rather than on driveways — that it has its own guide: how long concrete sealer lasts.

Lifespan vs. Resealing Interval

How long a sealer lasts and how often you should reseal are related but not the same question, and confusing them causes most over-sealing.

Lifespan is how long the sealer protects the part of the driveway that takes the most wear. Your resealing interval should be set by that, and only when the traffic lanes are actually worn. Because the edges and untrafficked areas outlast the lanes by years, resealing the whole surface every time the lanes wear builds up a thick film along the edges. That stacked film is less flexible than the pavement, cracks in freeze-thaw, and eventually peels.

In practice, a driveway whose lanes wear through in two years might get a lanes-only touch-up at year two and a full recoat at year three or four. The Short Version

Expect two to three years from a standard asphalt sealcoat, three to five from a premium or professional product on a driveway with moderate traffic and some shade, and one to two from a budget pail or a single thin coat. On concrete, expect five to ten years from a penetrating silane or siloxane and one to three from an acrylic film.

Ignore the years on the label; compare solids, filler and coverage rate instead. Fill cracks, clean properly, prime oil spots, apply two thin coats in warm, dry weather, cut in the edges and keep cars off for two days. Those steps decide whether you land at the top or the bottom of the range far more than the brand does.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does driveway sealer last on asphalt?

Two to three years for a typical asphalt-emulsion sealcoat applied in two thin coats over a clean, crack-filled surface. Premium polymer-modified and professional commercial-grade sealcoat can reach three to five years on a lightly used, partly shaded driveway. A budget formula or a single thin coat may last one to two. Tyre tracks and turnarounds wear through first; edges can stay protected for years longer.

Does a "10-year" driveway sealer really last 10 years?

No. Year ratings on retail sealer pails are product tiers within a brand's range rather than measured service life. A higher tier usually has more solids, filler or polymer and genuinely performs better, but the number assumes ideal application and cannot account for your traffic. Any warranty typically covers only replacement product or a refund. Expect three to five years from a premium asphalt sealer.

How long does concrete driveway sealer last?

It depends on the type. Penetrating silane and siloxane sealers soak into the concrete and leave nothing on the surface to wear off, so they last five to ten years, with high-solids silane products at the long end. Acrylic film sealers, which give concrete a darker or glossy look, sit on top and wear like any coating: two to three years for solvent-based acrylic and one to two for water-based. Use the water-beading test to check a penetrating sealer, since it does not change the concrete's appearance.

What makes driveway sealer wear off so fast?

Usually a combination of thin application and turning traffic. A single coat spread further than the label recommends leaves too little material to survive, and tyres turning at low speed — at a turnaround, the garage bend or when steering a stationary car — scrub it off quickly. Full sun, snowploughs, poor cleaning and sealing below about 50°F all take years off as well.

How can I make my driveway sealer last longer?

Fill cracks first, clean thoroughly with a scrub brush and driveway cleaner rather than just a hose, and prime oil stains so sealer bonds there. Apply two thin coats, brush in the edges, and seal only at 50°F or above with a dry forecast. Keep cars off for 24–48 hours, raise plough skids in winter, and touch up worn traffic lanes between full coats.

How do I know if my driveway sealer has worn off?

Splash water on the tyre tracks. If it beads or sits on top, the sealer is still working; if it darkens the surface and soaks in within a minute, it has worn through. On asphalt the tracks also fade to grey against dark edges. Peeling or flaking sheets are a different problem — a bond failure, not normal wear.

How long does driveway sealer last before it needs a second coat?

If you mean the second coat of the same job, it goes on as soon as the first is dry to the touch and no longer tacky — typically four to eight hours in warm, sunny weather, longer when it is cool or humid. If you mean resealing, the next full coat is due when the traffic lanes have worn grey and soak up water, typically after two to three years on asphalt.
