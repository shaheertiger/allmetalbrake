If you've walked the driveway sealer aisle at a big-box home improvement store, you've very likely seen Latex-ite pails stacked next to the other big consumer brands. Latex-ite is one of the most recognizable names in DIY asphalt maintenance, and its two core sealers — the Optimum and the Airport Grade — show up constantly in homeowner questions. The question we hear most is simple: are Latex-ite sealers actually good, or are they selling on shelf presence rather than product merit?

This review answers that question through research rather than hands-on trials. We did not run our own field test of these products. Instead, we've worked from the manufacturers' published product descriptions and label directions, the general chemistry of asphalt-emulsion sealers, and widely accepted pavement-maintenance practice to explain what each product is, what its label claims, what its formulation type usually means for durability, dry time and climate suitability, and which kind of homeowner it suits. Where a specification varies by batch, retailer or pail size, we say so and tell you to check the label — the label on the pail you actually buy is always the final word.

What Is Latex-ite?

Latex-ite is a long-established American brand of asphalt pavement maintenance products: driveway sealers, crack fillers and patching materials aimed mainly at homeowners and sold through home improvement retailers and online. The brand has changed corporate hands over the years, so the company name printed on a pail can differ depending on when it was produced, but the product names homeowners shop for — Optimum and Airport Grade among them — have remained recognizable.

The name "Latex-ite" refers to latex (rubber polymer) chemistry. Early consumer driveway sealers were fairly simple asphalt emulsions — tiny droplets of asphalt suspended in water with emulsifiers and clay. Adding synthetic latex rubber polymers to that emulsion is meant to make the cured film more flexible and more resistant to cracking as the pavement expands and contracts. Polymer modification is now common across the consumer sealer market, but it remains the core idea behind the Latex-ite line.

The Latex-ite driveway sealers covered here are water-based asphalt-emulsion products rather than coal tar sealers. That distinction matters: coal tar sealers contain high levels of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and a number of states and municipalities restrict or ban them. Asphalt-emulsion sealers avoid that issue, clean up with soap and water while wet, and generally have lower odor and VOC content than solvent-based products. As with any product, confirm the formulation on the label — asphalt-based products will say so — especially if you live somewhere with sealer regulations.

What asphalt emulsion does not do is penetrate deeply into the pavement. It forms a thin film on top of the asphalt that shields the binder from UV, water and chemical spills. That film wears with traffic and sunlight, which is why every product in this category is a recurring maintenance item rather than a one-time fix. Understanding that basic chemistry is the most useful filter for reading any sealer's marketing claims.

The Latex-ite Line and the Budget Competitor

Latex-ite sells several sealers, but the two most widely stocked and most frequently compared are the Optimum and the Airport Grade. We also include the Black Jack Drive-Maxx 500 below because it is a common lower-cost alternative on the same shelf. Here's how the three are positioned before we get into detail.

The Latex-ite Optimum is the brand's mainstream filler & sealer. It is a polymer-modified asphalt emulsion that the manufacturer positions as able to fill minor cracks as you seal, so a separate crack-filling step isn't needed for hairline cracks. This is the product most homeowners see first and the one most often recommended for a typical residential driveway. Price-wise it sits in the mid tier of consumer sealers.

The Latex-ite Airport Grade is the brand's premium offering. The name is a marketing reference to the demands of airport pavement — a positioning claim, not a certification we can verify. It is marketed as a more heavily fortified polymer-modified formulation intended for longer service and tougher conditions than the Optimum. It is priced above the Optimum, typically in the upper tier of consumer asphalt sealers.

The Black Jack Drive-Maxx 500 comes from a different brand altogether: Black Jack, made by Gardner-Gibson. The verified product listing describes it as a "Premium 5 Year Filler & Sealer" in a 5-gallon pail that "fills small cracks." The "500" corresponds to the 5-year rating on the label, and it sits below the Drive-Maxx 700 in Black Jack's own range. It is a budget-to-mid-priced asphalt-emulsion filler & sealer and a natural price comparison against the Latex-ite Optimum.

Latex-ite Optimum — Full Review

1
POLYMER-MODIFIED FILLER & SEALER
Latex-ite Optimum Driveway Filler & Sealer
🏆 Best in Line

The Latex-ite Optimum is one of the best-known consumer driveway sealers in the US, and its appeal is easy to understand from what it is: a water-based asphalt emulsion modified with latex rubber polymers and loaded with fillers so that it can do two jobs at once — fill hairline cracks and seal the surface. That combination is aimed squarely at the homeowner who wants a decent result in one weekend with one product.

What the formulation means. A polymer-modified emulsion like the Optimum cures by evaporation: the water leaves, the asphalt droplets coalesce, and the latex polymer helps the resulting film stay flexible rather than brittle. Flexibility is what helps a sealer film ride out seasonal expansion and contraction without cracking at joints and edges. The filler content is what lets a product like this be squeegeed into small cracks. It is not a substitute for a dedicated crack filler on wider cracks — filler-sealers are designed for hairline and small cracks only. Check the label for the maximum crack width the Optimum is rated to fill; for anything wider than that, use a separate crack filler first and let it cure per its own directions.

Application. Filler-sealers in this class are thick, pourable materials applied with a squeegee or a sealer brush. The general sequence on labels for this category is consistent: clean the surface thoroughly, remove oil and grease spots with a degreaser and rinse, let the pavement dry, pour the sealer in a line across the driveway and spread it in a thin, even coat, working it into small cracks with the squeegee edge. Two thin coats generally outlast one heavy coat — thick coats can stay soft longer, track, or crack as they cure. Follow the Optimum label on whether one or two coats is recommended and how long to wait between them.

Oil spots and prep. No water-based asphalt sealer bonds well over active oil contamination. Oil softens asphalt binder and prevents the emulsion from adhering, so the sealer can peel or stay discolored over the stain. Degreasing and, for stubborn spots, an oil-spot primer before sealing are standard practice. Prep, not product choice, is the most common reason a sealer job fails early.

Weather and cure. Labels for water-based asphalt sealers generally call for application when air and surface temperatures are at least around 50°F and rising, with no rain expected for at least 24 hours. Dry-to-touch and foot-traffic times are typically measured in hours, while vehicle traffic usually needs a longer wait — commonly 24 to 48 hours, longer in cool or humid weather. Humidity and shade slow evaporation significantly. Use the Optimum label's specific drive-on time and add margin in marginal weather.

Longevity. Treat any multi-year figure on a sealer pail as a best-case number under ideal prep, application and conditions. Thin-film asphalt-emulsion sealers wear fastest in tire paths and turning areas and in strong sun. In practice, many homeowners reseal mid-tier emulsion products every two to three years, sooner on heavily used or sun-baked driveways. The pavement-maintenance industry generally recommends resealing when the surface turns gray and the aggregate starts to show, not on a fixed calendar.

✓ Pros

  • Fills small cracks during sealing — fewer products for minor damage
  • Water-based — soap and water cleanup while wet
  • Thick, forgiving consistency suits first-time DIYers
  • Polymer modification helps the film stay flexible
  • Widely available at home centers and online
  • Mid-tier price for a polymer-modified filler & sealer

✗ Cons

  • Any multi-year label claim assumes ideal prep and conditions
  • Thin-film asphalt sealers fade faster in strong UV
  • Cracks wider than the label's rated width need a separate filler first
  • Won't bond over oil stains without thorough degreasing
Bottom Line: The Latex-ite Optimum is the sensible default for most asphalt driveways — it handles minor cracks, applies easily, cleans up with water, and sits at a fair mid-tier price. Prep well, apply thin, and follow the label's cure times.

Latex-ite Airport Grade — Full Review

2
PREMIUM POLYMER-MODIFIED
Latex-ite Airport Grade Driveway Sealer
💎 Premium Choice

The Latex-ite Airport Grade is the top of the brand's consumer line. It is still a water-based, polymer-modified asphalt emulsion — the same basic chemistry as the Optimum — but it is marketed as a more heavily fortified formulation intended to last longer and hold up better under demanding conditions. The "Airport Grade" name communicates the intended performance level; it is a marketing positioning, not a third-party certification.

What "more fortified" generally means. Within asphalt-emulsion sealers, the main levers manufacturers pull to improve durability are higher solids content (more material left behind per coat once the water evaporates), more polymer or rubber modification (better flexibility and adhesion), and filler or aggregate tuning (better wear resistance and texture). Products positioned at the premium end usually lean on some combination of these. The practical effect is a film that tolerates thermal movement, UV and traffic somewhat better than a basic emulsion. Latex-ite does not publish a complete formula, so treat any specific comparison of polymer percentages as unknown; what you can rely on is the label's own durability rating and directions.

Where it makes the most sense. The advantages of a more flexible, higher-solids film matter most in two situations. The first is freeze-thaw climates — the northern Midwest, the Northeast, the mountain West — where pavement expands and contracts repeatedly and water freezing in cracks pries them open. A more flexible film is less likely to crack at edges and joints. The second is high-UV regions — the Southwest and high-altitude areas — where sunlight oxidizes asphalt quickly and thin films fade fast. A heavier-bodied premium film generally holds its color and protection longer than an entry-level coat under the same sun.

Application. Application follows the same pattern as the Optimum: clean, degrease, dry, then squeegee or brush on thin, even coats. Premium sealers with higher solids tend to feel heavier to spread, so a good-quality squeegee helps. Coverage per pail varies with the surface — porous, never-sealed asphalt drinks far more product than a previously sealed driveway — so check the label's coverage estimate and buy a little extra rather than stretching the last coat thin. The same weather rules apply: temperatures around 50°F and rising, a dry forecast for at least 24 hours, and the label's drive-on wait before vehicles return.

Cost per year. The Airport Grade costs noticeably more per pail than the Optimum. Whether it pays off depends on how much longer it lasts on your driveway. If a premium product stretches your resealing interval even modestly — and you're doing the labor yourself — the cost per year of protection can end up close to the mid-tier product, with fewer weekends spent sealing. In a mild climate on a lightly used driveway, that advantage shrinks, and the Optimum is usually the better value.

✓ Pros

  • Latex-ite's premium tier, marketed for longer service life
  • Fortified polymer-modified film suits freeze-thaw and high-UV climates
  • Water-based, easy cleanup
  • Same familiar application method as the Optimum
  • Can be cost-competitive per year of protection in harsh conditions

✗ Cons

  • Noticeable price premium over the Optimum
  • Heavier body can be more work to spread
  • "Airport Grade" is a marketing name, not a certification
  • Not always stocked at smaller hardware stores
Bottom Line: The Latex-ite Airport Grade is the one to choose if you face hard freeze-thaw winters, intense sun, or heavy driveway use and want the longest interval you can reasonably expect from a water-based asphalt sealer. In mild conditions, the Optimum usually delivers better value.

Budget Competitor: Black Jack Drive-Maxx 500 — Full Review

3
BUDGET COMPETITOR · BLACK JACK (GARDNER-GIBSON)
Black Jack Drive-Maxx 500 Filler & Sealer
💰 Budget Option

The Black Jack Drive-Maxx 500 is not a Latex-ite product — it's part of Black Jack's Drive-Maxx line, made by Gardner-Gibson. We include it because it is one of the most common lower-cost alternatives a Latex-ite shopper will encounter. The product listing describes it as a "Premium 5 Year Filler & Sealer" in a 5-gallon pail that "fills small cracks." In other words, it is the same kind of product as the Latex-ite Optimum — an asphalt-emulsion filler & sealer — from a competing brand, typically at a lower or similar price.

What the "500" means. Black Jack's Drive-Maxx range is tiered by durability rating, and the number in the name tracks the rating on the label: the 500 is sold as a 5-year product, with the Drive-Maxx 700 positioned above it with a longer rating. As with any sealer, that rating is a manufacturer claim that assumes good prep and application; real-world life depends on traffic, sun, climate and how thick and even the coats go down.

Formulation and crack filling. As a water-based asphalt-emulsion filler & sealer, the Drive-Maxx 500 cures by evaporation and cleans up with water while wet, just like the Latex-ite products. Its filler content lets it be worked into small, hairline cracks during application. "Small cracks" is the operative phrase — check the label for the maximum crack width it is rated to fill, and treat anything wider with a dedicated crack filler first. Black Jack does not publish a full formula, so we can't say how its polymer content compares with the Latex-ite Optimum's; we'd treat the two as broadly comparable mid-range filler-sealers and let price, availability and the label specs decide.

Application and cure. The label directions follow the standard pattern for this category: clean and degrease, let the surface dry, apply with a squeegee in thin coats, and keep traffic off until the label's dry and drive-on times have passed. Expect the usual weather constraints — mild temperatures that are rising, no rain for at least a day, and longer cure times in cool, humid or shaded conditions. Check the pail for its stated coverage, which will vary with how porous your driveway is.

Who it suits. The Drive-Maxx 500 makes the most sense when price is a primary concern: rental properties, driveways you plan to resurface in a few years, or homeowners who are comfortable resealing on a shorter cycle. It's also a reasonable pick if your local store simply has it in stock and the Latex-ite products aren't — the two are closer in type than their branding suggests.

  • Filler & sealer that fills small cracks during application
  • Manufacturer-rated as a 5-year product
  • Water-based asphalt emulsion with soap-and-water cleanup
  • Sold in 5-gallon pails at major retailers and online

✗ Cons

  • Different brand — not part of the Latex-ite line
  • The 5-year rating assumes ideal prep and conditions
  • Only for small cracks — wider cracks need a separate filler

Choose it when price or availability decides; choose the Optimum or Airport Grade if you prefer the Latex-ite line or need its premium tier.

Latex-ite vs. Competitors

No review is complete without context on the alternatives you'll find on the same shelf or in the same search results. Here's how Latex-ite compares with the products it's most often weighed against, based on product category and published positioning.

Latex-ite vs. Black Jack Drive-Maxx (500 and 700)

Black Jack is Latex-ite's most direct rival on the shelf, and its Drive-Maxx line is tiered by durability rating. The Drive-Maxx 500 is labeled as a 5-year filler & sealer that fills small cracks; the Drive-Maxx 700 sits one step higher in Black Jack's range with a longer rating. A useful way to think about it is that the Drive-Maxx 500 lines up most closely with the Latex-ite Optimum, while the Drive-Maxx 700 is Black Jack's answer to a more premium tier like the Airport Grade. Within each brand, moving up a tier usually means more solids and modifiers and a higher price; across brands at the same tier, the products are similar in type. Our advice is to pick the tier that fits your climate and driveway first, then choose the brand based on price, availability and the label specs on the specific pail.

Latex-ite vs. Foundation Armor AR350 and AR500

These come up often in search results, but they solve a different problem. Foundation Armor's AR350 and AR500 are solvent-based acrylic sealers marketed for concrete, pavers and masonry — not asphalt-emulsion driveway sealers. The AR350 is marketed as a wet-look sealer and the AR500 as a higher-gloss option. If your driveway is concrete or pavers, that acrylic category is where to look and Latex-ite is the wrong product. If your driveway is asphalt, stick with an asphalt-compatible sealer such as the Latex-ite products; solvents can soften asphalt binder, so don't use a concrete acrylic on blacktop unless its label explicitly says asphalt is an approved surface.

Latex-ite vs. SealMaster

SealMaster is primarily a commercial pavement-maintenance brand that also sells to homeowners. Contractor-grade sealers are typically applied heavier, often with sand added, and are designed for parking lots and high-traffic surfaces. They can build a tougher wearing surface, but they are usually harder for a homeowner to apply well, may come in larger containers, and can be harder to find at retail. Some SealMaster products are coal tar-based, so read the label if that matters to you. For the average homeowner sealing a residential driveway every few years, the Latex-ite Optimum or Airport Grade is the more practical choice; for someone who wants contractor-style results and is comfortable with heavier application, a pro-grade product can be worth exploring.

Latex-ite vs. hiring a contractor

Professional sealcoating crews use spray or heavy squeegee equipment, commercial-grade sealers and, often, sand for traction and wear. The upside is speed and a uniform finish; the downside is cost, which for a typical residential driveway is usually several times the price of DIY materials. If your driveway is in decent shape and you're comfortable with a weekend project, a consumer product like the Optimum is a reasonable way to save money. If the pavement has extensive alligator cracking, potholes or drainage problems, a sealer won't fix them — get a paving contractor's assessment first.

Who Should Buy Latex-ite?

Based on what each product is and how it's positioned, Latex-ite makes sense in several specific situations:

First-time DIY sealers: The Optimum's water-based application, easy cleanup and built-in crack-filling ability make it one of the more approachable sealers for a first project. A thick, squeegee-applied filler-sealer is forgiving of uneven technique, and mistakes clean up with water while the product is still wet.

Homeowners with lightly cracked driveways: If your driveway has hairline to small cracks, a filler & sealer like the Optimum can address them in the sealing step without a separate crack-filling product and its extra cure time. For cracks wider than the label's rated width, fill those separately first — that's true of every filler-sealer, regardless of brand.

Freeze-thaw and high-UV homeowners who want a water-based product: If you prefer a water-based product for ease of application, environmental reasons or local regulations, and you live somewhere with hard winters or intense sun, the Airport Grade is the Latex-ite product positioned for those conditions.

Budget-first projects: If price is the deciding factor, the Black Jack Drive-Maxx 500 — a different brand — is a reasonable alternative to the Optimum. It's a 5-year-rated filler & sealer that fills small cracks, and it's well suited to rentals, short-horizon driveways, or homeowners happy to reseal more often.

Latex-ite is probably not the right choice for: concrete or paver driveways (asphalt emulsion is made for asphalt, not concrete); homeowners who want a high-gloss decorative look (asphalt emulsions dry to a flat-to-low-sheen black); driveways with structural problems such as widespread alligator cracking, potholes or base failure (repair first, seal later); or applications that call for contractor-grade, sand-loaded sealers on high-traffic commercial pavement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Latex-ite safe to use? Is it coal tar-free?

The Latex-ite driveway sealers covered in this review are water-based asphalt-emulsion products, not coal tar sealers — confirm "asphalt-based" or "coal tar-free" on the specific pail you buy. Coal tar sealers contain high levels of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and are restricted or banned in a number of states and municipalities. Water-based asphalt emulsions generally have lower odor and VOC content than solvent-based products. Standard precautions still apply: wear gloves and old clothes, avoid skin and eye contact, keep children and pets off the driveway until it's dry, and keep product out of storm drains and waterways. Let leftover material dry out before disposal according to local rules — don't pour liquid sealer down a drain.

How many pails of Latex-ite Optimum do I need for my driveway?

Start with the coverage figure printed on the Optimum pail, which is usually stated per pail and per coat. Measure your driveway (length × width, plus any flared apron), divide by the label coverage, and multiply by the number of coats. Thick filler-sealers cover far less area than thin penetrating sealers, and coverage drops significantly on rough, porous or never-sealed asphalt, so assume you'll land at the low end of the label's range on an older driveway. For example, a 20 × 30-foot driveway is 600 square feet; if the label lists a few hundred square feet per pail per coat, plan on at least two pails for one coat and more for two. Buying one extra pail and returning it unopened is usually easier than running short mid-job.

How long does Latex-ite need to dry before it rains?

Water-based asphalt sealers generally need a dry forecast of at least 24 hours after application, and the label for your product will state its specific requirement. The most vulnerable period is the first several hours, while the emulsion is still releasing water — rain then can dilute the sealer and wash it off the surface, leaving streaks or bare spots. Cool temperatures, high humidity and shade all slow drying. As a rule of thumb, apply on a warm, dry day with temperatures around 50°F or higher and rising, start early enough that the surface gets a full afternoon of sun, and avoid days with afternoon thunderstorms in the forecast. Keep vehicles off for the full drive-on time on the label. Our guide on how long driveway sealer takes to dry covers this in more detail.

Can I use Latex-ite on a concrete driveway?

No — Latex-ite driveway sealers are formulated for asphalt (blacktop) driveways, not concrete. Asphalt emulsion doesn't bond well to concrete and would leave a black coating that wears unevenly and can peel. Concrete needs its own sealer chemistry — a penetrating silane/siloxane sealer, an acrylic sealer formulated for concrete, or an epoxy/polyurethane coating system. See our guide to How to Seal a Concrete Driveway for the right product categories and application process.

Is the Latex-ite Airport Grade worth the extra cost over the Optimum?

It depends on your climate and how heavily the driveway is used. In mild climates with moderate sun and light traffic, the Optimum generally provides good protection and the premium for the Airport Grade is harder to justify. In freeze-thaw climates (the upper Midwest, New England, the mountain West) or high-UV regions (the Southwest and high-altitude areas), a more fortified, more flexible film is more likely to stretch the time between resealing, which narrows the cost gap on a per-year basis. If you're tired of resealing frequently in a harsh climate, the Airport Grade is worth considering; if the Optimum is already serving you well, the upgrade is optional.

Is Drive-Maxx a Latex-ite product?

No. Drive-Maxx is a Black Jack product line made by Gardner-Gibson. The Black Jack Drive-Maxx 500 is sold as a 5-year-rated filler & sealer that fills small cracks, and Black Jack also offers higher tiers such as the Drive-Maxx 700. The products are often shelved near Latex-ite and are similar in type — water-based asphalt-emulsion filler-sealers — which is why they're frequently confused. We cover the Drive-Maxx 500 in this review as a budget competitor from another brand.

How soon can I apply Latex-ite after a new asphalt driveway is poured?

Don't seal new asphalt right away. Fresh asphalt contains light oils that need time to evaporate while the binder cures and hardens. Sealing too early can trap those oils, slow curing and leave a soft surface that scuffs or deforms under tires. Guidance varies — sealer labels and paving contractors commonly cite waiting anywhere from a few months to a full year, and many pros prefer waiting through one full warm season. Follow the sealer label and your paving contractor's advice, and make sure the surface feels firm and isn't tacky before sealing. See our guide: When Should You Seal a New Asphalt Driveway?

Final Verdict

Latex-ite's two core sealers are sensible, widely available choices for asphalt driveways. The Optimum earns its popularity on practical grounds — a water-based, polymer-modified filler & sealer that handles minor cracks in the sealing step, applies easily and cleans up with water, at a mid-tier price. The Airport Grade is the brand's premium tier and makes the most sense where driveways take the most punishment: hard freeze-thaw winters, intense sun or heavy daily use.

If budget is the main constraint, the Black Jack Drive-Maxx 500 is a reasonable competitor from another brand — a Gardner-Gibson product rated by its manufacturer as a 5-year filler & sealer that fills small cracks. Remember that every product in this category is a thin protective film that wears over time: good prep, thin even coats and patience with cure times matter more to the result than the logo on the pail. For concrete driveways or a high-gloss look, you need a different category of sealer altogether.

Our Recommendation: Buy the Latex-ite Optimum for most asphalt driveways. Step up to the Latex-ite Airport Grade if you live in a freeze-thaw or high-UV region and want the longest interval between resealing. If price decides, the Black Jack Drive-Maxx 500 — a different brand — is a credible budget alternative.
