Engineered Hardwood vs Solid Hardwood — Which One Belongs in Your House

Both are real wood on top. The difference is what sits underneath, and that decides where the floor can go, how it reacts to humidity, and how many times it can be sanded back to new.

Solid hardwood is one piece of wood, usually 3/4" thick, milled with a tongue and groove and nailed to a wooden subfloor. Engineered hardwood is a real hardwood veneer — the wear layer — glued over a cross-laminated plywood or high-density fibreboard core. From a standing height, a good engineered floor and a solid floor of the same species and finish are indistinguishable. Underfoot and over decades they are not, and the differences are predictable.

The short answer: engineered floors win anywhere moisture or concrete is involved — basements, slabs, radiant heat, humid climates, wide planks — and a thick-veneer engineered board can be refinished nearly as often as solid. Solid wins on a wooden subfloor above grade where you want the longest possible life and the freedom to sand it many times over a century. The trap is cheap engineered with a paper-thin veneer, which looks fine in the box and can never be sanded at all.

⚡ The direct answer: On a plywood subfloor above grade in a stable climate, either works — choose solid for maximum refinishes, engineered for wide planks and faster installation. Below grade, over a concrete slab, or over radiant heat, choose engineered; solid is not recommended there. Whatever you buy, check the wear layer thickness: under 2 mm means little or no sanding ever, 3 mm or more means two to four full refinishes, and 4–6 mm approaches solid. Installed cost is similar — roughly $8–$18 per sq ft for solid and $6–$16 for engineered — so decide on suitability, not price.

How Each Floor Is Built

Solid Hardwood

A solid board is sawn from a log, kiln-dried to roughly 6–9% moisture content, and milled to a standard 3/4" thickness with tongue-and-groove edges. Widths run from 2-1/4" strip to 5" and beyond for plank. Thinner 5/16" and 1/2" solid products exist, but the 3/4" board is the one that defines the category and the one most refinishing figures assume.

Everything about solid flows from it being one piece of wood. It is as stable as the species and the cut allow, which is to say it swells and shrinks across its width with every seasonal humidity swing. Its usable thickness is the wood above the tongue — about 1/4" on a 3/4" board — and that is the reserve every future sanding draws on.

Engineered Hardwood

An engineered board has two parts. On top is the wear layer, a slice of real hardwood — oak, hickory, maple, walnut — anywhere from about 0.6 mm to 6 mm thick. Below it is the core, which comes in three main types:

  • Multi-ply plywood core — thin wood veneers stacked with each layer's grain at 90° to the one below, typically 5 to 11 plies. This is the classic engineered construction and the most stable. More plies of better species (birch, eucalyptus, poplar) generally mean a flatter, more dent-resistant board. Our guide to types of plywood explains why cross-lamination stops wood moving.
  • HDF (high-density fibreboard) core — compressed wood fibre, very flat and hard, common on click-lock floating products. Stable and dent resistant, but less tolerant of standing water at the joints than plywood.
  • Lamella or solid-wood core — strips of softwood laid crosswise under a thick wear layer, usually on premium wide-plank European-style boards. Stable and thick, at the top of the price range.

Total thickness runs from about 3/8" to 3/4". The cross-laminated core is the whole point: wood moves most across its grain, and gluing layers at right angles means each layer restrains the next. The veneer on top is too thin to push the core around, so the board stays close to its milled width as the seasons change.

One thing engineered is not is a laminate. Laminate flooring has a photograph of wood under a melamine wear layer and contains no hardwood surface at all — it is a different category with different strengths, covered in our best laminate flooring guide.

Wear Layer Thickness and How Many Refinishes

This is the single most important number on an engineered floor and the most frequently hidden one. Two refinishing methods matter, and they draw on the wear layer very differently.

Full sand and refinish takes the floor back to bare wood with a drum or belt sander, removes scratches, dents and old finish, and allows a colour change. A competent pass removes roughly 0.5–1 mm (about 1/32"). It is what restores a worn-out floor, and every one uses up wear layer you never get back.

Screen and recoat (also called buff and recoat) abrades only the existing finish with a fine screen or pad, then applies one or two fresh coats of polyurethane. It removes essentially no wood, so it can be repeated indefinitely, every three to seven years in a busy house. It renews the protective film and hides fine surface scratches; it does nothing for scratches that reach the wood, dents or grey water damage.

The ranges are conservative because floors are never perfectly flat. A sander takes more off high spots and board edges, and a floor with slight cupping can lose most of a thin wear layer on the first pass. Hand-scraped and wire-brushed textures also need more removal to sand flat, so a textured board with a 2 mm veneer realistically offers one sanding at most.

Put that against how floors actually get used. A residential floor that is screened and recoated on schedule often needs a full sanding only every 15–25 years. That means a 3 mm engineered floor offers 40–60 years of refinishable life and a 4–6 mm floor rivals solid for any realistic ownership period. The "solid can be sanded ten times" argument is true in principle and irrelevant to most buyers, who will sand it twice.

💡 Keep it from needing a sand. Most floors that end up being sanded did not need to be. The finish wore through in traffic lanes, the wood greyed, and only then did anyone call a refinisher. Screening and recoating while the finish is merely dull — for roughly a quarter of the price of a full refinish — protects the wear layer indefinitely. A good water-based polyurethane for floors is the usual recoat product, provided the existing finish accepts it (see the aluminium oxide section below).

Stability With Humidity

Wood gains and loses moisture with the air around it, and it expands and shrinks as it does — mostly across the width of the board, very little along its length. A 5" solid oak board can change width by roughly 1/32" to 1/16" between a humid August and a heated, dry February. Multiply that by thirty boards across a room and you see why solid floors open gaps in winter and crown or cup in summer.

Engineered boards move far less — commonly a third to a half as much as solid, sometimes less — because the cross-plied core resists the veneer's urge to swell. That has three practical consequences:

  • Width. Solid planks wider than about 5" are increasingly prone to cupping and gapping; many installers want 3/4" solid at 5" or narrower in humid or variable climates. Engineered is routinely made at 7"–10" wide with no particular stability penalty, which is why most wide-plank floors sold today are engineered.
  • Acclimation. Solid typically needs several days on site with the HVAC running, and its moisture content checked against the subfloor's before installation. Engineered still benefits from the same discipline — follow the manufacturer — but is far less punishing if conditions are slightly off.
  • Seasonal gaps. Both floors are happiest with indoor relative humidity between roughly 35% and 55% year-round. Solid punishes you visibly when you drift outside that range; engineered usually just shrugs.

Neither floor is waterproof. Engineered is dimensionally stable, which is not the same as water resistant: a flooded engineered floor will delaminate and swell at the edges just as a solid one will cup. If a room sees standing water — a laundry, a full bathroom, a basement with any history of seepage — a vinyl plank is the right answer rather than any wood; our best luxury vinyl plank flooring guide covers those rooms.

For a basement where engineered is going in, control the humidity before the floor arrives. A dehumidifier holding the space around 45–50% keeps the boards in range; our guide to what humidity to set a basement dehumidifier covers the setpoint and why lower is not always better for wood.

Below Grade

A basement slab is in contact with the ground and constantly releases water vapour. Solid wood nailed to a sleeper system above it will absorb that moisture from below and cup. Engineered can be floated over a vapour barrier and foam underlayment or glued down with a moisture-blocking adhesive, and its stability tolerates the swing between a damp summer and a heated winter. Test the slab first — a calcium chloride or in-slab RH test if the manufacturer specifies one, at minimum the taped-plastic-sheet test — and fix any active water before flooring over it. Our guide on how to fix a damp basement covers the sequence.

Over a Slab on Grade

Many single-storey and newer homes sit on a slab. Solid can go here only by building a nailable plywood subfloor over the concrete, which adds cost, height and time. Engineered glued straight to the slab, or floated over it, is the standard answer and keeps the floor height low enough to meet doorways and existing thresholds.

Radiant Heat

Radiant systems dry the wood directly from below and cycle it through temperature changes all winter. Engineered handles that well; most manufacturers who approve their product over radiant heat cap the floor surface temperature at around 80–85°F and want the system brought up gradually at the start of each heating season. Solid over radiant is possible with narrow quartersawn boards and careful moisture control, but most installers steer clients to engineered and most solid-flooring warranties are restrictive about it.

Installation Methods

Nail-down is how solid is installed and the only method for 3/4" solid. A flooring nailer drives cleats or staples through the tongue into a plywood or OSB subfloor, generally at least 5/8" and preferably 3/4" thick. It is fast and permanent and produces the solid, quiet feel people associate with hardwood. Thicker engineered boards (roughly 1/2" and up) can be stapled the same way over a wood subfloor.

Glue-down bonds the boards to the subfloor with a urethane or MS-polymer flooring adhesive. It is the standard method for engineered over concrete, feels nearly as solid as nail-down, and needs a flat substrate — usually within about 3/16" over 10 feet. Many adhesives double as a moisture barrier. It is messy, slower, and difficult to undo.

Floating applies only to engineered. Click-lock or glued-edge boards lock to each other and rest on an underlayment, not fastened to the subfloor at all. It is the quickest, most DIY-friendly method and works over concrete, existing flooring and radiant heat. The trade-offs are a slightly hollow sound unless the underlayment is dense, some flex underfoot, and a requirement for expansion gaps around every wall and under door casings. Underlayment choice matters more than people expect; the principles in our underlayment guide carry over, though always use the type the wood manufacturer specifies.

As a DIY job, floating engineered is the most achievable of all hardwood installs. Nail-down solid is reasonable for a confident DIYer with a rented flooring nailer. Glue-down over concrete and anything site-finished are best left to professionals.

  • Glue-down installation: roughly $3–$7, plus adhesive at $0.75–$1.50.
  • Floating installation: roughly $2–$4, plus underlayment at $0.30–$1.
  • Site sanding, staining and finishing of unfinished boards: roughly $3–$5 on top of installation.
  • Plywood subfloor over a slab to allow solid: $2–$4 extra.
  • Removing old flooring: $1–$3 depending on what is there.
  • Waste allowance: order 7–10% extra, more on diagonal or herringbone layouts.

Future refinishing costs the same for both: a full sand and refinish typically runs $3–$8 per sq ft, and a screen and recoat $1–$2. On a 500 sq ft open-plan space, the difference between a $1,500 full refinish and a $750 recoat every few years is the clearest financial argument for staying on top of maintenance.

Notice the overlap. Mid-grade engineered and prefinished solid land within a dollar or two of each other installed, which is why price alone rarely decides this. Where engineered genuinely saves money is on concrete, where it avoids the subfloor build-up that solid requires.

Lifespan and Resale

Solid hardwood commonly lasts 50–100 years and often outlives the house's second or third owner. Plenty of floors from the early twentieth century are still in service after half a dozen sandings. Its lifespan is limited mostly by how carelessly it is sanded and whether it has ever been flooded.

Engineered hardwood lifespan tracks its wear layer. A thin-veneer floor (under 2 mm) gets 20–30 years — the life of its factory finish plus recoats — and is then replaced rather than restored. A 3–4 mm floor is good for 40–60 years with maintenance. A 4–6 mm sawn-face floor on a quality core can reasonably be expected to last as long as most people will own a house, and several premium manufacturers back them with long structural warranties.

Resale perception is closer than flooring forums suggest. Buyers and listing agents treat both as "hardwood floors," and a quality engineered floor in good condition does not generally carry a meaningful discount against solid in the same house. Where engineered hurts is when it is obviously cheap — a hollow-sounding floating floor, visible veneer lines, or boards that cannot be refinished when the buyer's inspector notices wear. Solid still has a prestige edge in older, high-end housing stock where it is expected; engineered is the norm in new construction and condos.

If resale is the main concern, the ranking is roughly: well-kept solid ≈ thick-veneer engineered, then mid-grade engineered, then thin-veneer engineered, with laminate and vinyl a step below in buyer perception, though not necessarily in practicality.

Prefinished vs Site-Finished

Prefinished boards arrive with stain and several coats of finish already applied and cured under UV light in a factory. Almost all engineered flooring is prefinished, and so is a growing share of solid. Installation is the whole job: no sanding dust, no solvent smell, and you walk on it the same day. The factory finish is typically harder and more evenly applied than anything achievable on site. The trade-off is the micro-bevel along each board's edge, which marks the joints and catches dirt, and the fact that the finish does not bridge the seams, so spilled water can work into the joints.

Site-finished floors are installed raw, then sanded flat, stained and coated in place. The result is a perfectly flat surface with square edges and a continuous film of finish over the joints — the better choice in a kitchen where spills happen, and the only way to get an exact custom colour or to match an existing floor in an adjoining room. The cost is several days of disruption, dust, and cure time before furniture goes back. Water-based finishes have made this much easier to live with; see our guides to water-based floor polyurethane and the best applicators for it.

Unfinished engineered with a thick (3 mm+) wear layer can be site-finished like solid, which gives you the stability of engineered with the seamless look of a sanded floor. Do not site-sand anything with a veneer under 2 mm.

Aluminium Oxide Finishes

Most prefinished floors — engineered and solid — use a urethane finish loaded with aluminium oxide particles, sometimes alongside ceramic additives. It is extremely abrasion resistant, which is why manufacturers can offer 25- to 50-year residential finish warranties. Read those warranties carefully: they cover the finish wearing through to bare wood under normal use, not scratches, dents or pet damage.

The same hardness that makes aluminium oxide last makes it awkward to maintain. A scratch through it shows as a pale line and cannot be blended out the way one in a site-applied finish can. And ordinary fresh polyurethane struggles to bond to it, so a standard screen and recoat may peel. Recoating an aluminium-oxide floor usually needs a manufacturer-approved recoat system or a chemical abrasion and bonding primer. Ask the manufacturer what they approve before the floor needs it, and note it with the warranty paperwork.

Scratches, Dents and Pets

Surface hardness is set by the species, not the construction. A white oak engineered floor and a white oak solid floor scratch identically, because the top 2 mm of both is white oak. What differs is dent resistance: a heavy point load on a thin veneer is partly resisted by the core, so a dense HDF or many-ply birch core resists dents well, while a cheap soft core under a thin veneer can dent more easily than solid.

Janka hardness gives a rough guide to dent resistance: American walnut sits around 1,010, red oak around 1,290, white oak around 1,360, hard maple around 1,450 and hickory around 1,820. Hickory and white oak are the practical choices for busy households.

With dogs, the finish and the look matter more than the hardness. Claw scratches mostly go into the finish rather than the wood, and how visible they are depends on:

  • Sheen. Matte and satin hide scratches far better than semi-gloss; gloss shows every one.
  • Texture. Wire-brushed and hand-scraped surfaces camouflage new marks among existing ones.
  • Colour and grain. Mid-tone, busy-grained floors hide wear; very dark and very light smooth floors show it.
  • Refinishability. A solid or thick-veneer floor can be sanded once the dog years are over. A thin-veneer floor can only be recoated or replaced.

Pet accidents are a bigger threat than claws. Urine left on any wood floor works into the seams and stains the wood black, and neither construction resists it. If you have an elderly or untrained dog, our guide to the best flooring for dogs compares wood against the waterproof alternatives honestly.

How to Spot Cheap Engineered

The engineered category spans everything from excellent to disposable, often under similar-sounding names. These are the tells:

Wear layer not listed, or listed vaguely. Reputable manufacturers state it in millimetres on the spec sheet. "Real wood surface" with no number usually means 0.6–1 mm. If a seller cannot tell you, assume the worst.

Rotary-peeled veneer. The cheapest wear layers are peeled off a spinning log like the face of a sheet of plywood. Peeled veneer has a wide, wavy, swirling grain that looks like plywood rather than sawn lumber, and it tends to develop fine surface cracks — "face checking" — as it dries, because peeling stresses the fibres. Better floors use sliced veneer, cut flat from the log for natural-looking grain, or sawn-face (dry-sawn) veneer cut like lumber, which looks like solid wood and is the most durable.

Few or thin core plies. Look at the end of a board. A quality plywood core has many even plies with no gaps; a cheap one has three or four uneven layers, voids or soft filler. A board that flexes easily in your hands will flex on the floor.

Short boards. A box full of 12"–24" lengths gives a busy, choppy floor with many more joints. Quality product includes a good share of long boards.

Thin overall. Below about 3/8" total thickness with a thin veneer, the floor feels and sounds like a laminate.

Missing formaldehyde compliance. Engineered and composite wood products sold in the US should be labelled compliant with TSCA Title VI (and many show CARB Phase 2). No label is a reason to walk away.

Warranty language. A long "finish warranty" on a thin-veneer floor is not a promise of long life. Look for a structural warranty and for the warranty's stated moisture and installation conditions.

💡 The two-question test. Ask any seller: "What is the wear layer in millimetres, and how is the veneer cut — rotary, sliced or sawn?" A good product has a clear answer to both. If the answers are "about 1 mm" and "we don't know," you are looking at a floor with a finite life and no second chance, priced as though it were hardwood. At that point a good laminate or vinyl plank may be the more honest buy.

Put simply: engineered is the more versatile floor, and solid is the longer-lived one on the narrow set of subfloors where it belongs. A good engineered floor with a 3 mm or thicker sawn or sliced veneer closes most of the lifespan gap, goes in places solid cannot, and handles wide planks and humidity better. Solid remains the best choice on an above-grade wood subfloor when you want the floor to outlast you and plan to refinish it on site.

The worst choice in this comparison is not either category. It is a thin, rotary-peeled engineered floor bought as if it were hardwood. If budget pushes you toward that end of the market, compare it seriously against a high-quality laminate or rigid-core vinyl, which will usually outwear it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is engineered hardwood as good as solid hardwood?

A good one is, for most homes, and in some places it is better. The surface is the same real wood, so it looks and scratches the same as solid of the same species and finish. Its cross-laminated core makes it far more stable, so it can go over concrete, in basements and over radiant heat where solid is not recommended, and it suits wide planks. The real difference is refinishing reserve: a 3/4" solid board can be fully sanded four to six times or more, while engineered depends on its wear layer — none under 1 mm, one or two at 2–3 mm, three to five at 4–6 mm. Buy a 3 mm or thicker veneer and the gap is mostly academic.

How many times can engineered hardwood be refinished?

It depends entirely on the wear layer. Each full sanding removes roughly 0.5–1 mm, and floors are never perfectly flat, so allow a margin. Under 1 mm: no full sanding, only screen and recoat. 1–2 mm: possibly one careful sanding by a professional. 2–3 mm: one or two. 3–4 mm: two or three. 4–6 mm: three to five, comparable to solid. Screen and recoat, which only abrades the finish, removes essentially no wood and can be done as often as needed. Done every few years, it can delay full sanding for 15–25 years, which is why even a 3 mm floor can last decades.

Can solid hardwood be installed in a basement?

It is not recommended, and most solid-flooring manufacturers will not warranty it below grade. A basement slab continually releases moisture vapour, and solid wood absorbs it from below and cups, even over a sleeper system with a vapour retarder. Engineered hardwood is the wood option for basements: floated over a vapour barrier and underlayment or glued down with a moisture-blocking adhesive, after the slab has been tested and any active water problem fixed. Keep the basement's humidity steady with a dehumidifier around 45–50%. If the basement has any history of flooding, a waterproof vinyl plank is a safer choice than any wood.

Is engineered hardwood cheaper than solid?

Not by as much as people expect. Thin-veneer engineered is the cheapest real-wood floor at $5–$9 per sq ft installed, but mid-grade engineered ($7–$12) and prefinished solid ($8–$15) overlap heavily, and premium wide-plank engineered with a 4–6 mm veneer can cost as much as solid or more. Engineered saves money most clearly over concrete, where solid would need a plywood subfloor built on top of the slab at an extra $2–$4 per sq ft, and in DIY installs, where a floating engineered floor needs no nailer, adhesive or professional labour.

Does engineered hardwood scratch more easily than solid?

No. Scratch resistance comes from the species and the finish, and the top of an engineered board is the same wood with the same factory finish found on prefinished solid. Dent resistance can differ: a thin veneer over a soft or cheap core may dent more readily under a dropped object or furniture leg, while a dense HDF or many-ply birch core resists dents well. The bigger practical difference is what happens later. A heavily scratched solid or thick-veneer floor can be sanded back to new; a thin-veneer floor can only be recoated or replaced.

Can you put engineered hardwood over radiant floor heating?

Yes, if the specific product is rated for it, and it is the preferred wood option for radiant systems. Typical manufacturer conditions include a maximum floor surface temperature of about 80–85°F, gradual warm-up at the start of each heating season rather than switching straight to full output, and either glue-down or floating installation as specified. Narrower boards and plywood cores are generally more forgiving than very wide planks. Solid hardwood over radiant heat is possible in narrow, quartersawn boards but carries a real risk of gaps and cupping, and many solid-flooring warranties exclude it.

Does engineered hardwood hurt resale value?

Generally not. Listings and buyers describe both as hardwood floors, and a quality engineered floor in good condition is not usually discounted against solid. It is the norm in new construction and condos. Where it can hurt is when it is obviously low-grade: a hollow-sounding floating floor, a visibly thin or peeled veneer, or a worn floor that an inspector notes cannot be refinished. In older high-end homes where solid oak is expected, solid retains a modest prestige edge. Either way, a well-maintained floor matters far more than its construction.

Can you recoat a prefinished aluminium oxide floor?

Yes, but not always with a standard screen and recoat. Aluminium oxide finishes are hard enough that a buffer and screen struggle to abrade them properly, and fresh polyurethane often fails to bond to them and later peels. Most manufacturers approve a specific recoat system or a chemical abrasion and bonding primer process designed for factory finishes. Check what your flooring manufacturer approves before recoating, since an unapproved process can void the finish warranty. If the floor is solid or has a thick veneer, a full sand removes the factory finish entirely and you can then use any good floor polyurethane.
