Why Is My Driveway Sealer Peeling? Causes, Diagnosis and Fixes

Peeling is a bond failure, not a wear problem — the sealer never properly stuck to whatever was underneath it. The flakes themselves usually tell you why, and the fix depends entirely on the answer.

Driveway sealer peels because it never bonded properly to the surface underneath it. On asphalt, the usual culprits are a surface that was dusty, dirty or oily when the sealer went down; too many coats stacked up over the years until the film is thick and brittle; sealer applied in cold weather or just before rain so it never cured; or a new sealer that is incompatible with the old one. Less often it is asphalt sealed too young, a cheap or over-watered product, or tires scuffing a soft coat on a hot day.

The good news is that peeling is almost always a surface problem. The asphalt beneath is usually fine, and the fix is to remove whatever is loose, correct the cause, and put down a thin coat — or, in a surprising number of cases, do nothing and let the remaining flakes wear away. What you should not do is pour another heavy coat over the top, because that is how most peeling driveways got that way.

⚡ Quick Answer: Peeling means the sealer lost its grip, usually because of dirt or dust under it, oil spots that were not primed, layers built up from sealing too often, or cold or rain before it cured. Check the underside of a flake to see which. The fix: scrape and pressure-wash off everything loose, prime any oil spots, let the surface dry for at least 24 hours, then apply one thin coat within label conditions — typically 50°F and rising with a dry 24-hour forecast. If only small areas are flaking off old build-up, it is often better to leave it and let traffic wear it down.

Peeling vs Normal Wear: Know Which You Have

Before diagnosing anything, make sure the driveway is actually peeling. Sealer is a thin sacrificial coat, and it is supposed to wear. Normal wear looks like the black fading to dark grey, starting in the tire tracks and where cars turn, with the texture of the stone gradually showing through. The sealer gets thinner from the top down and disappears over two or three years. That is the product doing its job, and the answer is simply to reseal when the driveway is genuinely due — the timing guide on how often to seal a driveway covers how to tell.

Peeling is different. The sealer lets go from the bottom up, lifting as a film with a clean edge. You see flakes, curls or sheets, often with sharp outlines, and the surface underneath is a different colour — grey asphalt, an older layer of sealer, or a dusty film. Peeling can start within days of application or appear after the first winter, and it tends to come in patches rather than evenly across the drive. If you can get a fingernail under the edge and lift a piece, it is peeling. If you cannot, and the surface is just grey, it is wear.

There is a third look that people often confuse with peeling: a fine web of hairline cracks across the sealer, a bit like dried mud or crazed pottery glaze. That is the sealer film itself cracking, usually because it is too thick, and it is the stage just before flaking begins. It is worth recognising because it tells you to stop adding coats.

The Eight Causes of Peeling Sealer

1. A Dirty or Dusty Surface

This is the most common cause by a distance. Driveway sealer is designed to bond to asphalt, and anything between the two — dust, fine sand, dried mud, pollen, grass clippings, the grey powder of oxidised asphalt — becomes the layer the sealer actually sticks to. That layer has no strength of its own, so the sealer sits on a film of loose particles and lifts off as soon as it is stressed by traffic, water or a freeze-thaw cycle.

A quick sweep is not enough. Blowers and brooms move the loose grit but leave the fine dust that has settled into the texture of the asphalt. Most sealer labels call for the surface to be swept, then washed with water and a cleaner, then allowed to dry. Skipping the washing step is the single biggest reason DIY sealer jobs peel, and it is especially likely along the edges of the drive where soil washes in from the lawn, and in the bottom third of a sloped driveway where silt collects after rain.

2. Oil Spots Without Primer

Oil, transmission fluid and power-steering fluid soak into asphalt and soften the binder. Water-based sealers — which includes most asphalt emulsion, coal tar emulsion and acrylic products — cannot wet out over an oily surface. The sealer beads or skins over the spot, looks fine for a few days, and then flakes away in a patch that matches the outline of the stain almost exactly. The peeling is often slightly tacky or brown-tinged underneath.

The fix for this is well established: clean the stain as thoroughly as possible, then apply an oil-spot primer, which is a quick-drying sealing coat designed to lock the remaining oil in and give the sealer something to grip. Many sealer manufacturers sell a matching primer, and the label of the sealer will usually tell you which ones it is compatible with. Our guide to removing oil stains from a driveway covers the cleaning half of that job. If the oil has softened the asphalt so much that it feels spongy under a screwdriver, no primer will hold — that spot needs patching instead.

3. Too Many Coats and Build-Up

Sealing every year, or putting on two heavy coats every time, eventually builds a film of sealer that is far thicker than the product was designed for. Asphalt sealer is meant to be a thin skin that flexes with the pavement. A thick film is stiffer than the asphalt underneath it, it shrinks as it ages, and it moves differently in hot and cold weather. The result is crazing — that network of hairline cracks — followed by curling edges and flakes that peel off in layers.

You can identify build-up by turning a flake over: it will often be visibly layered, like a cross-section of plywood, with several coats of different shades of black. Build-up peeling usually starts in areas that get little traffic, such as along the edges, near the garage door and under where a car is parked, because traffic naturally wears the sealer off the tire paths. The usual cure for this is not to seal more but to seal less, which is counter-intuitive for many homeowners and inconvenient for the companies selling annual sealcoating contracts.

4. Applied in Cold Weather or Before Rain

Water-based sealers cure as the water evaporates and the binder particles fuse into a continuous film. That only works within a temperature window. Most labels call for 50°F and rising, with the air and pavement staying above about 50°F for the next 24 hours, overnight included. Below that, the particles do not coalesce properly and the film stays weak. Sealer that is chilled or frosted during the first night often turns dull, brownish or chalky, and it powders off or peels in flakes within weeks. Our guide on what temperature to seal a driveway explains why the overnight low matters as much as the afternoon high.

Rain causes a similar failure. A shower on uncured sealer re-wets the film, washes some of the binder off (you will see brown or grey streaks running to the street), and leaves what remains thin and poorly bonded. Heavy dew does the same thing on a smaller scale. Sealer caught by rain in the first few hours often looks streaky and then peels in irregular patches the following season. The detail on how long a fresh coat needs is in our article on rain after sealing a driveway.

At the other extreme, very hot pavement in direct sun can skin the sealer over before it has time to bond, particularly on an older, dry, porous surface. Many labels suggest lightly misting very hot asphalt with water before applying, leaving no standing puddles.

5. Incompatible Sealers

Not all sealers stick to each other. The classic failure is putting a water-based sealer over an oil-based one that was applied in the last year or so. Oil-based sealers penetrate the asphalt and leave a surface that remains slightly oily for many months, and a water-based emulsion over that behaves exactly as it does over an oil spot: it beads, skins and peels. The usual guidance is to wait a year or more after an oil-based coat before switching to water-based, or to prime first. There is more on telling the two families apart, and on switching between them safely, in our comparison of oil-based vs water-based driveway sealer.

Binder chemistry matters too. Coal tar and asphalt-based sealers are both water-based emulsions, but they are not the same material, and some manufacturers advise against putting one over the other. Acrylic sealers can have trouble bonding over a heavily built-up coal tar layer. If you are not sure what was used last time — common after buying a house — the safest approach is to choose a sealer whose label explicitly says it can go over previously sealed surfaces of any type, and to test a small area first.

6. Sealing Asphalt That Was Too New

Fresh asphalt contains light oils that keep working their way to the surface for months after paving. Seal it too soon and you are effectively putting sealer over an oily surface, with the same poor adhesion you get over an oil spot. The sealer may also slow the asphalt's own curing, leaving the surface soft. Most industry guidance is to wait at least six months after paving and ideally nine to twelve in cooler climates; our guide to when to seal a new asphalt driveway explains the reasoning. A useful sign of readiness is that water poured on the surface no longer shows an oily sheen and the asphalt has begun to lighten slightly in colour.

7. Poor-Quality or Over-Diluted Sealer

Budget sealers often have lower solids content, which means more water and less binder in the pail. That is not automatically a peeling problem, but it leaves a thinner, weaker film with less margin for error on preparation and weather. The bigger issue is dilution. Some products are designed to have a small amount of water added in hot weather; adding more than the label allows starves the film of binder so it cures as a weak, powdery layer that flakes and chalks off.

Not stirring properly has a similar effect. Sealers that contain sand or mineral fillers settle heavily in the pail, and if they are not mixed thoroughly the first part of the pail goes on watery and the last part as sludge. Patchy peeling that matches where you started or finished each pail is a good indicator of this.

8. Tire Scuffing on Hot Days

Sealer that is technically dry can still be soft for days or weeks, especially in hot weather when pavement temperatures can climb well past 120°F. If a car's wheels are turned while it is stationary, or someone drives on the drive too early, the tires can scuff, twist or lift the soft film. This shows up as peeled or smeared patches where cars turn, park or make three-point turns, often with tire tread marks visible in the sealer around them. Bicycle kickstands, motorcycle stands and trailer jacks can cause the same type of localised damage.

Scuffing is the least serious cause because it is cosmetic and local, and the surrounding sealer is usually well bonded. It mostly calls for patience: let the coat harden fully, and avoid turning the steering wheel while the car is stationary on the drive during the first few hot weeks.

How to Diagnose Which Cause You Have

Most peeling has one main cause, and the evidence is usually right in front of you. Start by lifting a few flakes and turning them over, then look at the pattern of where the peeling is happening. The table below gives the usual signatures.

Two quick tests help when the evidence is mixed. The first is a tape test: press a strip of duct tape firmly onto the sealer in an area that looks sound, rub it down hard, and rip it off quickly. If sealer comes up with the tape, adhesion is poor across a wider area than the visible peeling, and spot repairs alone will not hold. If the tape comes up clean, the problem is local.

The second is a scrape test: drag a stiff putty knife or floor scraper across the edge of a peeled patch. Well-bonded sealer resists and chips off in small pieces at most. Poorly bonded sealer lifts in sheets, and you will be able to work outward from the patch for some distance. The amount that comes away tells you how big the repair really is.

It is also worth separating a sealer problem from an asphalt problem. If the material coming up includes stone and the surface underneath is crumbling or pitted, the asphalt itself is breaking down and sealing will not fix it. That needs repair first — see our guide on fixing a crumbling asphalt driveway.

How to Fix Peeling Sealer

The repair follows the same order regardless of cause: remove everything loose, correct whatever caused the failure, let the surface dry, and apply a thin coat in the right weather. The differences are in how much you remove and what correction is needed.

Step 1: Remove All Loose Sealer

New sealer bonded to loose old sealer will peel along with it, so everything that is not firmly attached has to come off. For small areas, a long-handled floor scraper or a stiff putty knife is the best tool; work from the peeling edge outward until the sealer stops lifting. A stiff wire brush helps with curled edges and thin flakes in the texture of the asphalt.

For larger areas, a pressure washer does the job faster. A gas unit around 2,500–3,000 PSI is typical, and they are widely available to rent. Use a 25° or 40° tip rather than a zero-degree or turbo nozzle, keep the wand 8–12 inches from the surface, and move steadily. Asphalt is softer than concrete, and holding the nozzle too close can blast out the fine aggregate and leave a rough, pitted surface. The goal is to remove loose film, not polish the drive back to bare stone.

Finish by sweeping up all the flakes — they are messy, and bits tracked into the house leave black marks — and then washing the whole driveway, not just the repair areas, with a driveway cleaner or detergent and a stiff brush. Rinse thoroughly. Detergent residue left on the surface is another bond breaker.

Step 2: Treat Oil Spots and Correct the Cause

Clean any remaining oil stains with a degreaser, rinse, and let them dry. Then apply an oil-spot primer to every stain, following the label's drying time — often an hour or so before sealing. Do not skip small drips; they peel just like large ones.

If the cause was incompatibility with a recent oil-based coat, either wait out the period recommended by the sealer manufacturer or use a primer the label approves. If it was sealing too-new asphalt, the best fix is time: remove what has lifted and leave the drive unsealed until it has had at least six months, ideally a full year. If you suspect a weak or diluted product, buy a better grade this time and follow the mixing instructions exactly.

Step 3: Let It Dry Completely

After washing, give the asphalt at least 24 hours of dry weather before sealing, and longer after a cool, cloudy day. Water trapped in the surface keeps sealer from bonding just as dust does. A practical check: tape a small square of plastic sheet to the drive for an hour or two in the sun; if condensation forms underneath, the surface is still damp.

Step 4: Apply One Thin Coat

Seal only when the forecast shows at least 50°F and rising, staying above that overnight, with no rain for the period on the label — usually at least 24 hours. Apply one thin, even coat at the label's coverage rate. For most asphalt emulsions that means roughly 250–400 square feet per 5-gallon pail per coat on reasonably smooth asphalt, and a bit less on rough or porous surfaces. A thin coat that bonds is far better than a thick one that sits on top.

Whether to coat only the repaired areas or the whole drive depends on how extensive the peeling is. Spot-sealing small patches is fine functionally but tends to show as a slightly different shade of black for a season. If more than about a quarter of the drive needed repair, one thin coat over the whole surface gives a more even look — but on a driveway with build-up, keep that coat as thin as you can, or skip it on the heavily layered areas entirely. Our step-by-step guide on how to seal an asphalt driveway covers application technique, edging and coverage in more detail.

Step 5: Keep Traffic Off Until It Hardens

Most labels allow foot traffic after several hours and cars after 24–48 hours, longer in cool or humid weather. In hot weather the sealer stays relatively soft for a while after that, so avoid turning the steering wheel while stationary and keep kickstands and trailer jacks off the drive, or put a board under them, for the first couple of weeks.

When to Let It Wear Off Instead

Not every peeling driveway needs fixing. If the cause is build-up — and on driveways that have been sealed every year it very often is — adding another coat over a scraped surface just adds another layer to a film that is already too thick. The better choice is often to scrape off what is loose and then leave the driveway unsealed for a season or two.

Traffic, weather and sun will gradually wear down the remaining sealer, starting with the high spots and tire paths. Once most of the old film has worn thin and the asphalt is starting to show grey texture across the surface, you can resume sealing with a single thin coat and a much longer interval — every two to three years for most residential drives, rather than every year. The drive will look patchy in the meantime, which bothers some people more than others, but the asphalt itself is not at risk from a year without sealer.

Letting it wear off also makes sense for minor tire scuffs and a few isolated flakes on an otherwise sound coat. A patch repair on a small blemish often looks worse than the blemish itself.

💡 The build-up rule. If a flake shows three or more distinct layers, or the sealer surface is crazed with hairline cracks, stop adding coats. Scrape what is loose, wait for the rest to wear down, and seal less often from now on. More sealer will not fix a problem caused by too much sealer.

When Stripping Makes Sense

Full removal of driveway sealer back to bare asphalt is rarely necessary and is not a pleasant job. It is worth considering only when the build-up is so thick that it is peeling across large areas year after year, or when an incompatible layer underneath keeps causing every new coat to fail.

On asphalt, the realistic methods are mechanical. Repeated pressure washing with hot water, combined with scraping, removes a surprising amount of thick sealer and is the most practical DIY approach. Professionals may use hot-water pressure washers, rotary surface cleaners or light grinding. Chemical strippers designed for driveway sealer exist, but treat them with caution: anything strong enough to dissolve asphalt sealer is also capable of softening the asphalt binder underneath, and run-off must be kept out of drains and lawns. Read the label carefully and test a small area first.

If the old sealer is that thick, it is also worth pricing the alternative. On an older driveway where the asphalt is also showing its age, a thin overlay or resurfacing may cost not much more than professional stripping and resealing, and resets the clock completely.

Peeling Sealer on Concrete Driveways

Concrete driveway sealers fail for slightly different reasons. Penetrating sealers — silanes, siloxanes and silicates — soak into the concrete and leave no film, so they cannot peel. When a concrete driveway's sealer peels, it is almost always a film-forming acrylic. The common causes are moisture in the slab (the sealer was applied over damp concrete, or water vapour is rising from beneath), applying too thick, sealing too soon after the concrete was poured, or putting a water-based acrylic over an old solvent-based one.

Moisture-related failure often shows as white, cloudy patches as well as peeling. With solvent-based acrylics, a xylene-based product can sometimes re-dissolve the film and let trapped moisture out, though peeling areas usually still need to be stripped and resealed. For a driveway that keeps failing, switching to a penetrating sealer removes the peeling risk altogether. Our guide on how to seal a concrete driveway covers the options.

How to Stop It Happening Again

Nearly every peeling driveway could have been prevented by one of a handful of habits. They are not complicated, but they do require patience, which is where most jobs fall down.

Clean properly, not just sweep. Wash the whole drive with water and a driveway cleaner, scrub the edges and low spots where silt collects, rinse thoroughly and let it dry for a full day. This one step prevents more peeling than everything else on this list.

Prime every oil spot. Clean, then prime, even small drips under where cars park. It is cheap and fast.

Watch the overnight forecast, not just the day. Seal only when the temperature stays above the label minimum for 24 hours with no rain or heavy dew expected. A mid-morning start on a warm, dry day gives the coat the longest stretch of warmth before evening.

Apply thin coats at the label rate. Two thin coats are better than one thick one on bare or very worn asphalt, but on a drive that still has sealer on it, one thin coat is often enough.

Seal less often. Every two to three years is a sensible interval for most residential asphalt, and only when the old sealer has worn thin. Annual sealing is the main driver of build-up.

Stay with one sealer family. If you switch from oil-based to water-based or from coal tar to asphalt-based, follow the manufacturer's waiting period or priming instructions. If you do not know what was used before, choose a product labelled for use over all previously sealed surfaces and test a patch.

Stir thoroughly and do not over-water. Mix until there is no sediment at the bottom of the pail, and add water only if and as the label permits.

Wait out new asphalt. Leave a fresh driveway unsealed for at least six months, preferably a year.

Be gentle with fresh sealer. Keep cars off for the full label time, avoid turning the wheels while stationary for the first couple of weeks of hot weather, and put a board under kickstands.

The Short Version

Driveway sealer peels when it did not bond, and the underside of a flake usually tells you why: dust means the surface was not clean, layers mean build-up, an oily or tacky underside means oil or an incompatible coat.

To fix it, remove everything loose with a scraper or pressure washer, wash the drive, prime oil spots, let it dry for a day, and apply one thin coat in warm, dry weather.

If the problem is build-up, do the opposite of what instinct suggests: scrape off the loose material, leave the drive unsealed for a season or two, and seal less often from now on.

And if stone is coming up with the sealer, the asphalt itself needs repair before any sealer is worth applying.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my new driveway sealer peeling already?

Peeling within days or weeks of application almost always comes down to preparation or weather. The most common cause is dust or dirt left on the surface, because a sweep alone does not remove the fine dust in the asphalt's texture — the drive needs washing and a full day to dry. The next most common is cold or rain: water-based sealers need around 50°F and rising for 24 hours, overnight included, and a dry spell long enough to cure. Unprimed oil spots, an incompatible previous coat and tire scuffing on a hot day account for most of the rest. Lift a flake and check the underside to see which applies.

Can I put new sealer over peeling sealer?

Not over the loose parts. New sealer bonds to whatever is underneath, and if that is a flake that has already let go, the new coat will peel off with it. Scrape or pressure-wash away everything that lifts, wash the whole drive, let it dry for at least 24 hours, and then apply a thin coat over sound, firmly bonded sealer or bare asphalt. Do a tape test first: if duct tape pulls sealer up from areas that look fine, adhesion is poor over a wider area and you need to remove more before resealing.

Will peeling driveway sealer damage the asphalt?

Generally no. Sealer is a thin cosmetic and protective skin, and the asphalt underneath a peeling coat is usually in the same condition it was before sealing. Asphalt can go a year or two without sealer without harm. The exception is when the peeling exposes cracks that let water in, which should be filled before winter. If stone and asphalt are coming up with the sealer, the pavement itself is deteriorating, and that is a separate repair job rather than a sealer problem.

Should I pressure wash peeling driveway sealer?

Yes, it is the quickest way to remove loose sealer from larger areas. Use a gas pressure washer around 2,500–3,000 PSI with a 25° or 40° tip, keep the wand 8–12 inches from the surface, and keep it moving. Avoid zero-degree and turbo nozzles, which can gouge the asphalt and blast out the fine aggregate. Asphalt is softer than concrete, so the aim is to remove loose film, not to strip the drive back to bare stone. Let the surface dry for at least a day before resealing.

Does sealing a driveway too often cause peeling?

Yes, and it is one of the most common causes on older driveways. Each coat adds to the film, and after several years of annual sealing it becomes much thicker and stiffer than the asphalt underneath. It then crazes into a web of hairline cracks and flakes off in layers, usually in low-traffic areas like the edges and in front of the garage. The cure is to scrape off what is loose, let the rest wear down, and seal only every two to three years once the old sealer has worn thin.

Why is sealer peeling only where my car parks?

Two likely causes. The first is oil: small drips from the car soften the asphalt and stop water-based sealer from bonding, so it flakes off in patches that follow the stains. Clean those areas and prime them with an oil-spot primer before resealing. The second is tire scuffing, where turning the steering wheel while the car is stationary twists the fresh, soft sealer, especially in hot weather. Tread marks in the sealer around the damage point to scuffing, which is cosmetic and usually best left to wear in.

Can you put water-based sealer over oil-based sealer?

Only after the oil-based coat has had time to weather, or with a primer the manufacturer approves. Oil-based sealers leave the asphalt surface slightly oily for many months, and a water-based emulsion over that cannot bond properly — it peels in sheets. The usual guidance is to wait a year or more after an oil-based application before switching. Going the other way, oil-based over water-based, is generally less of an issue, but the label of the product you are applying is the final word.

How do I get peeling sealer off my concrete driveway?

Peeling on concrete usually means a film-forming acrylic sealer that lost adhesion, often because of moisture in the slab or a coat applied too thick. Loose areas can be scraped and pressure-washed off, but a patchy acrylic usually needs a chemical stripper made for acrylic concrete sealers to get an even surface, followed by a thorough rinse. If the problem was moisture, consider resealing with a penetrating silane or siloxane sealer instead — it soaks in and forms no film, so it cannot peel.
