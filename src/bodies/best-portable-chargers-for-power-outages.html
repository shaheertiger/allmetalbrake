Most power outages are not dramatic. The fridge stays shut, the house gets quiet, and within a few hours the real problem becomes obvious: every phone in the house is draining faster than usual because it is hunting for a weak cell signal, everyone is using it as a flashlight, and nobody charged it last night. A portable charger is the cheapest, simplest piece of outage equipment you can own, and for a short outage it is often the only one you need.

The trouble is that the category spans an enormous range. A pocket power bank that tops up one phone, a laptop power bank that fits in a backpack, a battery with a household outlet on the side, and a 1 kWh portable power station that can also run a refrigerator are all sold as "portable chargers," "portable battery packs" or "portable power sources." They are labeled in different units, too: power banks in milliamp-hours (mAh), power stations in watt-hours (Wh). This guide translates between them, shows you how many charges each size really delivers, and names six widely sold options from Anker, EcoFlow and Jackery, arranged from smallest to largest, so you can pick the one that matches how long your outages usually last and what you need to keep alive.

⚡ The short answer: Every household should have at least one 20,000–25,000 mAh USB-C power bank (roughly 75–90 Wh), such as the Anker 737 PowerCore 24K, which recharges a phone three to four times and can top up a laptop. If your outages run longer than a day, or you have a CPAP, a router or several people to keep connected, step up to a roughly 300 Wh power station such as the Anker SOLIX C300X or EcoFlow River 3 Plus. If you also want to keep the fridge cold, you are shopping for a 1 kWh class unit like the Jackery Explorer 1000 v2 or EcoFlow Delta 3 Plus — that is a different job from charging.

mAh vs. Wh: Reading the Label Honestly

Power banks are labeled in milliamp-hours because that is how phone batteries were historically marketed. The problem is that mAh is only meaningful alongside a voltage, and the voltage on a power bank label is not the voltage that comes out of the USB port. That is why a "20,000 mAh" bank does not charge a "5,000 mAh" phone four times.

The honest unit is the watt-hour, which measures energy regardless of voltage. To convert, multiply the mAh figure by the cell voltage and divide by 1,000. Lithium-ion power bank cells are nominally 3.6–3.7 V, so:

Wh = mAh × 3.6 (or 3.7) ÷ 1,000.

A 24,000 mAh bank therefore holds about 86–89 Wh; a 10,000 mAh bank about 36–37 Wh. Many power banks print the watt-hour figure in small type on the case or base, because airlines and shipping rules require it. When it is there, trust it over the mAh number.

Label capacityStored energyDelivered (60–70%)Typical use
5,000 mAh~18 Wh~11–13 WhOne partial phone charge
10,000 mAh~36–37 Wh~22–26 Wh1.5–2 phone charges
20,000 mAh~72–74 Wh~44–52 Wh3 phone charges or half a laptop
24,000 mAh~86–89 Wh~52–62 Wh3–4 phone charges or most of a laptop
27,000 mAh~97–100 Wh~58–70 WhThe airline-limit ceiling

Why Only 60–70% Arrives

Energy is lost at every step. The bank boosts its 3.6–3.7 V cells up to 5 V, 9 V, 15 V or 20 V for USB, and that conversion generates heat. The phone then steps the voltage back down to charge its own battery, losing more. The cable adds resistance, and charging a warm phone while it is in use wastes still more. Real-world delivered energy from a power bank typically lands around 60–70% of the stored figure. Faster charging generally runs a little less efficiently than slower charging, and a cold bank delivers less until it warms up.

Power stations are labeled in watt-hours already, and their USB ports draw from a higher-voltage battery pack through a single conversion stage, so they tend to deliver a higher share — around 85% is a fair planning figure, the same one we use for AC runtime in the power station buying guide. Charging a phone from a power station's AC outlet through its wall adapter adds an extra conversion and wastes energy; use the USB ports directly whenever you can.

How Many Phone Charges Will You Get?

Phone batteries also hide behind mAh labels, but at a slightly higher nominal voltage (around 3.85 V on many recent phones). A typical current phone stores somewhere around 12–20 Wh; large "Max" and "Ultra" models sit at the top of that range, compact models toward the bottom. We use 15 Wh as a planning number.

The math is: charges = (bank Wh × efficiency) ÷ phone Wh. For the Anker 737: 87 Wh × 0.65 ≈ 57 Wh delivered, divided by 15 Wh ≈ 3.8 charges. For a 288 Wh power station: 288 × 0.85 ≈ 245 Wh, divided by 15 ≈ 16 charges.

Now the practical part: how long does a phone actually last in an outage? Far longer than the "charges" figure suggests if you manage it. A phone on low-power mode, screen dimmed, with Wi-Fi off once the router is dead, will often run a full day on one charge with moderate use. A household of four that each uses one charge a day needs about 60 Wh delivered per day. A single 24,000 mAh bank covers that for roughly one day; four 10,000 mAh banks cover it for one and a half; a 288 Wh station covers it for about four days before counting anything else.

💡 Weak signal is the hidden drain. When the power is out, nearby cell sites may be running on their own backup batteries or may go down, and phones burn energy searching for a signal. If you have no usable signal, switch to airplane mode and turn it off periodically to check messages. That alone can double how long a phone lasts.

USB-C Power Delivery Wattages Explained

Capacity tells you how much energy a charger holds. Output wattage tells you how fast it can hand that energy over, and whether it can charge a particular device at all. USB-C Power Delivery (PD) negotiates a voltage and current between the charger and the device; the wattage on the box is the best the charger can offer, not what every device will draw.

USB-C outputWhat it charges wellNotes
Up to 12–18 WPhones at moderate speed, earbuds, watchesTypical of older USB-A banks
20–30 WPhones at full fast-charge speed, small tabletsEnough for nearly every phone
45–65 WTablets, ultrabooks, handheld game consolesMost thin laptops charge here
100 WMost 14–15 inch laptops at full speedNeeds a cable rated for 5 A
140 W and upLarge performance laptopsUses the newer extended-power range; needs a compatible device and a 240 W-rated cable

Three practical points follow. First, for phones alone, anything over about 30 W per port is wasted; buy capacity, not wattage. Second, for laptops, match the bank's USB-C output to your laptop's own charger rating. A 65 W laptop will charge from a 45 W bank, slowly, and may lose ground if you work on it at the same time. Third, the cable matters: a cheap cable rated for 3 A caps the connection at 60 W regardless of what the bank and laptop can do. Keep at least one 5 A (100 W or 240 W rated) USB-C cable in your outage kit.

Also watch the total. Many banks advertise a high single-port figure that drops when you plug in a second device, because the total output is shared. If you want to charge two laptops at once, look for the combined output rating, not the headline number.

Power Banks With an AC Outlet

Between laptop power banks and power stations there is a class of chargers marketed as a "portable outlet": a power bank of roughly 60–100 Wh with a small AC outlet on the side, usually limited to somewhere between 65 and 100 W. They exist for devices that only accept a wall plug, such as an older laptop with a barrel-jack charger, a camera battery charger, an electric shaver or a small lamp.

They are useful in a narrow way, but understand the trade-off. The AC outlet runs through a tiny inverter that wastes a meaningful share of an already small battery, and the low wattage limit means it will not run anything with a motor or heater. If all your devices charge over USB-C, a plain USB-C bank of the same size will give you more charges. If you need several AC devices, a 300 Wh class power station such as the Anker SOLIX C300X or EcoFlow River 3 Plus gives you three times the energy and proper outlets for the price of a couple of premium power banks. Before buying a "portable outlet" bank, check whether the outlet is pure sine wave (better for electronics) and what its continuous wattage limit is.

Phones

Charge every phone to full as soon as a storm warning goes out. Turn on low-power mode at the start of an outage, not when the battery is already low, and use a dedicated flashlight rather than the phone's light. Download offline maps and save utility outage numbers before you need them.

Lights

Lighting is where many people waste their phone batteries. A rechargeable LED lantern or a USB-powered LED light uses very little energy and frees the phone for communication. Headlamps on AA or AAA batteries remain one of the best outage tools ever made: no charging, long shelf life, both hands free. Never use candles as your main light source if you can avoid it; they cause a disproportionate share of outage house fires.

Radios

When cell networks are congested or down, a battery-powered AM/FM radio with NOAA weather bands is how you hear about restoration times, boil-water notices and evacuation orders. Many emergency radios charge over USB and include a hand crank and a small solar panel; more on those below.

Medical Devices

If anyone in the house depends on powered medical equipment, plan for it separately and generously. A CPAP machine is manageable: without the heated humidifier it draws roughly 30–60 W, so a 300 Wh class station gets most users through a night and a 1 kWh unit through two or three. Ask the manufacturer for a DC power cord, which lets the CPAP run from the station's 12 V outlet without inverter losses. Oxygen concentrators are a different matter; many draw far more power continuously, and they should be backed by the supplier's own battery or cylinder plan rather than a consumer charger. Register with your utility's medical-needs program if one exists, and tell your equipment supplier what your backup plan is. Power banks are fine for small devices like glucose meters, hearing-aid chargers and insulin coolers that charge over USB.

Using Your Car as a Charger

Your car is a large battery with an engine-driven alternator attached, and in an outage it can be an excellent charger — used correctly. A USB-C car charger plugged into the 12 V socket can deliver 30–100 W depending on the adapter, enough to charge phones and many laptops at full speed. A small plug-in inverter adds a household outlet, but the 12 V socket is typically fused for only about 120–180 W, so keep the load modest.

Follow a few rules. Never run the engine in a garage, even with the door open; carbon monoxide builds up fast and seeps into the house. Park outside, in the open, away from windows and doors. Do not drain the car battery with the engine off for long; most 12 V starting batteries are not designed for deep discharge, and a dead car is a bigger problem than a dead phone. If you idle to charge, the fuel use is small, and a gas tank stores a great deal of energy compared with any battery — but keep the tank above half when storms are forecast, because gas stations need power to pump.

A car can also recharge a power station. Most power stations accept a 12 V car-socket charging cable. Input from a car socket is limited to roughly 100–120 W, so a 288 Wh station refills in around three hours of driving; a 1 kWh station takes a full day's driving, which makes the car a supplement rather than a solution for the larger units. For extended outages with heavier loads, a home generator is the more realistic recharging source.

Hand-Crank and Solar Radios

Emergency radios with a hand crank and a small solar panel are excellent at one job: keeping the radio itself running indefinitely. They are poor at another job they are often marketed for: charging your phone. A minute of brisk cranking generates only a tiny amount of energy, enough for a few minutes of radio listening or perhaps a brief emergency call, but nowhere near a full phone charge. The built-in solar panel is typically a watt or two at best, which can top up the radio's internal battery over a sunny day but adds very little to a phone.

Buy one for the radio, the flashlight and the weather alerts, and treat its USB output as an emergency trickle. Look for NOAA weather band reception, the ability to run on ordinary AA batteries as well as its rechargeable pack, and a USB-C charging input so you can top it up from your power banks.

Solar Panels for Power Banks and Stations

Solar is far more practical at a larger scale. A folding 100 W panel, which delivers about 60–75% of its rating in good sun, can put roughly 250–350 Wh into a power station over a day with four to five peak sun hours — enough to refill a 288 Wh station or recharge a household's phones for several days. Small folding panels of 20–30 W with USB outputs can charge a phone directly in strong sun, but they are sensitive to clouds and angle, and phones often stop charging when the output dips; charging a power bank from the panel and then the phone from the bank works better. The Small Things That Make It Work

  • Cables for every device. At least two USB-C to USB-C cables (one rated 5 A), a USB-C to Lightning cable if anyone has an older iPhone, and a USB-A to USB-C cable for older banks and car chargers.
  • A multi-port wall charger. When power returns briefly, you want to refill everything at once.
  • A labeled bin. Keep the kit in one place the whole household knows, with a card listing what is in it and when it was last charged.
  • A charging rota. In longer outages, recharge power banks from the power station during the day while someone is awake, rather than overnight unattended.
  • Printed numbers. Utility outage line, insurance, family contacts, medical supplier. A dead phone should not mean lost numbers.

Storage and Self-Discharge

An outage charger is only useful if it is charged when the outage starts, and lithium batteries do not hold a charge forever. Lithium-ion power banks typically lose a few percent per month to self-discharge, plus a small draw from their own circuitry; after six months in a drawer a bank charged to full may be noticeably lower. LFP power stations self-discharge slowly as well, but their displays and standby electronics can drain them faster if left switched on.

For long-term storage, manufacturers commonly recommend keeping lithium batteries partially charged — roughly 50–80% — and topping them up every three months or so. In practice, for outage kits we suggest a simple rhythm: check and top up everything when the clocks change in spring and fall, and again whenever a major storm is forecast. Storage at 100% for long periods ages cells faster, but the difference over a year or two is small compared with the cost of an empty bank in an outage; if in doubt, keep the main bank full.

Temperature matters as much as charge level. Do not store power banks in a hot car through summer — high heat is the fastest way to age lithium cells and, in rare cases, cause swelling. Cold reduces available capacity temporarily, and lithium batteries should not be charged below freezing. A closet inside the house is ideal.

⚠️ Retire swollen or damaged banks. A power bank that has puffed up, cracked, gets hot when idle or smells odd should not be charged again. Do not throw it in the trash; take it to a battery recycling drop-off, many of which are at home improvement and electronics stores.

For devices that run on disposable batteries — headlamps, radios, smoke detectors — lithium AA and AAA cells hold their charge for many years in storage and perform well in the cold, which makes them a good complement to rechargeable gear in a kit that may sit untouched for a long time.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best portable charger for a power outage?

For most households, a 20,000–25,000 mAh USB-C power bank such as the Anker 737 PowerCore 24K is the best single charger. It stores roughly 86–89 Wh, enough for three to four phone charges or most of a laptop charge, and its 140 W USB-C output handles laptops as well as phones. If several people need to stay connected for more than a day, or you want to keep the Wi-Fi router running, a roughly 300 Wh power station such as the Anker SOLIX C300X or EcoFlow River 3 Plus is better value per phone charge. Pair either with a pocket bank per person so nobody waits for a turn.

How many times will a 20,000 mAh power bank charge a phone?

Usually about three times, not four or five. Convert the label to watt-hours first: 20,000 mAh at the cells' 3.6–3.7 V is about 72–74 Wh. Power banks typically deliver 60–70% of that to the phone after voltage conversion and heat losses, so roughly 44–52 Wh actually arrives. A typical smartphone battery holds around 15 Wh, so that is about three full charges. A compact phone with a 12 Wh battery may get closer to four, while a large phone with 19 Wh or more will get two and a half. Charging slowly and keeping the phone off while it charges helps.

How do I convert mAh to watt-hours?

Multiply the mAh figure by the battery voltage and divide by 1,000. Power bank cells are nominally 3.6–3.7 volts, so a 10,000 mAh bank holds about 36–37 Wh, a 24,000 mAh bank about 86–89 Wh, and a 27,000 mAh bank right around the 100 Wh airline limit. Watt-hours are the better unit because they measure energy regardless of voltage, which lets you compare power banks directly with power stations, which are always rated in watt-hours. Many banks print the Wh figure on the case for airline purposes; when it is there, use it instead of calculating from the mAh number.

Is a power bank or a power station better for outages?

They do different jobs. A power bank is small, cheap and portable, and it is the right tool for keeping one or two phones alive through a short outage or in a go-bag. A power station holds far more energy — a 288 Wh unit is roughly three of the largest airline-legal banks — and adds AC outlets, so it can charge a whole family's phones and laptops for days and also run a router, lamp, fan or CPAP. A 1 kWh station can even run a refrigerator. Most well-prepared households own both: pocket banks for each person and one station as the central charging hub.

Can a power bank charge a laptop?

Yes, if it has a USB-C Power Delivery port with enough wattage and your laptop charges over USB-C. Match the bank's output to your laptop's charger: thin laptops typically need 45–65 W, most 14–15 inch laptops 65–100 W, and large performance laptops up to 140 W. A lower-wattage bank will still charge the laptop slowly, but may not keep up if you work on it at the same time. You also need a cable rated for the wattage; a standard 3 A cable caps the connection at 60 W. Laptops with barrel-jack chargers need a bank with an AC outlet or a power station.

Can I take a power bank on a plane?

Yes, in your carry-on only, if it is 100 Wh or less, which is roughly 27,000 mAh or less at 3.6–3.7 V. Banks between 100 and 160 Wh need airline approval and are usually limited to two per passenger. Anything over 160 Wh, which includes every portable power station in this guide, is not allowed in either the cabin or checked luggage. Spare lithium batteries are never allowed in checked bags. Make sure the bank's capacity is printed legibly on the case, and check your airline's current policy, since some restrict using power banks during the flight.

Do hand-crank chargers really work for phones?

Only as a last resort. A hand crank generates very little energy, so a minute of cranking typically yields a few minutes of radio listening or perhaps enough for a short emergency call, not a meaningful phone charge. The small solar panels built into emergency radios are similarly limited, usually a watt or two. They are excellent for keeping the radio itself and its flashlight running indefinitely, which is genuinely valuable in a long outage. For phone charging, rely on power banks, a power station, your car's 12 V socket, or a folding solar panel of 20 W or more charging a power bank in full sun.

How long does a power bank hold its charge in storage?

A healthy lithium-ion power bank typically loses a few percent of its charge per month in storage, so a fully charged bank is still mostly full after a few months but noticeably lower after six months to a year. Heat speeds both self-discharge and permanent aging, so store banks indoors rather than in a hot car. For outage kits, check and top up every bank about every three months, such as when the clocks change and again when a storm is forecast. Retire any bank that swells, cracks or gets hot while idle, and take it to a battery recycling drop-off rather than the trash.
