For years, the conventional wisdom among hardscape contractors was simple: if you wanted serious paver protection with real color enhancement, you used a solvent-based sealer. Water-based products were seen as the weaker, shorter-lived option — fine for budget jobs, not for premium installations. That picture has changed. Water-based acrylics, polymer blends, and silane/siloxane penetrants are now mainstream, and in several categories that matter to homeowners — VOC emissions, odor, cleanup, regulatory availability, and ease of do-it-yourself application — they hold clear practical advantages. Solvent-based sealers still tend to produce the deepest color and can be more forgiving in cool or damp conditions, but for many residential patios, walkways, and pool decks, a well-chosen water-based sealer is the sensible choice. If your joints use polymeric sand, water-based products also tend to be the easier fit — though you should always confirm compatibility on both labels.

This guide focuses only on water-based paver sealers. We're not against solvent products — they have legitimate strengths. But for most homeowners doing routine maintenance on residential pavers, the best water-based options cover every look you're likely to want: invisible natural protection, a matte finish that also locks joint sand, a subtle low-gloss enhancement, or a full wet look. Rather than claim hands-on results we can't document, we built this list from research: manufacturer product descriptions and labels, technical data where available, verified retail listings, and the well-established chemistry of each sealer type. Every product below is sold as a water-based formula.

For each product we look at the chemistry, the finish it produces, whether it penetrates into the paver or forms a film on top, what the manufacturer says about coverage and recoating, which surfaces it suits (concrete pavers, clay brick, natural stone, pool decks, freeze-thaw climates), and the trade-offs you accept by choosing it. Where we aren't certain of a figure such as coverage per gallon, VOC content, or recoat window, we tell you to check the label rather than guess — formulas and directions change, and the label on the container you buy is the final word. One note on names you may see elsewhere: Ghostshield Cryli-Tek 5505 and Armor AR350 are solvent-based acrylics, so they don't belong in a water-based roundup; Ghostshield's water-based sibling, Cryli-Tek 5500, is included below.

⚡ Quick Answer: For most pavers, the Foundation Armor SX5000 WB is the best water-based choice for invisible, natural-look penetrating protection. If you want a natural matte look that also binds the sand in your joints, choose SEK Surebond SB-1300. For a darkening wet look, SEK Surebond SB-6000 is our pick, with Ghostshield Cryli-Tek 5500 for a subtler low-gloss enhancement and Glaze 'N Seal 173 as a widely available wet-look alternative. Water-Based vs. Solvent-Based Paver Sealers: The Full Picture

Understanding the real differences between water-based and solvent-based sealers helps you make an informed decision rather than relying on outdated assumptions or marketing language. Here's an honest comparison across the dimensions that matter most.

VOC Content and Safety During Application

This is where water-based products have the clearest advantage. Solvent-based paver sealers use petroleum-derived solvents — commonly xylene, acetone, or similar carriers — that evaporate as the sealer dries. These VOCs (volatile organic compounds) are strong-smelling, flammable, and irritating to breathe, so solvent products call for organic-vapor respiratory protection, good ventilation, and no ignition sources nearby. Several states and regional air districts restrict the VOC content of sealers sold for architectural use, which is why some solvent formulas are unavailable in certain areas. Water-based sealers use water as the main carrier and are generally formulated to far lower VOC levels; the exact figure varies by product, so check the label or Safety Data Sheet if a specific g/L limit matters to you. Application near children's play areas, gardens, and pools is more comfortable with water-based products, though you should still follow the label's personal protective equipment guidance.

Color Enhancement: The Traditional Gap

Solvent-based acrylics have historically produced more intense color enhancement and deeper gloss than water-based alternatives, partly because solvent carriers wet out the paver surface aggressively and leave a clear, glossy film. Modern water-based wet-look formulas — such as the SEK Surebond SB-6000 and Glaze 'N Seal 173 below — are designed specifically to darken and enrich paver color, and low-gloss products like Ghostshield Cryli-Tek 5500 offer a middle ground. If a mirror-like, high-gloss finish is your top priority, a solvent-based acrylic may still be the stronger choice. For most homeowners, though, a water-based wet-look or low-gloss sealer delivers a clearly enhanced appearance compared with unsealed pavers.

Durability and Reapplication Frequency

How long a sealer lasts depends far more on its type — penetrating or film-forming — and on your sun exposure, traffic, and climate than on whether it's water- or solvent-borne. Film-forming sealers (acrylics and polymer blends) sit on top of the paver and wear gradually, especially in traffic lanes and in strong sun, so they need periodic maintenance coats. Penetrating sealers (silane/siloxane and reactive silicate products) work inside the paver, have no surface film to scuff or peel, and are generally positioned by their makers as longer-lasting. Manufacturers state their own recoat guidance, and it varies from product to product — check the label, and use the simple water-bead test described in the FAQ to judge when your surface actually needs attention.

Cleanup and Environmental Impact

Water-based products clean up with soap and water while still wet — a major practical advantage for DIY application. Solvent-based sealers require solvent for tool cleanup and spills, and leftover solvent and rags need careful handling and disposal. That said, "water-based" doesn't mean "harmless to dump": don't rinse any sealer, water-based or not, into storm drains, and dispose of leftovers according to the label and your local household hazardous waste rules.

Compatibility with Joint Sand and Polymeric Sand

Many paver installations use polymeric sand in the joints — a sand blended with binders that activate with water to form a firm joint that resists washout, weeds, and ants. Sealer compatibility with polymeric sand is a real concern: some sealers can haze or interact poorly with joint binders, and sealing before the sand has fully cured can trap moisture. Most polymeric sand manufacturers publish a waiting period before sealing and list compatible sealer types, so read both the sand bag and the sealer label. If your joints use ordinary jointing sand instead, a joint-stabilizing sealer such as the SEK Surebond SB-1300 is designed to bind that sand in place as part of the sealing process.

1
WATER-BASED SILANE-SILOXANE
Foundation Armor SX5000 WB Penetrating Sealer
🏆 Best Overall — Penetrating, Natural Look

The Foundation Armor SX5000 WB is a water-based silane/siloxane penetrating sealer — the water-borne version of Foundation Armor's SX5000 line. Instead of forming a coating on top of the paver, silane and siloxane molecules soak into the pores of concrete, brick, and many natural stones and bond there, lining the pore walls with a water-repellent (hydrophobic) layer. The surface looks essentially unchanged — no gloss, no darkening, no change in texture — but water that lands on it tends to bead rather than soak in. That combination is exactly what many paver owners want: real protection that doesn't change how the pavers look or feel underfoot.

Why does penetrating protection matter so much for pavers? Water is the root of most paver problems. Absorbed moisture drives freeze-thaw damage in cold climates, feeds algae and moss in shaded, humid areas, carries dissolved salts to the surface as efflorescence, and lets spills wick in and stain. A silane/siloxane water repellent reduces how much liquid water enters the paver while still allowing water vapor to escape — it's "breathable" — which is why this chemistry is widely used on masonry walls, bridge decks, and driveways as well as pavers. Because nothing sits on the surface, there's no film to peel, scuff, whiten, or turn slick when wet, which also makes it a sensible choice around pools.

The water-based formulation makes the SX5000 WB easy to handle for a DIYer: low odor, soap-and-water cleanup, and no need to manage flammable solvent. Penetrating sealers of this type are typically applied with a low-pressure pump sprayer or roller to a clean, dry surface, saturating evenly without leaving puddles; the label gives the exact method, number of coats, dry times, and coverage range. Coverage varies a lot with porosity — a dense, new concrete paver absorbs far less than an old, open-textured one or a soft clay brick — so measure your area carefully and check the label's coverage figures before deciding how many gallons to order.

Penetrating sealers don't wear away from the top like films do; instead, they gradually lose effectiveness as UV and weathering degrade the treated surface layer. Manufacturers generally position silane/siloxane products as longer-lasting than film-forming acrylics, but the realistic interval depends on your exposure. Check Foundation Armor's current label or product page for its stated service life and recoat guidance, and use the water-bead test to decide when to reapply. The main limitations are the flip side of its strengths: it won't enhance color, it can't penetrate through an existing film sealer (that would need to be stripped first), and it doesn't bind joint sand.

Be aware that almost any sealer can deepen paver color slightly, especially on porous or weathered pavers, so try a small test spot first if you want to see exactly how your pavers will look. For homeowners who like the look of unsealed pavers but are tired of sweeping sand back into the joints every spring, the SB-1300 is the most practical pick on this list.

Application is different from a penetrating sealer like the SX5000 WB. Joint-stabilizing sealers are generally applied generously enough for the material to flow down into the joints, and excess is then worked off the paver faces so it doesn't puddle or dry cloudy. The joints need to be full and the sand dry before you start. Follow the label for the number of coats, coverage, dry times, and how soon the surface can take foot traffic. If your joints already contain polymeric sand rather than ordinary jointing sand, check both the SB-1300 label and the polymeric sand manufacturer's guidance before applying, since the two products are designed to do overlapping jobs.

It doesn't create the deep, inside-the-paver water repellency of a silane/siloxane product, so in severe freeze-thaw climates some homeowners prefer a penetrating treatment for the paver body. And as with any water-based acrylic, it shouldn't go over an existing solvent-based acrylic without stripping or confirming compatibility first.

✨ Best Wet Look

The SEK Surebond SB-6000 is the wet-look counterpart to the SB-1300 — a water-based polymer blend resin sealer sold specifically as a wet-look, darkening product. For homeowners who want their patio to look "finished," this is the water-based product built for that job, from a manufacturer that focuses on paver care.

It helps to understand how wet-look water-based sealers work. The resin fills the surface pores of the paver and leaves a thin, clear layer on top. Light reflects off that smoother layer instead of scattering from the rough paver surface, so colors read darker and richer — the same effect you see when pavers are wet. The trade-off is that the more the film sits on top, the more it behaves like a coating: it needs periodic maintenance, it can scuff in high-traffic areas, and its slip resistance deserves attention, especially when wet.

Application follows the usual rules for water-based film-formers: start with a clean, completely dry surface, apply thin, even coats, and avoid puddles. Thick coats, damp pavers, or application in hot direct sun can cause the film to dry cloudy or white (often called "blushing"). The label gives the number of coats, coverage, recoat window, and temperature range — read it before you start. Check the label as well for whether SEK rates this product for joint sand stabilization; don't assume it does the SB-1300's job. Because darkening isn't reversible without stripping, always try a test patch on a spare paver or an out-of-the-way corner first.

Slip resistance is the practical consideration with any wet-look finish. On smooth or tumbled pavers, around pools, and on steps, a glossy film can be slicker when wet than bare pavers. If the manufacturer permits it, an anti-slip additive is worth considering for those areas. Durability depends on traffic and sun: expect to apply maintenance coats over time, following SEK's label guidance on timing and surface prep. If you'd rather avoid that maintenance entirely, a penetrating sealer like the SX5000 WB is the better fit — you just give up the color enhancement.

Paver Sealer Buying Guide

Penetrating vs. Film-Forming: The First Decision

Every product on this list falls into one of two camps, and choosing the right camp matters more than choosing the right brand. Penetrating sealers — the SX5000 WB and RadonSeal Plus here — soak into the paver and protect it from the inside. They leave the surface looking natural, don't change slip resistance, and have no film to peel or scuff, which makes them low-maintenance. Their limitation is cosmetic: they don't enhance color. Film-forming sealers — the SB-1300, SB-6000, Cryli-Tek 5500, and Glaze 'N Seal 173 — leave a thin layer at the surface. That layer is what delivers color enhancement, sheen, and (in the SB-1300's case) joint sand binding, but it also wears over time and needs maintenance coats. If you're not sure which camp you're in, ask yourself one question: do you want your pavers to look different after sealing? If not, choose a penetrating sealer.

Gloss Levels Explained: Natural, Matte, Low-Gloss, and Wet Look

Gloss level is one of the most important aesthetic choices in paver sealing, so think it through before you buy. Natural finishes (typical of penetrating sealers) leave pavers looking as they do when dry — no enhancement, no sheen, just protection. That's the right choice if you prefer an unaltered look, have natural stone with inherent beauty you don't want to change, or want to avoid any change in traction. Matte natural-look films, like the SB-1300, add surface protection and joint binding with minimal visual change. Low-gloss enhancers, like the Cryli-Tek 5500, deepen color with a restrained sheen that suits traditional and informal hardscapes. Wet-look finishes, like the SB-6000 and Glaze 'N Seal 173, produce the most noticeable color saturation and shine — striking on well-installed pavers, but potentially over-done on rustic installations.

Safety is a practical consideration in gloss selection: glossy film finishes on smooth pavers can be slippery when wet. For any surface that regularly gets wet — pool decks, areas near hose bibs or irrigation overspray, and steps — consider a penetrating or matte product, or add an anti-slip additive to a film-forming sealer if its manufacturer permits it.

Joint Sand: Ordinary Sand, Polymeric Sand, and Joint-Stabilizing Sealers

What's in your joints affects which sealer makes sense. If your joints are filled with ordinary jointing sand and you keep losing it to rain and blowers, a joint-stabilizing sealer such as the SB-1300 is designed to bind that sand as part of sealing. If your joints use polymeric sand, the sand itself already provides binding; in that case, follow the polymeric sand maker's waiting period before sealing and confirm that your chosen sealer is compatible. Penetrating sealers treat the paver body but don't bind joint sand at all, so if joint stability is your main problem, they won't solve it on their own.

Coverage Rates: How to Calculate What You Need

Coverage rates on sealer labels are usually given as a range, because porous surfaces absorb much more than dense ones. On first-time sealing of bare, porous pavers, expect coverage toward the lower end of the range. To estimate: measure your paver area in square feet, divide by the lower ("porous surface") coverage figure on the label, and add about 10% for waste and overlap. If the label calls for two coats, estimate each coat separately — the second coat often goes further because the surface is less absorbent after the first. For example, a 500 sq ft patio with a label figure of 200 sq ft per gallon for the first coat would need about 2.5 gallons for that coat before the waste allowance. It's better to have a little left over than to run out mid-application and leave a visible seam.

Sealer and Paver Material Compatibility

Not all sealers work equally well on all paver materials. Concrete pavers are the most forgiving and accept most sealer types. Clay brick pavers are more sensitive — penetrating silane/siloxane sealers are a common choice, while film-forming sealers can trap moisture and contribute to efflorescence on some brick, so test a small section first. Natural stone pavers (travertine, limestone, bluestone, flagstone) vary widely in porosity and mineral content, and film-forming sealers can haze or adhere poorly on some stones; impregnating sealers labeled for natural stone are usually the safest starting point. Reactive deep-penetrating products such as RadonSeal Plus are designed around cement-based materials. Whatever you choose, check the label's list of approved surfaces and test an inconspicuous area before committing to the whole job.

Who Should Buy Which Sealer

If you want your pavers to look exactly as they do now and need as little maintenance as possible, choose the Foundation Armor SX5000 WB. If you like the natural look but your joint sand keeps washing out, the SEK Surebond SB-1300 handles both jobs. If you want faded pavers to look rich and freshly wet, go with the SEK Surebond SB-6000, or the Glaze 'N Seal 173 if availability matters most. If you want a bit more color depth without obvious shine, the Ghostshield Cryli-Tek 5500 is the targeted choice. And if you have porous concrete pavers or an adjoining concrete driveway in a freeze-thaw climate, RadonSeal Plus offers invisible protection inside the concrete.

When to Seal New Pavers

The timing of initial sealing on a new installation depends on whether polymeric sand was used and whether efflorescence (white mineral deposits) has appeared. With polymeric sand, wait at least the period specified by the sand manufacturer so the binder can cure; sealing too early can cause cloudiness or poor adhesion. Without polymeric sand, wait until the pavers have cured and any initial efflorescence has been treated and rinsed. Sealing over active efflorescence can trap mineral salts under a film and cause persistent whitening, so treat it first with an efflorescence cleaner used according to its label, rinse thoroughly, and let the surface dry completely before sealing.

Application Best Practices for Water-Based Paver Sealers

Water-based sealers are easy to work with compared to solvent-based alternatives, but following best practices — and the specific label of the product you buy — makes the difference between a clean, even result and a blotchy one.

🌡️ Application Conditions: Follow the temperature range on your product's label, and plan for dry weather with no rain forecast for at least the label's stated dry time. Avoid applying film-forming sealers on very hot pavers or in direct midday sun, where they can dry too fast and look uneven; early morning or late afternoon is usually better in warm climates. The surface must be completely dry — after rain or cleaning, give it plenty of drying time, especially in humid weather.

Surface Preparation

Clean the entire surface with a paver cleaner or degreaser, scrubbing with a stiff brush to remove organic growth, dirt, and grease. Rinse thoroughly and allow complete drying. Treat any efflorescence with a product made for that purpose, following its label, then rinse and dry. Inspect joint sand — if it's low, replenish it before sealing (and before applying a joint-stabilizing sealer, make sure the joints are full and dry). If an old sealer is failing (chalking, peeling, delaminating), remove it with an appropriate stripper before applying new sealer, and remember that penetrating sealers require bare pavers.

Application Method

For most residential paver projects, a low-pressure pump sprayer or a roller gives the most efficient and even coverage; your label will specify which it prefers. Apply thin, even coats in overlapping passes, keeping a wet edge to avoid lap marks. For textured pavers, back-rolling or back-brushing helps work the sealer into recesses. Avoid puddling in low spots and on paver faces — work excess out before it dries. For multi-coat systems, wait the label's recoat time before applying the next coat, and apply it perpendicular to the first for even coverage. Joint-stabilizing sealers are the exception to "thin coats": they're applied so material reaches the joints, as the label directs.

Curing and Traffic Management

Dry times for foot and vehicle traffic vary by product, temperature, and humidity, so follow the label rather than a general rule of thumb. As a habit, keep furniture off newly sealed surfaces until the label says it's fully dry, avoid washing or pressure washing the surface for the period the manufacturer recommends, and avoid dragging heavy objects or making tight vehicle turns on a fresh film. Keep pets and children off the surface until it has dried.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are water-based paver sealers as good as solvent-based?

For many residential applications, a quality water-based sealer is a good choice — but "as good" depends on what you want. Water-based penetrating sealers like the Foundation Armor SX5000 WB use the same basic silane/siloxane chemistry that has long been used to protect masonry, and they offer lower odor and easier cleanup. For film-forming finishes, solvent-based acrylics still tend to give the deepest color and highest gloss and can be more forgiving in cool conditions, while water-based wet-look and low-gloss products such as the SEK Surebond SB-6000 and Ghostshield Cryli-Tek 5500 offer meaningful enhancement with lower VOCs. For typical patios, walkways, and pool decks, the trade-offs favor water-based products for most homeowners.

Can I apply a water-based sealer over an old solvent-based sealer?

Generally, no — this is one of the most common mistakes in paver resealing. Water-based acrylic sealers often don't bond reliably over solvent-based acrylic films, which can lead to peeling or clouding. Penetrating sealers can't reach the paver at all through an existing film. If your existing sealer is solvent-based, either strip it before switching to a water-based product or reseal with a compatible solvent-based product, and check the new sealer's label for its specific guidance on previously sealed surfaces. If you don't know what's on your pavers now, test a small, inconspicuous spot with a little xylene: solvent-based acrylics typically soften and become tacky, while water-based films and penetrating sealers typically don't react.

How long does a water-based paver sealer last?

It depends on the type of sealer, your climate, and how much traffic and sun the surface gets. Penetrating sealers (like the SX5000 WB and RadonSeal Plus) generally last longer than film-forming sealers because there's no surface film to wear away, while film-formers (like the SB-1300, SB-6000, Cryli-Tek 5500, and Glaze 'N Seal 173) need maintenance coats as they wear in traffic lanes and under strong UV. Each manufacturer states its own recoat guidance on the label, so use that as your starting point. To check whether your surface needs attention, do the water-bead test: if water poured on the surface beads up, the sealer is still working; if it soaks in quickly and darkens the paver, it's time to reapply.

Will a water-based sealer make my pavers slippery?

Penetrating sealers like the SX5000 WB and RadonSeal Plus leave no surface film, so they don't meaningfully change traction. Film-forming sealers can reduce traction compared to unsealed pavers, particularly wet-look finishes on smooth pavers. For any surface that regularly gets wet — pool decks, areas near fountains or water features, and steps — choose a penetrating or matte product, or add an anti-slip additive to a film-forming sealer if the sealer's manufacturer permits it, following the additive maker's mixing instructions. Be especially cautious on steps, where a slick finish creates real slip-and-fall risk.

Do I need to seal pavers? What happens if I don't?

Pavers can survive unsealed for many years — paving brick and concrete pavers were around long before modern sealers. But sealing offers benefits many homeowners value: enhanced color (with film-forming products), reduced weed and moss growth, protection against oil and food stains, reduced water absorption in freeze-thaw climates, and — with a joint-stabilizing sealer like the SB-1300 — better joint sand retention. Unsealed pavers in humid, shaded areas tend to grow algae and moss more readily, and unsealed pavers near outdoor kitchens and dining areas stain more easily. Sealing isn't mandatory, but for many homeowners it's a worthwhile maintenance step.

Which water-based sealer should I choose for a wet look?

For a full wet look, the SEK Surebond SB-6000 is our first choice because it's a paver-focused, darkening wet-look product; the Glaze 'N Seal 173 Clear Wet Look WB Sealer is a widely available alternative. If you want enhancement with less shine, the Ghostshield Cryli-Tek 5500 offers a low-gloss wet look. Whichever you choose, try a test patch first — darkening is hard to undo — and think about slip resistance on pool decks and steps.

How do I remove a failing water-based paver sealer?

Failing water-based acrylic sealers are typically removed with a stripper formulated for acrylic or water-based coatings. Apply it according to its label, allow the specified dwell time, scrub with a stiff brush, and rinse thoroughly — some strippers recommend a pressure washer for the rinse. Older, thicker build-ups may need more than one application. Follow the stripper's instructions for neutralizing and rinsing, and let the surface dry completely before resealing. Check the stripper's label to confirm it's appropriate for the type of sealer you're removing and for your paver material, and test it on a small area first.
