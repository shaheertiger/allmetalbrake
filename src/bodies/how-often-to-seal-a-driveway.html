How Often Should You Seal Your Driveway? Less Often Than You Think

The yearly sealcoat is a habit, not a requirement — and on most asphalt it does more harm than good. Here are the real intervals for asphalt, concrete and pavers, and how to tell when yours is actually due.

Seal an asphalt driveway every two to three years, not every year. Seal concrete every five to ten years if you use a penetrating silane or siloxane sealer, or every two to three years if you use a film-forming acrylic. Seal pavers every three to five years. Those are starting points; the right number for your driveway moves with how much sun it gets, how many vehicles use it, the climate, and which sealer went down last time.

The most common mistake is not sealing too rarely — it is sealing too often. A sealcoat is meant to wear away in the traffic lanes before the next one goes on. Recoat while the last layer is still intact and the coats stack into a thick, brittle film that cracks, peels and turns slippery when wet, and you end up paying to fix a problem the sealer created.

⚡ The direct answer: Asphalt — every 2–3 years for most homes; stretch to 3–4 on a shaded, lightly used driveway, tighten to about 2 in hot full sun or with heavy traffic. Never annually as a matter of routine — over-sealing builds a film that cracks, flakes and gets slick. Concrete — 5–10 years with a penetrating silane/siloxane, 2–3 years with an acrylic film sealer. Pavers — 3–5 years. New asphalt gets its first coat at 6–12 months, not sooner. The real trigger is condition, not the calendar: reseal when water stops beading and the surface has faded to grey.

Asphalt: Every 2–3 Years, and Why Not Every Year

Asphalt pavement is stone aggregate held together by a petroleum binder. That binder is what makes the surface flexible and waterproof, and it is also what the sun attacks. Ultraviolet light and oxygen gradually harden the binder — the process is called oxidation — which is why a driveway fades from black to grey and why old asphalt turns brittle and starts shedding stones. A sealcoat is a sacrificial layer that takes that UV and weather exposure instead of the pavement, and fills the fine surface pores so water and oil cannot soak in.

Because it is sacrificial, a sealcoat is supposed to wear. On a typical residential driveway, a properly applied two-coat job is visibly worn in the tyre tracks somewhere between year two and year three, while the edges and the parts nobody drives on still look reasonably dark. That pattern is the sealer doing its job, and it is the signal to reseal. The number of years it takes to get there is the honest answer to "how often" for your particular driveway.

The annual habit comes partly from contractors — sealcoating is a repeat-business trade, and a yearly visit is good business — and partly from the satisfaction of a freshly black driveway. Neither is a reason to do it. Industry guidance from sealer manufacturers and pavement associations converges on the same two-to-three-year range for residential asphalt, with yearly sealing reserved for commercial lots that see hundreds of vehicles a day. If you want the wider case for and against sealing at all, our guide to the pros and cons of sealing an asphalt driveway covers it.

What Moves the Number

Sun exposure. The single biggest variable. A south-facing driveway in Arizona or Texas with no shade oxidizes far faster than a north-facing one under trees in Oregon. Full, all-day sun pushes the interval toward two years; heavy shade can stretch it to four.

Traffic. One car leaving in the morning and returning at night is light use. Three cars, a delivery van, a boat trailer and teenagers doing three-point turns is heavy use. Turning tyres scrub sealer off far faster than straight-line rolling, so a driveway with a turnaround wears fastest at the turn.

Climate. Hot climates shorten intervals through UV and heat. Cold climates with repeated freeze-thaw cycles are hard on the pavement itself, but the threat there is water entering cracks, so crack filling matters more than the sealcoat frequency. Snowplough blades and shovel edges also scrape sealer off in winter.

Sealer type. Refined-tar (coal-tar) sealers have historically lasted longest, often three to five years, but they are banned or restricted in a growing number of states and municipalities because of their polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon content. Asphalt-emulsion sealers — the most common retail type — usually last two to three years. Cheap, thin, fast-dry formulas may last one. Premium acrylic and polymer-modified products tend to sit at the upper end of the emulsion range. The differences between solvent and emulsion chemistries are covered in our oil-based vs water-based driveway sealer comparison.

Application quality. Two thin coats outlast one thick one, and a sealer applied to a dirty, oily or damp surface fails early regardless of brand. A job squeezed in on a cold evening will not cure properly either — timing matters as much as the product.

What Over-Sealing Does

Over-sealing is the most common sealcoating problem on residential driveways, and it is entirely self-inflicted. Here is what happens when coats go on faster than they wear off.

The film builds up. Each coat adds thickness. On untrafficked areas — the edges, the apron by the garage, the strip beside the lawn — nothing wears it away, so after four or five annual coats those areas carry a thick crust of sealer sitting on top of the pavement rather than protecting it.

Thick film cracks. Sealer is far less flexible than the asphalt beneath it. A thin coat moves with the pavement as it expands in heat and contracts in cold. A thick film cannot, so it develops a network of fine cracks — often mistaken for cracking in the asphalt itself — that lets water in underneath.

It peels and flakes. Once water gets beneath a thick film, the layers start to separate from each other and from the pavement. Patches lift and flake, leaving a blotchy mix of black and grey that looks worse than an unsealed driveway and has to be scraped or wire-brushed back before anything can be applied.

It gets slippery. Sealer fills the texture between the aggregate. A thin coat leaves enough of the stone profile for grip; stacked coats bury it under a smooth, glossy skin that is genuinely slick when wet and worse with leaves or frost on it. Some sealers include sand for traction, which helps, but stacking coats still smooths the surface out over time.

If you are already in this position, stop sealing. Let the traffic lanes wear back, wire-brush or scrape any peeling areas to a sound surface, and when you do reseal, do only the worn sections or apply a single thin coat over the whole thing. Some homeowners with heavy traffic but quiet edges seal just the tyre tracks in the off years — a perfectly reasonable compromise that avoids build-up where it causes most of the trouble.

💡 The fingernail test. Press a fingernail or coin edge into the sealer on an edge strip nobody drives on. If it leaves a dent in a soft, rubbery layer, or flakes off in a sheet you can see the thickness of, there is already more than enough sealer there. Healthy sealcoat is a thin, hard skin barely distinguishable from the pavement.

Concrete and Pavers

Concrete: Choose the Sealer, and the Interval Follows

Concrete does not oxidize the way asphalt does, so its sealer is not shielding the binder from UV. It is keeping water, de-icing salt and oil out of the pores. Water that soaks in and freezes causes scaling and spalling — the surface flaking away in patches — and salt makes that dramatically worse. How often you reseal depends almost entirely on which kind of sealer you choose.

Penetrating silane and siloxane sealers soak into the concrete and react inside the pores to form a water-repellent zone a few millimetres deep. Nothing sits on the surface, so tyres have nothing to wear off, there is no film to peel, and the slip resistance and appearance of the concrete are unchanged. They last five to ten years, with higher-solids silane products at the long end. They are the right default for most driveways, especially in freeze-thaw climates.

Acrylic film sealers form a thin coat on top, which is what gives them a darker, enhanced or wet look. That film is exposed to UV and abrasion exactly as an asphalt sealcoat is, so it wears in two to three years, and the same over-sealing rules apply: stacked acrylic coats go cloudy, peel and get slippery. Choose them for the appearance, not the longevity.

Epoxy and polyurethane coatings belong in garages rather than on exterior driveways; epoxy yellows in sunlight and both trap moisture under a non-breathable film. If you are starting from scratch, our guide to sealing a concrete driveway walks through the preparation, and our concrete driveway sealer reviews compare penetrating and film-forming products side by side. New concrete should cure for at least 28 days before any sealer goes on.

Pavers: 3–5 Years, Joints Included

Paver sealer protects the face of the pavers from staining and fading and, just as importantly, stabilizes the joint sand so it does not wash out or sprout weeds. Plan on three to five years. Film-forming wet-look sealers sit at the shorter end because the film wears; penetrating, natural-look paver sealers tend to last longer and fade more gracefully. On a new installation, wait until any efflorescence — the white, chalky bloom common in the first months — has worked its way out before sealing, or you lock it under the film.

Signs Your Driveway Is Due

The calendar is a guide; the surface tells you when it is actually time. Check in late spring or early summer, when the driveway is dry and the weather is good for sealing anyway.

Water no longer beads. The single best test. Pour or sprinkle a cupful of water on the traffic lanes. On a protected surface it beads or sits on top for a while. If it darkens the surface immediately and soaks in within a minute, the pores are open and water is getting into the pavement. This works on asphalt, concrete and pavers alike, and it is the most reliable signal for penetrating concrete sealers, which do not change the look of the surface at all.

The colour has faded to grey. On asphalt, fresh sealer is deep black. As it wears, the traffic lanes go charcoal and then grey, and the aggregate stones start to show their own colour. When the tyre tracks are clearly greyer than the edges, the sealcoat is gone where it matters. On concrete and pavers with film sealers, the equivalent is a loss of the enhanced colour or sheen.

Fine surface cracks are appearing. Hairline cracks and a fine network of shallow surface cracking in asphalt indicate the binder is drying out. Sealing at this stage, after filling the cracks, slows the process considerably. Wide or deep cracks are a different matter — see below.

Ravelling has started. Ravelling is the loss of fine aggregate from the surface: you notice loose grit and small stones on the driveway, and the surface texture gets rougher and more open. It is oxidation in its later stages. Early ravelling is a strong signal to seal now; widespread, deep ravelling means the surface has worn past what a sealer can bind back together.

Oil and stains soak in rather than sitting on top. Drips that leave a permanent dark mark within hours, rather than wiping off, tell you the surface is unprotected.

If none of these apply, wait another season even if it has been three years. If two or more apply after eighteen months, your conditions are tough and two years is your interval.

The First Seal on a New Asphalt Driveway

Fresh asphalt is still releasing the lighter oils in its binder, and it needs to do that before it is sealed. Sealing too soon traps those oils, which keeps the pavement soft — prone to scuffing, tyre marks and indentations from car jacks and kickstands — and can stop the sealcoat bonding properly. The standard guidance is to wait six to twelve months after paving: closer to six in a hot climate where the oils cure out quickly, closer to twelve in a cool one or for a driveway paved late in the season. A practical test is to pour water on it: when the water beads less and the surface no longer has a fresh, oily sheen, it is ready.

Our full guide on when to seal a new asphalt driveway covers the timing in detail. After that first coat, the normal two-to-three-year cycle begins. The first seal also matters disproportionately: getting a new driveway sealed once in its first year or so, before oxidation gets going, is a large part of what separates a 25-year driveway from a 12-year one.

DIY asphalt costs are materials plus a squeegee, a broom, crack filler and a cleaner. Most retail asphalt-emulsion sealers cover roughly 250–400 square feet per five-gallon pail per coat, so two coats on 600 square feet is four or five pails. The point of the table: sealing an asphalt driveway every year roughly doubles or triples the annual cost compared with a two-to-three-year cycle, and the extra money buys a worse result. The penetrating concrete sealer has the lowest cost per year of anything here, which is the clearest argument for choosing it over an acrylic on a plain concrete driveway.

The comparison that matters most, though, is against replacement. A new asphalt driveway typically costs several thousand dollars, and resurfacing a few thousand. If sealing every two to three years and filling cracks every year adds even five years to a driveway's life, it has paid for itself many times over.

Maintenance Between Seals

The work between sealcoats does more to extend driveway life than the sealcoat itself, and it takes an afternoon a year.

Fill cracks every year. Water entering cracks is what actually destroys driveways: it softens the base beneath and, in cold climates, freezes and widens the crack every winter. Fill every crack wider than about 1/8 inch each year, ideally in late spring or early autumn when the cracks are mid-width. Pourable or squeeze-bottle rubberized fillers handle cracks up to about 1/2 inch; wider ones need backer rod first, or a cold-patch product. Our asphalt crack filler guide compares the options. On concrete, use a flexible polyurethane or silicone-based crack sealant rather than a rigid patch, so the joint can still move.

Deal with oil spots promptly. Petroleum products dissolve asphalt binder — gasoline, oil and transmission fluid soften the pavement under a persistent drip and eventually leave a crumbly hollow. Blot fresh spills with cat litter or an absorbent, then scrub with a driveway degreaser. Before resealing, treat any remaining stains with an oil-spot primer so the new sealer bonds. On concrete, an oil stain will not damage the slab structurally, but it will stop a sealer penetrating, so clean it out first. A drip tray under a leaking car is the cheapest fix of all.

Keep it clean. Sweep or blow off leaves, pine needles and grass clippings, which hold moisture against the surface and stain. Rinse off fertilizer granules and remove moss and weeds from cracks and edges before they widen them.

Manage water. Point downspouts away from the driveway, keep edges graded so water drains off rather than pooling against the side, and clear ice dams or snow piles that sit on the surface for weeks. Asphalt edges with no support crumble; backfilling them with soil or gravel helps.

Avoid point loads and hot-day scuffing. Kickstands, trailer jacks, ramps and dumpster feet punch dents into asphalt on hot days; use a board under them. Turning the steering wheel while stationary scrubs the surface, especially in summer.

Go easy with de-icers on concrete. Rock salt is hard on concrete under a year old, and products based on ammonium nitrate or ammonium sulphate attack concrete chemically at any age. Sand for traction is the gentlest option.

When Sealing No Longer Helps

Sealer is preventive maintenance for sound pavement. It cannot bind together pavement that has structurally failed, and sealing a driveway in that state is money spent on appearance only. These are the signs you are past the point where sealer helps.

Alligator cracking. A pattern of interconnected cracks forming small, many-sided blocks that look like reptile skin. It is a fatigue failure of the pavement and almost always means the base underneath has lost its support, usually because water got in. Filling and sealing over it hides it for a season; the cracks return because the cause is below the surface.

Potholes, depressions and rutting. Sunken areas, tyre ruts and dished spots that collect water indicate the base has settled or washed out.

Wide or spreading cracks. Cracks wider than about an inch, cracks with edges that crumble when you fill them, or cracks with a vertical step between the two sides point to base movement rather than surface ageing.

Advanced ravelling. When stones come away across the whole surface and the pavement crumbles at the edges, the binder has oxidized through. See our guide to fixing a crumbling asphalt driveway for what can be patched and what cannot.

The options from here depend on how much is affected. Isolated failure — one alligatored patch where water pooled, a pothole by the garage — is fixed by cutting out the failed asphalt and the soft base beneath, recompacting new base, and patching with hot-mix asphalt; then the rest of the driveway can go back on a normal sealing cycle. Widespread surface failure over a sound base calls for resurfacing: a new 1.5–2 inch layer of asphalt over the old after milling or repairs. Widespread base failure means full removal and replacement. A rough rule: if more than a quarter of the driveway shows structural distress, or it is past 20 years and grey and crumbling throughout, get resurfacing and replacement quotes rather than buying more sealer.

💡 The heel test for structural damage. Push down hard with your heel on a cracked area on a warm day. If it flexes, feels spongy, or pieces shift under your foot, the base has failed and no surface product will help. Sound pavement with surface cracks feels solid.

The Short Version

Asphalt: first coat six to twelve months after paving, then every two to three years. Fill cracks every year. Reseal when water stops beading and the traffic lanes have faded to grey, not because a year has passed. If the edges are building up thick sealer, seal only the traffic lanes next time. When you do reseal, our step-by-step guide to sealing an asphalt driveway covers cleaning, priming and the two-thin-coats method, and checking the right temperature to seal a driveway keeps the coat from failing before it cures.

Concrete: wait 28 days after pouring, then use a penetrating silane/siloxane and reapply every five to ten years, or an acrylic every two to three if you want the enhanced look.

Pavers: every three to five years, after efflorescence has cleared on a new install.

Everything: the water-beading test beats the calendar. And once alligator cracking, potholes or base failure show up, stop spending on sealer and start pricing repair or resurfacing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should you seal an asphalt driveway?

Every two to three years for most homes. Stretch it to three or four years for a shaded, lightly used driveway in a mild climate, and tighten it to about two years for a driveway in full sun, in a hot climate, or with several vehicles and a lot of turning. The better trigger than the calendar is condition: when water soaks in rather than beading and the tyre tracks have faded to grey, it is time. Sealing every year, without that wear, builds up a thick film that cracks, peels and gets slippery.

Is it bad to seal your driveway every year?

On a residential driveway, usually yes. Sealcoat is meant to wear away in the traffic lanes before the next coat goes on. Sealing every year while the previous coat is still intact stacks layers, especially on edges and areas nobody drives over, and that thick film is far less flexible than the asphalt underneath. It develops fine cracks, lets water in beneath it, peels and flakes, and smooths over the surface texture so the driveway becomes slippery when wet. If your tyre tracks genuinely wear out in a year, seal just those lanes in between full coats.

How often should you seal a concrete driveway?

It depends on the sealer. Penetrating silane and siloxane sealers soak into the concrete and form a water-repellent zone below the surface; with nothing on top to wear off, they last five to ten years and are the best choice for most driveways, particularly in freeze-thaw climates where salt and water cause scaling. Acrylic film sealers sit on the surface, give a darker or wet look, and wear in two to three years from UV and tyres. Test by sprinkling water on the slab: if it darkens and soaks in immediately, the sealer has worn out.

How do I know when my driveway needs sealing?

Sprinkle water on the traffic lanes. If it beads or sits on the surface, the sealer is still working. If it darkens the surface and soaks in within about a minute, the pores are open and it is time. On asphalt, other signs are the tyre tracks fading from black to charcoal or grey, fine hairline surface cracks, loose grit and small stones appearing on the surface (ravelling), and oil drips leaving permanent stains within hours. If none of these show, wait another season even if the usual interval has passed.

How long should you wait to seal a new asphalt driveway?

Six to twelve months after paving. Fresh asphalt is still releasing light oils from its binder, and sealing too soon traps them, leaving the pavement soft and prone to scuffs, tyre marks and dents, and preventing the sealer from bonding well. In hot climates the oils cure out faster, so six months is often enough; in cool climates, or for a driveway paved in autumn, wait closer to twelve and seal the following summer. The surface is ready when it has lost its fresh, oily sheen and water no longer beads strongly on it.

How often should pavers be sealed?

Every three to five years. Film-forming wet-look sealers are at the shorter end because the film wears under tyres and sun; penetrating natural-look sealers usually last longer and fade gradually rather than peeling. On a new installation, wait until any white efflorescence has worked out of the pavers — often a few months — before the first coat, otherwise the haze is locked in under the sealer.

Should I fill cracks before sealing, and how often?

Yes, every time, and ideally every year whether or not you are sealing that year. Water entering cracks is what actually destroys driveways, softening the base and, in cold climates, freezing and widening the crack each winter. Fill cracks wider than about 1/8 inch with a rubberized pourable or squeeze-bottle filler, using backer rod or cold patch for cracks wider than about 1/2 inch, and let the filler cure according to the label before sealing over it.

When is a driveway too far gone to seal?

When the damage is structural rather than surface ageing. Alligator cracking — interconnected cracks forming small blocks like reptile skin — almost always means the base below has failed. Potholes, sunken areas, ruts, cracks wider than about an inch, and widespread ravelling where stones come away across the whole surface are the other signs. Sealer cannot bind failed pavement back together. Isolated failures can be cut out, the base rebuilt and the area patched with hot-mix asphalt; widespread surface failure over a sound base calls for a 1.5–2 inch resurfacing overlay; widespread base failure means replacement. If more than about a quarter of the driveway is affected, get resurfacing quotes instead of buying sealer.
