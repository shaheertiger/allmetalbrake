The best laminate flooring for most homes is an AC4-rated, 10–12 mm click-lock plank from a line sold as waterproof, with an attached pad or a quality separate underlayment. Pergo Outlast+ is the safest all-round choice for busy households; Mohawk RevWood Plus is the pick for homes with dogs and frequent spills; Mannington Restoration Collection has the most convincing wood visuals. AC3 is fine for bedrooms. Below AC3 is not worth buying for anywhere that gets real traffic.

The thing that matters most after the AC rating is the water claim, and it is the most misunderstood spec in the category. "Waterproof laminate" means the surface and joints resist topical water — spills, pet accidents, damp mopping — for a stated time. It does not mean the floor survives a leaking dishwasher or a flooded basement, and every warranty in this category is written around that distinction.

⚡ The quick answer: For scratch-resistant laminate collections for busy homes, buy AC4 for kitchens, hallways and anywhere with pets or kids; AC3 is enough for bedrooms. Choose 10–12 mm for a solid, quieter feel; 7–8 mm is a budget compromise. Pick a line marketed as waterproof for kitchens and entries, and read what its warranty requires. Budget roughly $1–$5 per sq ft for materials and $3–$10 per sq ft installed, before removal and subfloor prep. Use vinyl, not laminate, in full bathrooms and damp basements.

AC Ratings Explained

The AC (abrasion class) rating comes from the European laminate standard EN 13329, and North American manufacturers certified through NALFA certify to comparable performance requirements. The rating combines several measures — abrasion with a Taber machine, impact, stain resistance, swelling at the edges, and burn resistance — and the lowest result sets the class. It describes the melamine wear surface, not the core or the joints.

RatingIntended useWhere it makes sense at home
AC1Moderate residentialClosets, guest rooms — rarely sold now
AC2General residentialBedrooms with light traffic
AC3Heavy residential / moderate commercialBedrooms, offices, living rooms without pets
AC4General commercialKitchens, halls, entries, homes with pets and kids
AC5Heavy commercialOverkill for most homes; harder and often louder

For a home, the practical decision is AC3 or AC4. AC4 costs a little more and is the right answer for any room people walk through with outdoor shoes or where a dog runs. AC5 is a commercial product; it does not make a noticeable difference at home and the selection of styles is much smaller. If a product lists no AC rating at all, assume it is AC3 or lower and price it accordingly.

Note that AC ratings are a laminate standard. When you see one on a vinyl listing, it is borrowed terminology — vinyl is judged on wear layer thickness in mils instead, which our luxury vinyl plank guide explains.

Thickness vs Feel Underfoot

Laminate thickness is usually quoted for the plank itself, from about 7 mm to 12 mm, with a few 14–15 mm products. Some listings include the attached pad in the figure, which flatters thinner planks, so check whether "12 mm" means 12 mm of board or 10 mm plus a 2 mm pad.

Thickness does not make the surface more scratch-resistant — that is the AC rating's job. What it changes is how the floor feels and sounds:

  • 7–8 mm: the budget band. Lighter, cheaper, and more likely to sound hollow and telegraph subfloor dips. Acceptable in bedrooms over a good underlayment.
  • 10 mm: the sweet spot for most homes. Noticeably more solid, with stronger locking profiles and better tolerance of a slightly imperfect subfloor.
  • 12 mm: the most wood-like feel and sound, room for deeper bevels and embossing, and the best at bridging minor dips. Worth it for open living areas.

Thicker planks also need a little more height at door thresholds and transitions, which matters where laminate meets tile or an existing hardwood floor. Plan transitions before you buy.

Water-Resistant vs Waterproof Laminate

Laminate's core is high-density fibreboard — compressed wood fibre. Older laminate swelled at the edges if a spill sat in the joints, and that swelling is permanent. Modern lines attack the problem in three ways: water-repellent treatment of the core, sealed or coated plank edges, and tighter locking joints that keep water on the surface. The marketing then splits into two camps.

Water-resistant laminate is warranted against topical spills for a limited period if they are cleaned up — the window varies by brand and collection. Waterproof laminate is warranted against topical water damage from spills, pet accidents and normal cleaning, often for longer, sometimes with no stated time limit on surface water. Neither means the floor can sit in water.

💡 What laminate water warranties typically require. Installation exactly per the instructions, including expansion gaps; in some lines, sealing the perimeter with silicone in kitchens, laundry rooms and bathrooms where they are permitted; cleaning up water within the stated window; and using the manufacturer's approved cleaning method. They typically exclude flooding, plumbing and appliance leaks, water from below, subfloor moisture, and damage from steam mops. Read the warranty for the exact collection before you buy — it is the spec that matters.

The practical takeaway: waterproof laminate is good in kitchens, entries and homes with pets, where water arrives from above in small amounts. It is not a substitute for vinyl in a full bathroom or a below-grade basement, where water can come from below or sit for hours. For kitchens specifically, our guide to the best vinyl flooring for kitchens covers the case where vinyl is the better call.

Scratch Resistance, Pets and Kids

Scratch resistance is laminate's strongest argument. The wear layer is a melamine resin surface reinforced with aluminium oxide, and it is considerably harder than the urethane finish on most hardwood or the wear layer on most vinyl. An AC4 laminate shrugs off dog claws, toy cars and chair legs that would mark other wood-look floors.

A few choices make the difference between a floor that looks new for years and one that shows every mark:

  • Texture and sheen: embossed, matte surfaces hide fine scratches; smooth, glossy surfaces show them.
  • Colour: mid-tone browns and greys with visible grain hide scratches, dust and pet hair best. Very dark and very light solid colours show everything.
  • Grip: smooth laminate can be slippery for older dogs. A textured surface and runners in main paths help.
  • Furniture: felt pads on every chair and table leg, and wide furniture cups under heavy pieces.
  • Grit control: a doormat at every exterior door. Tracked-in sand does more damage than claws.

Laminate does not dent like vinyl, but it can chip at the edge if something heavy and sharp lands on it. For a broader comparison across floor types for pets, including where tile or vinyl wins, see our guide to the best flooring for dogs.

Underlayment and Attached Pads

Laminate needs an underlayment to cushion the plank, absorb minor subfloor irregularities and reduce the hard, hollow sound laminate is known for. Laminate pads differ from vinyl pads, and the two are not interchangeable: many rigid-core vinyl manufacturers forbid soft foam that laminate tolerates, and their thin pads are tuned for a different plank. Our guide to underlayment for vinyl plank covers the vinyl rules; for laminate, the logic is:

  • Attached pad: do not add a second cushioning underlayment on top of it. Doubling up makes the floor bouncy, stresses the locks and usually voids the warranty.
  • Over concrete: lay a 6-mil polyethylene vapour barrier with taped, overlapped seams, unless the attached pad or underlayment has an integrated barrier the manufacturer approves for slabs.
  • No attached pad: a 2–3 mm foam or combination foam-and-film underlayment is standard; premium acoustic underlayments made of dense foam, rubber or cork reduce noise.
  • Upstairs and condos: an acoustic underlayment with published sound ratings, and check building rules — many condos specify minimum ratings.

Always use what the plank's installation guide approves. An unapproved pad is one of the easiest ways to lose warranty coverage.

Installation Basics and Acclimation

Laminate is one of the most DIY-friendly floors: click-lock planks float on the underlayment with no glue or nails. Most installation problems trace back to a handful of steps.

Acclimation. Many manufacturers specify leaving the unopened boxes flat in the room for 48 hours at normal living conditions, roughly 60–80°F and 35–65% relative humidity. Some modern lines have shortened or dropped the requirement; follow the guide for your product. In a damp space, run a dehumidifier before and during installation.

Subfloor. It must be flat, dry, clean and sound. A common tolerance is within 3/16 inch over 10 feet. Fill dips with floor patch, sand or grind high spots, and fix squeaks before the floor goes down. On concrete, test for moisture first and, if the slab is damp, sort out the cause — our guide on how to fix a damp basement covers the usual culprits.

Expansion gaps. Laminate moves with humidity. Leave the gap the guide specifies — usually 1/4 to 3/8 inch — at walls, pipes, door frames and fixed objects, hidden by baseboard or quarter-round. Large rooms and long runs may need a transition strip; check the maximum run length for your product.

No pinning. Do not nail baseboard into the floor, and do not set kitchen islands or built-in cabinets on top of a floating floor. Install those first and run the laminate up to them.

Layout. Stagger end joints by at least 8–12 inches, mix planks from several boxes to avoid pattern repeats, undercut door casings so the floor slides under, and order around 10% extra for cuts and waste.

Where Not to Use Laminate

  • Full bathrooms with a shower or tub: even waterproof laminate is a poor bet where water pools; many manufacturers exclude or restrict them. Use vinyl or tile.
  • Below-grade basements: slabs can push moisture up from below, which no topical warranty covers. Choose SPC vinyl.
  • Laundry rooms and mudrooms with standing water: a washer leak or snowmelt sitting overnight can swell the core.
  • Unconditioned spaces: sunrooms, cabins and garages with wide temperature and humidity swings move the floor beyond its gaps.
  • Under fixed cabinetry or heavy islands: pinning a floating floor causes buckling.
  • Over carpet or soft, uneven subfloors: the locking joints fail.
  • Where you want to refinish: laminate cannot be sanded. If refinishing matters, look at real wood.

The Short Version

For a busy household's main floor, buy Pergo Outlast+: AC4, 10 mm with an attached pad and a waterproof system built for everyday spills. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best laminate flooring brand?

Pergo, Mohawk, Mannington and Shaw are the most established national brands, and all make good laminate at the top of their ranges. Pergo Outlast+ is the most dependable all-rounder for busy homes, Mohawk RevWood Plus suits pets and spills, and Mannington Restoration Collection has the most realistic visuals. Brand matters less than the specs on the exact collection: an AC4 rating for kitchens, halls and pets, 10–12 mm thickness for a solid feel, and a water rating whose warranty terms you have read. A brand's entry-level collection can be weaker than a store brand's best.

What AC rating do I need for laminate flooring?

AC3 for bedrooms, home offices and living rooms without pets; AC4 for kitchens, hallways, entries and any home with dogs or young children. AC4 costs only slightly more and is the right default for main living areas. AC5 is a heavy-commercial rating that makes little practical difference in a house and narrows your style choices. If a laminate lists no AC rating, assume AC3 or lower. The rating describes the wear surface's resistance to abrasion, impact, stains and edge swelling under EN 13329 — it does not describe the core or how much water the joints can take.

Is waterproof laminate really waterproof?

It is waterproof against water from above, under conditions the warranty sets. Waterproof lines combine a water-repellent core treatment, sealed edges and tight joints so spills, pet accidents and mopping do not reach the fibreboard core. Warranties typically require installation exactly per the instructions, prompt cleanup within any stated window, and in some cases perimeter sealing in wet rooms, and they exclude flooding, appliance or plumbing leaks, water from below and subfloor moisture. That makes it a strong choice for kitchens and entries, and a poor one for full bathrooms and below-grade basements, where vinyl is safer.

Is laminate or vinyl plank better for dogs?

Laminate is better at resisting claw scratches because its AC4 melamine surface is harder than most vinyl wear layers. Vinyl plank is better at handling pet accidents, because its core is fully waterproof and urine that sits in a joint will not swell it. For most dog owners, a waterproof AC4 laminate with a textured, mid-tone surface is an excellent choice for living areas; if accidents are frequent or the dog is elderly, vinyl or a mix of both makes sense. Textured surfaces also give dogs better grip than smooth, glossy planks.

Is thicker laminate better?

Thicker laminate feels and sounds more like real wood and bridges minor subfloor dips better, but it is not more scratch-resistant — that depends on the AC rating. A 10 mm plank is the sweet spot for most homes; 12 mm gives the most solid feel and allows deeper bevels; 7–8 mm is a budget option for bedrooms. Check whether the quoted thickness includes an attached pad, because some listings add the pad to the figure. Thicker planks also raise floor height slightly at doorways and transitions.

Do I need underlayment for laminate with an attached pad?

Not a cushioning one. Adding foam under a plank that already has an attached pad makes the floor bouncy, stresses the locking joints and usually voids the warranty. Over concrete, you typically still need a 6-mil polyethylene vapour barrier underneath unless the manufacturer says the attached pad includes an approved barrier. Planks without an attached pad need a laminate underlayment, typically 2–3 mm foam or a foam-and-film combination over concrete, or a denser acoustic product upstairs. Laminate and vinyl pads are not interchangeable, so use what the plank's guide approves.

How much does laminate flooring cost per square foot?

Roughly $1–$2 per square foot in materials for budget AC3 laminate, $2–$3.50 for mid-range water-resistant lines, and $3–$5 for premium AC4 waterproof collections. Professional installation adds about $2–$5 per square foot, bringing installed costs to roughly $3–$10. Budget separately for old floor removal, underlayment if there is no attached pad, levelling compound, trim and transitions, and order around 10% extra for waste. The mid-to-premium tier offers the best value for busy homes, because the move to AC4 and a water rating costs little relative to what it adds.

Does laminate need to acclimate before installation?

Many manufacturers specify about 48 hours with the unopened boxes lying flat in the room where they will be installed, at normal living temperature and humidity — roughly 60–80°F and 35–65% relative humidity. Some newer lines have shortened or removed the requirement, so follow the installation guide for your product, because it is a warranty condition. More important than the hours is that the room is at its normal conditions: heating or air-conditioning running, paint and drywall dry, and any damp problem fixed before the floor goes in.
