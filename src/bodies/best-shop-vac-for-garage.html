Best Shop Vac for Garage — Buy on Suction and Hose, Not Peak HP

Every box shouts horsepower, and the number tells you almost nothing. Sealed suction, airflow, hose diameter and the filter decide whether a wet/dry vac cleans up a garage, a flooded basement or a ground slab — here is how to match one to the job.

For most garages the right shop vac is a 12- to 16-gallon wet/dry vac with a drain port, a 2-1/2-inch hose for floors, and a fine-dust filter or bag — something in the class of the Ridgid 16-gallon NXT or the Craftsman 16-gallon. It handles sawdust, leaves blown in under the door, a burst water heater and the occasional basement seep. What it handles badly is fine dust from drywall sanding or concrete grinding; for that you fit a cyclone separator in front of it or use a true dust extractor.

The most useful thing to know before shopping is that the "peak horsepower" on every box is close to meaningless. A 5 HP vac with a wide hose can out-clean a 6.5 HP vac with a narrow one. Sealed suction, airflow, hose diameter and filter decide performance — and the job you are buying for decides which of those to prioritise.

⚡ The short answer: General garage and workshop cleanup — a 16-gallon wet/dry vac with a drain port, a 2-1/2-inch hose for floors and a dust bag fitted. Drywall dust or grinding a slab for epoxy — a HEPA dust extractor with automatic filter cleaning, or at minimum a shop vac with a cyclone separator and HEPA filter. A flooded basement — a pump-out vac or a utility pump, not repeated lifting of a 100-lb-plus tank. Car interiors and quick spills — a small or cordless vac you will actually get out.

Why "Peak HP" Is Meaningless

Peak horsepower is typically derived from testing the bare motor, outside the vacuum, at a momentary load point it never reaches in normal use. It is not what the motor delivers while running, and it is not a measure of suction.

The arithmetic makes the point. A 120-volt, 15-amp household circuit supplies at most about 1,800 watts. One horsepower is 746 watts, so no plug-in vac can draw much more than 2.4 horsepower continuously — and most shop vacs run at 10–12 amps, well under that. A box that says 6.5 peak HP describes a figure the motor never sustains on any circuit in your house.

Within one brand, peak HP roughly ranks motor size. Across brands it is unreliable, and it says nothing about how hard the vac pulls on a blocked nozzle or how much air it moves through an open one.

The Specs That Actually Matter

Sealed Suction (Water Lift)

Sealed suction is measured by blocking the inlet and recording how high the vacuum lifts a column of water, in inches. It describes pull against resistance: heavy debris such as gravel and screws, water drawn up a long hose, grit packed into the joints of a slab. Large mains-powered shop vacs typically sit somewhere around 50 to 80 inches; small and cordless models lower. Prioritise it for water and heavy material.

Airflow (CFM)

Airflow is the cubic feet of air per minute moving through an open inlet, and it governs how well the vac collects light material spread over an area — sawdust, leaves, the fine grit on a garage floor. A vac with modest water lift but high airflow sweeps a floor quickly; the reverse is good at puddles and slow on dust. Published CFM is usually measured at the motor with no hose attached, and every foot of hose, every bend and every loaded filter cuts it. Treat it as a ceiling, not a working figure.

Hose Diameter: 1-1/4, 1-7/8 and 2-1/2 Inches

  • 1-1/4 inch — standard on small vacs and dust extractors, and the size that fits most power tool dust ports. Light and flexible, but clogs on anything larger than a wood chip. Right for a sander; frustrating for floors.
  • 1-7/8 inch — the default on most mid-size and large shop vacs, and the size most accessories fit.
  • 2-1/2 inch — best for floors, leaves, shavings, gravel and water. With nearly twice the cross-section of a 1-7/8-inch hose it moves far more air and rarely clogs. A 2-1/2-inch hose and floor nozzle is often the most noticeable upgrade you can make to a large vac.

Length matters as much as diameter: a 20-foot hose loses noticeably more suction than a 7-foot one. Move the vac rather than adding hose.

Tank Size vs. Portability

A 16-gallon vac does not suck harder than a 12-gallon one because of its tank; it holds more, weighs more and takes more floor space.

2–6 gallons suits car interiors, quick spills, the workbench and stairs. Small vacs get used because they are easy to grab, but they fill quickly and are poor for water beyond a spill. 9–12 gallons is the practical middle for a one- or two-car garage with mostly dry debris — light enough to lift into a truck bed. 14–16 gallons is right if water is part of the brief or woodworking generates real volume, and at this size big wheels and a drain port matter.

Rated capacity is tank volume, not what the vac collects: the float shutoff cuts in before the tank is brim-full, and a bag takes space. And water weighs about 8.3 pounds per gallon, so a 16-gallon tank three-quarters full is over 100 pounds before you count the vac.

Filters: Matching the Media to the Mess

  • Standard pleated cartridge — fine for general debris and sawdust; passes visible fine dust and clogs quickly on drywall or concrete powder.
  • Fine-dust cartridge — denser media for drywall dust and ash. Holds more, clogs faster, needs regular tapping out.
  • HEPA cartridge — rated to capture 99.97% of particles at 0.3 microns. The choice for silica and drywall dust. Loads up quickly, so pair it with a bag or separator.
  • Dust bags — catch material before it reaches the cartridge. The single best accessory for fine dust: the filter stays clean, suction stays high, and emptying is a bag into the bin rather than a cloud in the driveway.
  • Foam sleeve — for wet pickup. Paper cartridges not rated for water turn to mush and should come off first.

Keep a spare cartridge so a loaded one can be swapped in thirty seconds and cleaned later.

Drywall and Concrete Dust: Shop Vac or Dust Extractor?

Drywall compound and concrete dust blind a standard filter in minutes, at which point suction collapses and the dust stays on the floor or comes out the exhaust.

Grinding a Garage Slab for Epoxy

If you are preparing a floor for an epoxy garage floor coating, the vacuum is not an afterthought. Diamond grinding throws off crystalline silica dust, a serious long-term lung hazard, and the coating will not bond to a floor that still carries a film of it.

OSHA's construction silica standard gives a useful benchmark for handheld surface-prep grinders: a shroud plus a dust collector providing at least 25 CFM per inch of wheel diameter, a filter of 99% or greater efficiency, and a cyclonic pre-separator or filter-cleaning mechanism. For a 7-inch cup wheel that is 175 CFM at the shroud — more than a typical shop vac delivers through a hose once its filter loads. The rule binds employers, not homeowners, but your lungs do not know the difference.

If you rent a walk-behind grinder, rent the matching extractor with it. For edges with an angle grinder, a shop vac with a HEPA filter and a cyclone separator is the minimum, plus a fitted respirator. Our epoxy garage floor cost breakdown treats grinder and vacuum as one budget line for this reason.

What a Dust Extractor Does Differently

A dust extractor is built to run continuously on fine dust from a power tool: HEPA filtration as standard, automatic or push-button filter cleaning that pulses air back through the filter so suction does not fade after ten minutes, a tool-triggered outlet that starts the vacuum with the sander, and a 1-1/4-inch antistatic hose. The trade-off is cost — typically several times a big-box shop vac — and a narrow hose that is poor for floors. For one epoxy floor and the odd drywall patch, a shop vac with a separator and HEPA filter is a reasonable compromise; for a whole basement of drywall or repeated grinding, the extractor pays for itself.

Cyclone Separators: The Best Cheap Upgrade

A cyclone separator sits between the hose and the vac, usually on a 5-gallon bucket. Incoming air spins inside the cone, throwing dust and debris to the wall and down into the bucket, so the vac's filter sees a fraction of the material and suction stays high.

The names most people meet are the Dust Deputy from Oneida Air Systems, a true cyclone cone, and the Dustopper, a simpler bucket-lid separator. Baffle lids catch chips and coarse debris well; for drywall and concrete dust, a cone-style cyclone is the one to buy. The cost is a little suction lost to extra fittings and a second container to empty. Use a sturdy bucket — a thin one can collapse if the hose is suddenly blocked.

Wet Pickup, Drain Ports and Flooded Basements

For water, take off the paper cartridge unless it is rated wet, fit the foam sleeve, and use the widest hose you have. A float seals the inlet when the tank is full — when suction drops and the motor note rises, the tank is full, not broken. Dry the tank and filter before going back to dry work, or sawdust turns to paste. A drain port near the base lets you wheel the vac to a floor drain or the driveway and empty it without lifting; on a 16-gallon vac it is close to essential.

Flooded Basements

A shop vac is the right tool for the last inch of water and what wicks out of carpet, and the wrong tool for the first few hundred gallons. A 20-by-30-foot basement with an inch of standing water holds roughly 370 gallons — over 20 loads of a 16-gallon vac. Pump-out models solve the lifting: a small pump in the tank discharges continuously through a garden hose run to a drain or outside, so the vac never fills. For deeper water, a submersible utility pump moves far more per hour than any vac.

Vacuuming is the symptom, not the cure. If water comes in more than once, spend the money on the walls and grading — see our guide to waterproofing basement walls.

⚠️ Electrical safety in standing water. If water is near outlets, the panel or appliance wiring, shut off power to the area at the breaker before wading in. Plug the vac into a GFCI-protected outlet, keep cord connections out of the water, and do not vacuum water that may contain fuel, solvents or sewage without proper precautions.

Cordless Vacs, Noise and Wall-Mount Units

Cordless. If you already own a battery platform, a cordless wet/dry vac becomes one of the most-used tools in the garage because it is always ready. Milwaukee M18 FUEL, DeWalt 20V MAX and Ryobi 18V ONE+ all make wet/dry vacs that share packs with their drills — our Milwaukee vs. DeWalt comparison and Ryobi tools review help if you are still choosing a platform. Expect runtime in minutes at full power on one pack and less sealed suction than a 16-gallon mains vac: it is the vac for the car and the bench, not a flooded basement.

Noise. Shop vacs commonly run in the 75 to 90-plus decibel range, and extended exposure above about 85 decibels contributes to hearing damage — wear ear protection for long sessions. Dust extractors are generally quieter.

Wall-mount. Wall-mount vacs hang on a bracket and reach the whole garage with a long hose, freeing floor space — ideal for quick cleanups and car detailing. Tanks are small and the long hose costs suction, so pair one with a floor-standing vac for water and heavy debris.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size shop vac is best for a garage?

For most garages, 12 to 16 gallons — enough for an afternoon of sawdust and real water pickup, and large models usually have a drain port so you never lift a full tank. Choose 16 gallons if water or woodworking volume is likely, 9 to 12 for a one-car garage with mostly dry debris. A small or cordless vac makes a good second machine for the car. Tank size does not make a vac suck harder.

Is peak horsepower important on a shop vac?

Very little. It is a momentary bare-motor figure, not what the vac delivers in use — a 15-amp household circuit supplies only about 2.4 horsepower continuously, so 6.5 peak HP is never sustained. It roughly ranks motors within one brand. Sealed suction, airflow, hose diameter and filter decide real performance, and running amps is a better guide to motor size.

What hose size is best for a shop vac?

A 2-1/2-inch hose for floors, leaves, shavings and water, because it moves far more air and rarely clogs. A 1-7/8-inch hose is the general-purpose default. A 1-1/4-inch hose fits power tool dust ports but clogs on larger debris. Keep the hose as short as the job allows — every extra foot costs suction.

Can I use a shop vac for drywall dust?

Yes, but not with the standard filter alone, which clogs in minutes. Use a dust bag in the tank, a fine-dust or HEPA cartridge, and ideally a cyclone separator such as the Dust Deputy in front. For sanding a whole basement, a HEPA dust extractor with automatic filter cleaning holds suction far better.

What vacuum should I use when grinding concrete for epoxy?

A HEPA dust extractor with filter cleaning, or at minimum a shop vac with a HEPA filter and a cyclone separator. Grinding creates crystalline silica dust, and coatings will not bond to a dusty slab. OSHA's construction benchmark for handheld surface-prep grinders is 25 CFM per inch of wheel diameter — 175 CFM for a 7-inch cup wheel. If you rent a walk-behind grinder, rent its matching extractor, and wear a fitted respirator.

Can a shop vac clean up a flooded basement?

It handles the last inch and water wicking out of carpet, but not large volumes: an inch of water over 20 by 30 feet is roughly 370 gallons, over 20 loads of a 16-gallon vac. A pump-out vac discharges continuously through a garden hose, and a submersible utility pump is faster still. Cut power near outlets or wiring first and use a GFCI outlet.

Do I need a HEPA filter on a shop vac?

Not for sawdust, leaves and general debris. For drywall, concrete and masonry dust, ash, or anything from grinding, yes — a HEPA cartridge captures 99.97% of particles at 0.3 microns where a standard filter passes fine dust out the exhaust. It loads faster and costs more, so pair it with a bag or separator.

Are cordless wet/dry vacs worth it?

As a second vac, yes, especially if you already own the batteries. Milwaukee M18, DeWalt 20V MAX and Ryobi 18V ONE+ wet/dry vacs are always ready for the car and quick spills. Expect short runtime at full power, lower suction and small tanks. Models that also run corded, such as the DeWalt DCV581H, remove the runtime worry.
