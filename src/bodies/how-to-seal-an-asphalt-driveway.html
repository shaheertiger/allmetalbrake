How to Seal an Asphalt Driveway — Two Thin Coats, Done Right

Sealcoating is a one-weekend job with a cheap squeegee and a few pails. Whether it lasts three years or peels by spring comes down to the prep, the weather and keeping each coat thin.

To seal an asphalt driveway, you clean it thoroughly, fill the cracks, prime any oil stains, then spread two thin coats of sealer with a squeegee or brush on a dry day between roughly 50°F and 90°F, letting the first coat dry before the second and keeping cars off for 24–48 hours afterwards. A typical two-car driveway takes one day of prep and repairs, one day of coating, and $150–$450 in materials.

The work is not difficult, but almost every failed DIY job fails the same way: sealer applied too thick, over a dirty or oily surface, or with rain or cold arriving too soon. This guide is for blacktop. Concrete takes a completely different product and method — penetrating or film-forming sealers rolled or sprayed on, no crack-and-squeegee routine — so if your driveway is concrete, follow our guide on how to seal a concrete driveway instead.

⚡ The short version: Seal only when the asphalt has gone grey and water soaks in rather than beading. Plan on two thin coats, with each 4.75-gallon pail covering roughly 250–400 sq ft per coat — about four to five pails for a 600 sq ft driveway. Apply when both air and pavement are above 50°F and rising, with 24 hours of dry weather ahead. Walk on it after 4–8 hours, drive on it after 24–48 hours.

Does Your Driveway Need Sealing?

Sealcoat is a sacrificial layer shielding the asphalt binder — the black petroleum that holds the stones together — from UV, oxygen and fuel drips. As binder oxidises it turns brittle and grey, lets go of the aggregate and lets water into the base, where freeze–thaw does the real damage. Sealing slows that; it does not fix structural failure.

Three Quick Checks

The colour. Fresh or recently sealed asphalt is deep black. Asphalt that needs sealing has faded to a dull charcoal or light grey, usually worst in the sunniest stretch and the tyre tracks. If you can see the individual stones standing proud of the surface, the binder has already worn away from around them and you are overdue.

The water bead test. Pour a cup of water on a few spots, including the most worn. If it beads up and sits on the surface, the previous sealcoat is still working — leave it another year. If it darkens the asphalt and soaks in within a minute or so, the surface is open and ready for sealer.

The age. New asphalt must not be sealed straight away. The oils in fresh blacktop need time to cure out, and trapping them under sealer leaves the surface soft and prone to scuffing. The usual guidance is to wait six to twelve months — our guide on when to seal a new asphalt driveway covers how to tell when it is ready. After that, most driveways want sealing every two to three years rather than annually; sealing more often than the surface wears just stacks up coats. See how often to seal a driveway for how traffic and climate shift that interval.

When Sealing Will Not Help

Sealer is a coating, not a repair. Alligator cracking — the interlocking pattern that looks like reptile skin — signals a failing base underneath, and sealer will not hold it together. Neither will it fill ruts, sunken areas or edges that are breaking off into the lawn. If large areas are loose and crumbling, read our guide to fixing a crumbling asphalt driveway before spending money on sealer; on a driveway that is structurally gone, an overlay or replacement is the honest answer.

Tools, Materials and Quantities

Working Out How Much Sealer to Buy

Multiply length by width and add any parking pads or turnarounds. A single-car driveway is often 10–12 ft by 40–50 ft (400–600 sq ft); a two-car driveway 20–24 ft wide by 20–40 ft long (400–960 sq ft).

Most emulsion and acrylic sealers in 4.75-gallon pails quote 250–400 sq ft per pail per coat; some thinner premium products quote more. Expect the low end on old, porous asphalt and on the first coat, which the surface soaks up, and the high end on smooth surfaces and second coats.

Choosing the Sealer

Retail driveway sealers fall into three broad groups. Asphalt emulsion sealers are the budget standard — water-based, low odour, easy to clean up, and generally lasting one to three years. Acrylic and polymer-modified sealers cost more, tend to go on thinner and wear longer, and many add fillers or rubber for small-crack bridging. Refined coal tar sealers resist fuel and UV well but carry high levels of PAHs (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons), are harsh on skin, and are banned in several states, including Washington and Minnesota, and in many municipalities — check local rules before buying. Some contractors still use oil-based asphalt cutback sealers, which penetrate well but smell strongly and are restricted in some areas. For a full comparison of the chemistry, see oil-based vs water-based driveway sealer, and for specific products our best asphalt driveway sealer rankings.

Buy all the pails as the same product: different brands dry to visibly different blacks and sheens.

The Full Shopping List

  • Driveway sealer — quantity from the table above.
  • Squeegee/brush combination applicator on a long handle, about 18–24 inches wide. Rubber blade one side, stiff bristles the other. Budget a second one for the second coat if the first gets clogged.
  • Crack filler — pourable or squeeze-bottle for cracks up to about ½ inch; one quart typically fills 30–50 linear feet of ¼-inch crack, depending on depth. Our best asphalt crack filler guide sorts them by crack width.
  • Foam backer rod for cracks deeper than about ½ inch.
  • Cold patch asphalt for potholes — a 50 lb bag fills roughly 0.5 cubic feet, about 3 sq ft at 2 inches deep.
  • Hand tamper (borrow or buy) for compacting patch.
  • Oil spot primer — one quart treats a typical garage-front set of drip stains.
  • Driveway cleaner or degreaser and a stiff scrub brush.
  • Hose or pressure washer, stiff push broom, and a leaf blower if you have one.
  • Edging tool or string trimmer, flat shovel and a putty knife for cracks.
  • 4-inch disposable paintbrush or old stiff brush for cutting in edges.
  • Drill with a mixing paddle — the single most useful extra on this list.
  • Painter's tape or plastic sheet to protect concrete walks and the garage slab.
  • Gloves, safety glasses, disposable clothes and shoes, and caution tape or sawhorses to block the driveway.

Picking the Weather Window

Weather ruins more sealcoats than product choice does. Water-based sealers cure by evaporation, which only works properly when it is warm and dry. You need:

  • Temperature: 50°F or warmer for both air and pavement, rising during application and staying above 50°F overnight for at least the first 24 hours. Many products specify 55°F; check the label. Our guide on what temperature to seal a driveway goes deeper on the minimum and maximum.
  • Not too hot: above about 90°F air temperature, or on blacktop baking in full summer sun, the sealer skins over before it levels, leaving drag marks and poor bonding. In high summer, work in the morning or late afternoon.
  • Dry for 24 hours after the final coat, ideally 48. A shower an hour or two after application can wash uncured sealer into the gutter; what to do if that happens is covered in rain after sealing a driveway.
  • Low humidity and some sun: humid, overcast days slow drying dramatically, sometimes doubling cure times.
  • No heavy dew or fog forecast on the first night, which can leave white blush on fresh sealer.

In most of the US that means late spring to early autumn. Start by mid-morning so the coat gets a full day of warmth.

💡 Plan the two days back to back. Day one is cleaning and repairs, which also gives washed asphalt time to dry and crack filler time to set. Day two is coating. Look for a three-day dry stretch — prep day, coating day, and a dry day after — before you start pulling weeds.

Prep: Weeds, Edges, Cleaning, Oil

Sealer only bonds to clean asphalt. Dust, grass clippings, pollen, sap and oil all form a layer between the two, and a coat that sits on dirt peels off in sheets within a season. Prep is two-thirds of the job and the part most worth taking slowly.

1. Pull Weeds and Clear the Cracks

Pull or dig out every weed growing in cracks and along the edges, then scrape the cracks clean with a putty knife, screwdriver or crack-cleaning tool. Roots and soil left in a crack stop the filler bonding and the weed simply grows back through. If roots are deep, a spot treatment of weed killer a week or two before the job saves effort.

2. Edge the Sides

Grass and soil creep over the edges of almost every driveway. Cut the turf back with an edging tool or string trimmer to expose the full width of the asphalt, then scrape away the soil with a flat shovel. Sealing the true edge matters: the edge is where water gets under the asphalt first, and it is the part of the driveway most likely to crumble.

3. Clean the Whole Surface

Sweep hard, blow off the fine dust, then wash from the top of the slope down with a hose jet or pressure washer, scrubbing driveway cleaner into grimy areas. With a pressure washer, use a fan tip and keep it moving — a narrow tip held close can strip binder and loosen aggregate on old, oxidised asphalt.

Let the surface dry before you seal. Most water-based labels accept, and some recommend, a slightly damp surface on hot days to slow drying, but standing water and puddles in low spots must be gone.

4. Treat Oil and Fuel Stains

Sealer will not stick to oil. Even after scrubbing, oil left in the pores of the asphalt keeps leaching up and pushes fresh sealer off within weeks. Scrub every drip with degreaser and rinse — our guide to removing oil stains from a driveway covers stubborn ones — then, once dry, brush a coat of oil spot primer over each stain extending an inch or two beyond it. Primers typically dry in an hour or so; follow the label before sealing over them. Areas that are soft or crumbling from long-term fuel exposure need cutting out and patching rather than priming.

Fill Cracks and Patch Holes First

Sealer is not a crack filler, despite what some labels imply. Products with added filler can bridge hairline cracks, but anything wider than about ⅛ inch needs to be filled separately and allowed to cure before the sealcoat goes on. Water entering cracks is what destroys the base, so this step does more for the life of the driveway than the coating itself.

Cracks Up to ½ Inch

Clean and blow out the crack, then fill with a pourable or squeeze-bottle rubberised filler, slightly below flush. It may sink as it soaks in and need a second pass. Scrape off any excess so no raised ridge shows through the sealer.

Cracks Wider or Deeper Than ½ Inch

Push foam backer rod into the crack so the top sits about ½ inch below the surface, then fill over it. Without backer rod, a deep crack swallows a whole bottle of filler and still settles. For cracks over about an inch wide, filler alone tends to shrink and pull away — treat them as a patch instead.

Potholes and Depressions

Cut or dig out loose material to firm edges and a solid base, ideally square-sided. Fill with cold patch asphalt in layers of no more than about 2 inches, compacting each one hard with a hand tamper. Overfill the final layer by about ½ inch, then tamp it down flush — driving a car tyre over a piece of plywood laid on top of the patch is a common way to finish compacting it.

Give repairs time before sealing. Pourable crack fillers often need several hours to a day to cure, depending on temperature and depth. Cold patch stays relatively soft for weeks; many manufacturers suggest waiting before sealing over it, so check the bag. If a patch is still soft, seal around it and come back to it at the next sealing.

Applying the First Coat

Mix the Sealer Properly

The solids in driveway sealer — asphalt, clay, sand and filler — settle in storage, so unmixed sealer goes on watery from the top of the pail and as sludge from the bottom. Stir thoroughly with a drill and mixing paddle until no sediment is left on the bottom, and stir again briefly before each pour.

Protect Adjacent Surfaces and Cut In

Tape or cover any concrete you do not want stained: the garage slab apron, walkways, kerbs and the edge of the road if it is concrete. Sealer that splashes onto concrete soaks in and is very hard to remove once dry.

Then cut in. With a disposable brush, paint a strip of sealer 6–12 inches wide along every edge — the garage, the walks, around drains and along the lawn — so the squeegee never has to work right up to an edge where it would splash concrete and grass.

For most homeowners a combination tool is the right answer: spread with the rubber side and flip to the brush on rough patches. Rented spray equipment is rarely worth it on a single driveway, and overspray drifts further than you expect.

Work in Sections

Start at the top of the driveway — usually by the garage — and work toward the street so you never have to walk back across fresh sealer. Mentally divide the driveway into sections about as wide as the driveway and 4–6 feet deep.

  1. Pour a ribbon of sealer in an S-shape across the section — only as much as you can spread in a minute or two. It is easier to add a little more than to chase a pool of excess down the slope.
  2. Spread it with the squeegee, pulling the sealer across the width in long, overlapping strokes. Aim for a thin, even coat — you should be able to see the texture of the asphalt through the wet sealer, not a smooth black pond.
  3. Finish each section with light strokes all in one direction, so the drag lines line up.
  4. Move to the next section while the edge of the last one is still wet, and blend into it.

Avoiding Lap Marks and Puddles

Lap marks appear where fresh sealer goes over a strip that has started to dry, leaving a double thickness with a different sheen. Keep a wet edge by working modest sections, avoiding the hottest sun, and only taking breaks at the end of a section.

Puddles are worse. Sealer that pools in low spots or along the edges stays soft for days, tracks onto shoes, cracks as it dries and eventually peels. Spread any puddle out immediately with the squeegee. Two thin coats always outlast one thick one: a thin coat cures fully and bonds, a thick one skins on top and stays soft underneath.

The Second Coat

The first coat soaks into the open, oxidised surface; the second builds the wearing layer that gets the driveway to a two- to three-year life, and usually stretches further per pail. On a heavily worn driveway, an extra pass on the tyre tracks is worth the half-pail it costs.

Timing. Apply the second coat once the first is dry to the touch and does not mark when walked on — typically 4–8 hours in warm, sunny weather, and often the next morning in cooler or humid conditions. Follow the label: some products specify a minimum gap, and many water-based sealers state that the first coat must not be tacky. Applying the second coat over a soft first coat traps moisture and leaves a soft film that scuffs.

Direction. Some pros apply the second coat at right angles to the first, which helps fill any spots the first pass missed and evens out drag lines. On a long, narrow driveway that is awkward, so finishing in the same lengthwise direction is fine — the important thing is consistency within each coat.

Barricades, Drive-On Times and Cleanup

Block the Driveway

Before you start, move every car you will need in the next two days onto the street. Then block the street end with sawhorses, bins or caution tape — delivery drivers and visitors will walk straight across fresh sealer if nothing stops them. Switch off any sprinklers that reach the driveway for 24–48 hours.

Cleanup

Water-based sealer washes off tools, skin and splashes easily while wet and is very hard to shift once dry, so keep a bucket of water and rags nearby and wipe splashes on concrete or siding immediately. Rinse tools onto the lawn or into a bucket, never into the storm drain. Dried sealer usually needs mineral spirits or scraping, and on concrete often leaves a shadow. Let leftover sealer dry out with the lid off and check local disposal rules; store unopened pails somewhere frost-free.

Common Mistakes

Applying it too thick. The most common error. Thick sealer looks great for a week, then stays soft, scuffs, cracks into a fine web and peels. Two thin coats, every time.

Skimping on cleaning. Sealer bonds to dust instead of asphalt, and it peels. Run a hand across the washed, dry surface: if your palm comes up dusty, it is not clean enough yet.

Sealing over oil, or into open cracks. Oil pushes the sealer off within weeks; sealer poured into a crack shrinks and splits. Prime and fill first.

Not mixing properly. Uneven colour and thin watery patches across the driveway are almost always sediment left at the bottom of the pail.

Sealing in the wrong weather. Cold nights, rain within hours or a 95°F afternoon cause blush, wash-off or drag marks respectively.

Sealing too often. Sealing every year on a lightly used driveway builds layers of sealcoat that crack and flake off together. Do the water test first.

Sealing new asphalt too early. Within the first six months, the surface stays soft and marks easily.

Driving on it too soon. Tyres lift soft sealer and track it into the garage and street. Give it the full 24–48 hours and longer if it is cool.

Cost: DIY vs Professional

Retail driveway sealer typically runs from about $25 per 4.75-gallon pail for basic asphalt emulsion to $60 or more for premium acrylic and polymer-modified products. Add crack filler, oil spot primer, applicator and incidentals, and a DIY job on a typical 600 sq ft driveway lands in the ranges below.

Professional sealcoating usually runs about $0.12–$0.25 per square foot for residential asphalt, so roughly $150–$450 for the same 600 sq ft driveway, often with a minimum charge. That is similar to the DIY figure, because contractors buy bulk sealer at a fraction of retail and spray it quickly. What the contractor adds is speed and equipment; what DIY adds is control over the prep, which is the part cheap contractors cut. DIY suits a small-to-medium driveway in decent shape. A professional makes more sense on very large driveways, on heavily cracked surfaces that need hot-pour crack sealing, or if the weekend is worth more to you than the work.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you seal an asphalt driveway yourself?

Confirm it needs it first: grey asphalt that soaks up water rather than beading it. Pull weeds, edge back the grass, sweep and wash, and let it dry. Degrease and prime oil stains, fill cracks, patch potholes with compacted cold patch, and let the repairs cure. Then, on a day above 50°F with 24 dry hours ahead, mix the sealer thoroughly, cut in the edges with a brush and squeegee on a thin coat in sections from the garage toward the street. Add a second thin coat once the first is dry to the touch, and keep cars off for 24–48 hours.

How many coats of sealer does an asphalt driveway need?

Two thin coats. The first largely soaks into the open, oxidised surface; the second builds the wearing layer that delivers a two- to three-year life. One thick coat is worse than two thin ones because it skins over and stays soft underneath, then cracks and peels. An extra pass on the tyre tracks helps on very worn driveways, while a recently sealed driveway in good shape may need only one maintenance coat.

How much driveway sealer do I need?

Most 4.75-gallon pails cover roughly 250–400 sq ft per coat. Divide the driveway's area by the coverage for each coat, using the low end for the first coat on old, grey asphalt and the high end for the second. A 600 sq ft driveway with two coats typically takes four to five pails; 1,000 sq ft about seven. Buy one spare on larger jobs — running out mid-driveway leaves a visible line, and unopened pails can usually be returned.

Should I use a squeegee or a brush to apply driveway sealer?

A squeegee for most driveways, a brush for rough and porous ones — ideally a combination tool with both. The squeegee spreads a thin, even coat quickly and makes puddles easy to avoid. The brush works sealer into deep texture a squeegee rides over, which helps on old, coarse asphalt. Spraying is what contractors do on large areas, but it is rarely worth renting equipment for one driveway.

How long should I wait between coats of driveway sealer?

Until the first coat is dry to the touch and does not mark when walked on — usually 4–8 hours in warm sun, often the next morning in cool or humid weather. Check the label for a stated minimum. A second coat over a tacky first coat traps moisture and leaves a soft film that scuffs and peels.

Do I need to fill cracks before sealing a driveway?

Yes. Sealer is a thin coating, not a filler; poured into a crack it shrinks, splits and lets water through to the base, which is what eventually causes potholes. Fill cracks up to about ½ inch with rubberised filler, use foam backer rod in deeper ones, treat anything over an inch wide as a patch, and let the filler cure before sealing.

Can you seal an asphalt driveway after it rains?

Yes, once the puddles are gone. Many water-based sealers tolerate a slightly damp surface, but standing water dilutes the sealer and stops it bonding, so check low spots and cracks. The forecast matters more than yesterday's rain: you need at least 24 dry hours after the final coat, ideally 48, with temperatures staying above 50°F.

Is it cheaper to seal a driveway yourself?

Usually a little, not a lot. DIY materials for a 600 sq ft driveway run about $150–$450; professional sealcoating at $0.12–$0.25 per sq ft comes to a similar $150–$450, because contractors buy bulk sealer far below retail. The real DIY advantage is control over cleaning, oil priming and crack filling — the steps budget contractors skip most often.
