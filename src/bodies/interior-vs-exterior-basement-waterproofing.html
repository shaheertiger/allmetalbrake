Interior vs Exterior Basement Waterproofing: Which Is Better?

Exterior waterproofing keeps water out of the wall. Interior waterproofing lets it in and gets rid of it. Both work — on different problems, at very different prices, with very different amounts of digging.

Exterior waterproofing is the better method in principle: it is the only approach that keeps water out of the foundation wall altogether, and it is what building codes describe for new construction. Interior waterproofing is the better choice for most existing homes in practice: an interior drain tile and sump system costs roughly a third to half as much, goes in over a few days without touching the yard, and handles the most common kind of basement leak — water under pressure entering at the joint between wall and floor — just as reliably.

So the honest answer is that neither is better across the board. Exterior work is worth its price on new construction, on a single wall that can be dug cheaply, where the wall itself needs structural repair from outside, or where a finished basement would otherwise have to be gutted. Interior work wins nearly everywhere else. And a large share of damp basements need neither, because the water is coming from a downspout or from condensation.

⚡ The direct answer: Exterior (excavation, membrane, footing drain, backfill) stops water before it enters the wall — $80–$200 per linear foot, typically $10,000–$30,000+ for a whole house. Interior (perimeter drain tile and sump, plus coatings or crack injection) manages water after it gets in — $35–$90 per linear foot, typically $4,000–$15,000. Choose exterior for new builds, structural wall repair, or one accessible problem wall. Choose interior for most leaking basements in existing homes. Fix gutters and grading first either way.

What Each Method Involves

Exterior Waterproofing

Exterior waterproofing works from the soil side of the foundation, and it starts with a hole. A crew excavates a trench along the wall all the way down to the footing — typically six to nine feet in a full basement — wide enough to stand and work in. On deep digs or poor soil the trench has to be shored or benched for safety, and every buried line in the way has to be located first.

With the wall exposed, the sequence is broadly the same across contractors:

  • Clean and repair the wall. Old tar and soil are scraped or pressure-washed off, cracks are routed and patched, and block walls are often parged with a cement coat so the membrane has a smooth surface to bond to.
  • Apply the membrane. This is the waterproofing itself: a thick fluid-applied rubberised asphalt or polymer coating, a self-adhering sheet membrane, or bentonite clay panels that swell into a seal when wet. A true membrane is elastic enough to bridge hairline cracks that open later.
  • Add drainage board. A dimpled plastic sheet goes over the membrane. It protects the coating from backfill damage and, more importantly, gives water against the wall a free path straight down to the footing rather than letting it sit and build pressure.
  • Lay new footing drain. Perforated pipe, usually four inches, is set in washed gravel beside the footing, wrapped in filter fabric to keep soil out, and run to daylight on a sloping lot or to a sump pit where the lot is flat.
  • Backfill and regrade. Ideally with free-draining gravel or sand near the wall rather than the original clay, then topsoil graded away from the house.

One distinction matters a great deal here. Most houses already have something on the outside of the foundation — typically a thin sprayed or brushed coat of asphalt. That is damp-proofing, which codes require on most foundations to slow vapour and soil moisture, and it is not designed to stop liquid water under pressure. Codes call for full waterproofing only where a high water table or severe soil conditions exist. So a homeowner who says "the outside was waterproofed when the house was built" very often has a coating that was never meant to keep a wet basement dry.

Interior Waterproofing

Interior waterproofing is a family of methods applied from inside the basement, and it helps to separate them because they do very different jobs.

  • Interior drain tile and sump. The main event. A strip of floor slab, usually about a foot wide, is cut and broken out around the perimeter. Perforated pipe is laid in gravel beside the footing, a sump pit is installed, and the floor is re-poured over the pipe. A dimpled plastic membrane is usually fitted to the lower wall so water seeping through it runs down behind the membrane into the drain. On block walls, small weep holes are drilled into the bottom course so water collecting in the hollow cores can drain out. A sump pump then lifts the collected water out of the house.
  • Wall coatings and sealers. Masonry waterproofers, crystalline products and cementitious coatings applied to the inside face of the wall. They reduce dampness and light seepage through porous concrete and block. The methods and products are covered in our guides on how to waterproof basement walls and the best basement wall sealers.
  • Crack injection. Polyurethane or epoxy injected into cracks in poured concrete walls under pressure, filling the crack through the full thickness of the wall.
  • Vapour barriers. Plastic sheeting or dimple membrane on the walls, and sometimes the floor, to keep moisture from reaching finishes and the basement air. In a drainage system the wall membrane also directs water to the drain.

What Each One Actually Stops

This is the comparison that matters most, and the one sales conversations tend to blur.

Exterior waterproofing keeps water out of the wall. The membrane sits between the wet soil and the concrete, the drainage board relieves pressure against it, and the footing drain carries water away before it can rise. Done properly, the foundation itself stays dry. That has knock-on benefits interior methods cannot match: a dry wall does not wick moisture into the basement air, does not deposit the white mineral crust described in our guide to efflorescence on basement walls, and, in a hollow block wall, does not fill its cores with water that freezes near grade in winter.

Interior drainage manages water that gets in. It does not try to stop water reaching or entering the wall. It accepts that groundwater will press against the foundation, intercepts it at the footing level — the lowest point, where most of it enters — and pumps it out before it reaches the floor. The wall stays wet on the outside and often damp inside it; the basement stays dry. For a block wall, the weep holes and membrane mean water running down inside the cores is caught too.

That is a less elegant solution, and it is easy to make it sound inferior. But it is extremely effective against the leak homeowners actually see most often: a ring of water at the base of the walls after heavy rain, caused by groundwater building up around the footing and pushing through the cold joint where the floor slab meets the wall. Interior drain tile relieves that pressure at exactly the point it occurs. It also handles water coming up through the floor near the perimeter, which no wall coating on either side can touch.

Interior coatings and sealers stop much less. They are worthwhile against dampness, sweating and minor seepage through porous walls. They are not built to resist water under real hydrostatic pressure, and on the inside face of a wall the water is pushing them off rather than against the surface. Water that is blocked by a coating also simply goes somewhere else — commonly the floor joint. A coating is a finishing measure or a fix for a mild problem, not a substitute for drainage.

Crack injection is the interior exception. Because the injected polyurethane fills the crack through the entire wall thickness and out to the soil side, a properly injected crack is sealed against water much as an exterior patch would seal it. On a poured wall whose only leaks are two or three cracks, injection from inside does the same job as digging, at a fraction of the price.

💡 The short way to remember it. Exterior systems keep the wall dry. Interior drainage keeps the basement dry. If your goal is a usable, dry room, both deliver it. If your goal is a dry foundation — because the wall is deteriorating, bowing, or shedding efflorescence and spalling — only exterior work fully delivers it.

Cost Compared

Both systems are priced by the linear foot of wall, so the most useful number to know before talking to anyone is your basement's perimeter. A rectangular 1,000 sq ft basement runs about 130 feet; L-shapes and jogs add more. Full breakdowns, including sump work and bowing walls, are in our basement waterproofing cost guide.

On a typical 130-foot perimeter, then, full interior drainage lands around $4,500–$11,500 and full exterior around $10,500–$26,000. Exterior work almost always costs two to three times as much for the same length of wall, and the gap widens on the details.

What Pushes Exterior Prices Up

The headline per-foot figure assumes open, accessible soil. Every obstacle on the outside of the house adds money: a deck or porch that has to be supported or removed, a concrete driveway or patio that has to be cut and re-poured, an air-conditioning condenser, gas meter or electrical service that has to be moved or worked around, mature shrubs and trees, and neighbours close enough that equipment cannot reach. Deep basements, rock, high groundwater requiring dewatering, and urban lots where soil has to be trucked away rather than piled on the lawn can push whole-house jobs past $40,000. The restoration — new sod, plantings, paving — is frequently not in the quote at all.

What Pushes Interior Prices Up

Interior quotes rise with finished space, because drywall, framing, flooring and built-ins along the perimeter have to come out before the slab can be cut. Extra sump pits, a battery backup pump, a long or buried discharge line, full-height wall membrane rather than a short strip, and stairs or chimney bases that interrupt the perimeter all add cost. A battery backup adds roughly $600–$1,800 installed and is worth it on any system the basement depends on.

Cost Over Time

The upfront gap is partly offset by running costs. An interior system needs a sump pump replaced every seven to ten years or so, uses electricity, and benefits from an annual check. A footing drain that runs to daylight on a sloping lot needs no pump at all. Over thirty years that might add a few thousand dollars to the interior side — real, but rarely enough to close a $10,000-plus difference in installation cost.

Disruption: Yard or Basement

The two methods are disruptive in opposite places, and for many homeowners that, more than price, decides the question.

Exterior work disrupts the outside of the house. A whole-house excavation typically takes one to three weeks of on-site work, longer with weather or rock. The yard around the house becomes a trench and a spoil pile; landscaping beds next to the foundation are destroyed; walkways, patios and sometimes part of the driveway are broken out. Backfilled soil keeps settling for months, so expect to top up and regrade over the following year. Access matters, too: a narrow side yard between houses may mean hand digging, which is slow and expensive. On the other hand, nothing inside the house is touched, which is precisely why exterior work is attractive under a finished basement.

Interior work disrupts the basement. A typical full-perimeter drain tile job takes one to three days. It is loud — jackhammering or saw-cutting concrete — and dusty despite the plastic sheeting good crews use, and everything within a few feet of the walls has to be cleared. In an unfinished basement that is close to the whole cost of disruption. In a finished basement, at least the lower portion of every perimeter wall has to be opened up, carpet or flooring lifted along the edge, and baseboards and trim removed. Rebuilding that can add thousands of dollars that never appear in a waterproofing quote, which can tip the comparison toward exterior work on a heavily finished space.

The yard is untouched by an interior job apart from the discharge line, which should run well away from the foundation — ideally ten feet or more — so the pumped water does not simply circulate back to the wall.

Exterior systems are largely passive. Once a good membrane is buried it is out of sunlight and abrasion and lasts a very long time. The weak point historically has been the footing drain: older homes often have clay tile or early pipe without filter fabric that has silted up over decades, which is frequently why an old basement that was once dry starts leaking. A new exterior job replaces that drain, and on a sloping lot that drains to daylight there is no mechanical part to fail.

Interior drainage depends on a pump and on power. The buried pipe lasts as long as an exterior drain, but the system only works while the sump pump runs, and the moment it is most needed — a severe storm — is also when power cuts happen. A battery backup or water-powered backup pump, a high-water alarm, and a seasonal test (pour a bucket of water into the pit and watch it cycle) are what make an interior system dependable. Our sump pump guide covers primary and backup pump choices.

Warranties tend to favour interior work. Established interior waterproofing companies commonly offer lifetime or long-term warranties on the drainage system, often transferable to one subsequent owner. They usually cover water through the treated walls and wall-floor joint only, exclude windows, plumbing and condensation, and put the pump on a separate, shorter manufacturer warranty. Exterior contractors and excavators more often warrant workmanship for a set period and pass through the membrane manufacturer's material warranty, and terms vary widely. Neither type is worth much without the specific coverage, exclusions and transfer terms in writing before signing.

Which Suits Which Problem

Water at the Base of the Walls After Rain (Hydrostatic Pressure)

The classic case, and the one interior drain tile is designed for. Groundwater rises around the footing, pressure builds, and water pushes through the cold joint and sometimes up through the floor edge. Interior drain tile intercepts it at that level and relieves the pressure. Exterior work also solves it — the footing drain does the same job from outside — but at two to three times the cost. For most homes, interior wins here. If the water table is high enough that water rises through the middle of the floor, more pits or an under-slab drainage layout may be needed, and that is interior work too.

Seepage Through the Wall Face

Damp patches or trickles partway up the wall, especially on block. If it is light and limited to dampness, an interior coating may be enough. If water is actively running, the choice is between letting it in and draining it (interior membrane plus drain tile) or stopping it at the outside face (exterior membrane). Interior drainage is cheaper; exterior is the only one that keeps the wall itself dry, which matters if the block faces are spalling or crumbling.

Leaking Cracks in Poured Concrete

Usually neither large system. Injecting the cracks from inside costs $400–$1,000 each professionally and seals the full depth of the wall. A crack that is wider at one end, has offset faces, or is accompanied by bowing is a structural matter, not a waterproofing one.

Bowing or Leaning Walls

Structural first, waterproofing second — and this is where exterior often makes more sense. Interior anchors, steel beams or carbon fibre straps can stabilise a wall with modest movement, and an interior drain will reduce the water pressure that contributed to it. But excavating the outside lets a contractor relieve the soil load completely, push the wall back toward plumb, replace expansive clay backfill with gravel, and install membrane and footing drain while the wall is exposed. If the wall needs rebuilding, the outside will be dug anyway, so the waterproofing comes at a small marginal cost. Get an independent structural engineer's opinion before choosing.

New Construction or an Addition

Exterior, without question. The excavation is already open, so the cost of upgrading from basic damp-proofing to a real membrane, drainage board and properly filtered footing drain is typically a few thousand dollars for a whole house — a small fraction of what the same work costs once the soil, landscaping and driveway are in place. Many builders also rough in a sump pit; asking for interior drain tile under the slab at the same time is inexpensive insurance.

Condensation and Musty Air

Neither. Moisture beading on cold walls and pipes in humid summer weather, damp with no visible water entry, is condensation from the basement air rather than a leak — and no waterproofing system fixes it. A dehumidifier, insulation on the walls and cold pipes, and closing windows on humid days are the fixes. The tape-and-plastic test in our guide on how to fix a damp basement tells you which you have in a day or two, and a properly sized basement dehumidifier handles most of it.

Water Only on One Side, Only in Storms

Look outside before calling anyone. A downspout discharging at the foundation or soil sloping toward the house on that side is the usual culprit, and extensions, gutter repairs and regrading cost a few hundred dollars. If the problem persists after that, a single-wall solution — partial interior drain tile, or excavating just that wall — usually beats a full-perimeter system.

A Finished Basement You Do Not Want to Gut

The case where exterior work's price premium narrows the most. Once the cost of removing and rebuilding drywall, framing, flooring and built-ins around the perimeter is added to an interior quote, an exterior job on accessible walls can come close. It is worth getting both kinds of quote, and asking the interior contractor exactly how much finish has to come out.

Combining Both

Interior and exterior methods are not rivals in every case. Several combinations are common and sensible.

Exterior on one wall, interior on the rest. Where a single wall is the problem and it is accessible — no driveway, no deck — digging that one wall and fixing it properly can cost $4,000–$10,000. If the other walls have occasional seepage at the floor joint, a partial interior drain covers them without excavating the whole house.

Exterior membrane plus interior drain tile. The belt-and-braces approach for high water tables, deep basements below the groundwater level, and finished basements where any failure would be expensive. The exterior membrane keeps most water out; the interior drain catches whatever gets past it and any water rising under the slab. It is the most expensive option and justified mainly where flooding has been serious or the space is high-value.

Interior drainage plus coatings. Very common. The drain handles water under pressure at the footing; a wall coating or crystalline sealer above the membrane line reduces dampness and efflorescence on the upper wall. The coating is a finishing step, not the waterproofing.

Anything plus outside drainage. Whichever system you choose, extending downspouts at least six feet, keeping gutters clear and grading soil away from the house reduce the water either system has to deal with. With an interior system, that is the difference between a pump that runs occasionally and one that runs constantly and wears out early.

The Short Version

Before choosing either, rule out the cheap causes: walk the outside of the house in a heavy rain, extend every downspout, and check the grade. Run a simple condensation test. A surprising share of "waterproofing" problems end there.

If water is genuinely entering under pressure, interior drain tile with a sump and a backup pump is the right answer for most existing homes — around $4,000–$15,000, a few days of noise, and no damage to the yard. It manages water rather than stopping it, and it works.

Pay for exterior excavation and membrane — $10,000–$30,000+ — when the hole is already open (new construction), when a wall needs structural repair from outside, when one accessible wall is the whole problem, when the wall itself is deteriorating from saturation, or when the interior is finished enough that tearing it out would cost as much as digging.

And compare quotes by the linear foot, ask exactly what each contractor's system stops and what it only manages, and get the warranty, including what it excludes and whether it transfers, in writing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is interior or exterior basement waterproofing better?

Exterior is better in principle and interior is better value for most existing homes. Exterior waterproofing — excavating to the footing, applying a membrane and drainage board, laying a new footing drain and backfilling — keeps water out of the wall entirely, at $80–$200 per linear foot or roughly $10,000–$30,000+ for a whole house. Interior drain tile with a sump pump lets water reach the wall but intercepts it at the footing and pumps it out, at $35–$90 per linear foot or roughly $4,000–$15,000. Both keep the basement dry. Exterior is worth the premium for new construction, structural wall repair, a single accessible problem wall or a heavily finished basement; interior suits most other leaking basements.

Does interior waterproofing actually work?

Interior drainage does, and very reliably, against the most common leak: groundwater building up around the footing and pushing through the joint between the wall and floor slab. The perimeter drain tile relieves that pressure at the level where it occurs and a sump pump removes the water. What interior drainage does not do is keep the wall itself dry — water still enters the wall and is caught behind a membrane. Interior coatings and sealers on their own are a different matter: they help with dampness and light seepage but are not built to hold back water under pressure, and water they block often finds its way out at the floor joint instead.

Why is exterior waterproofing so expensive?

Because most of the cost is digging and putting things back. The trench has to reach the footing, often six to nine feet down, with shoring on deep or unstable cuts and utility locates beforehand. Soil has to be stored or hauled away, the wall cleaned and repaired, and membrane, drainage board and new footing drain installed before backfilling. Then decks, patios, walkways, driveway sections, air-conditioning units and landscaping that were in the way have to be rebuilt or replaced — and that restoration is frequently not included in the quote. On open, accessible walls exterior work runs $80–$200 per linear foot; obstacles and tight lots push whole-house jobs past $30,000.

Can I waterproof just one wall from the outside?

Yes, and it is often the most sensible use of exterior work. If one wall is responsible for the water — a side facing uphill, or one with a failed section of old footing drain — excavating only that wall typically costs $4,000–$10,000 where access is clear. The new footing drain has to be connected to an outlet, either daylight on a sloping lot or an interior or exterior sump. The main thing to rule out first is a cheaper cause on that side: a downspout discharging against the foundation or soil sloping toward the house often explains one-sided leaks for a few hundred dollars.

Is the tar on my foundation already waterproofing?

Usually not. The thin black asphalt coating sprayed or brushed onto most foundations is damp-proofing, which building codes require to slow soil moisture and vapour. It is not designed to stop liquid water under hydrostatic pressure, becomes brittle with age, and cannot bridge cracks that open after construction. Full waterproofing — a thicker elastic membrane, often with drainage board — is normally required only where a high water table or severe soil conditions exist. So a basement with original "waterproofing" that has started to leak may never have had a true membrane in the first place.

How long does interior vs exterior waterproofing last?

The buried parts of both last decades. A quality exterior membrane, out of sunlight and abrasion, commonly lasts 20–50 years, and a properly filtered footing drain lasts as long. Interior drain tile pipe in gravel under the slab is similarly long-lived. The difference is the moving parts: an interior system depends on a sump pump that typically needs replacing every seven to ten years, plus a backup pump battery every three to five, and it only works when the pump runs. Interior wall coatings are the shortest-lived element at roughly five to ten years. An exterior drain that runs to daylight has no mechanical parts at all.

Should I use interior or exterior waterproofing for a finished basement?

Get both kinds of quote. Interior drain tile requires opening at least the lower part of every perimeter wall, lifting flooring along the edge, and removing trim, built-ins and anything framed against the foundation. Rebuilding those finishes can add thousands of dollars that never appear in the waterproofing price, narrowing or erasing interior's usual cost advantage. Exterior work leaves the inside untouched, but its price depends heavily on access — a driveway, deck or patio against the wall can make it prohibitive. Where outside access is good and the finish is extensive, exterior often wins; where the yard is obstructed, interior with careful partial demolition usually does.

Do I need waterproofing if my basement walls are just damp?

Probably not a full system. Dampness without visible running water is often condensation — humid air meeting cool concrete — especially if it is worst in summer. Tape a square of plastic sheet to the wall for a day or two: moisture on the room side means condensation, which a dehumidifier and insulation fix; moisture behind it means water coming through the wall. Light seepage through porous block or concrete is often handled by an interior coating or sealer for a few hundred dollars DIY, plus clearing gutters and extending downspouts outside. Drain tile or excavation is for water that actually runs, pools or rises at the floor joint.
