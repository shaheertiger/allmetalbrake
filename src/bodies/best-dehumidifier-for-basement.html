Best Dehumidifier for a Basement — How to Choose the Right One

Most basements need a 50-pint unit on the 2019 rating, with a built-in pump unless there is a floor drain. Capacity labels changed in 2019, cold floors cut output, and drainage decides whether you will actually keep it running.

For a typical damp, musty basement, the best dehumidifier is a 50-pint unit rated under the 2019 Department of Energy standard, with a built-in condensate pump, set to around 50% relative humidity and left to run. That one specification covers most basements up to roughly 1,500–2,000 square feet. The pump matters because a bucket you have to empty twice a day is a dehumidifier you will eventually stop using, and most basements have no floor drain where a gravity hose can reach it.

The rest of the decision is about three things the headline capacity hides. Pint ratings changed in 2019, so older sizing advice overstates the number you need. Compressor dehumidifiers lose much of their capacity below about 65°F, which is where unfinished basements spend half the year. And a dehumidifier only removes moisture from the air — if water is coming through the walls or floor as liquid, it treats the symptom while the cause continues.

⚡ The quick answer: Most basements: a 50-pint (2019 DOE) Energy Star unit with a built-in pump, auto-defrost and auto-restart. Small or finished basement under ~800 sq ft: a 35-pint unit. Cold unfinished basement in the 50s°F: a low-temperature whole-house or LGR unit, or a desiccant model. Large, very wet or whole-home needs: a whole-house dehumidifier (70–130+ pints/day). Set it to about 50%, and fix any liquid water at the wall first.

The 2019 Pint Re-Rating — Why Old Advice Is Wrong

Until 2019, US dehumidifier capacity was measured at 80°F and 60% relative humidity. Warm, humid air gives up water easily, so the numbers were flattering. In June 2019 the Department of Energy's revised test procedure took effect, measuring portable units at 65°F and 60% relative humidity — much closer to a real basement. The machines did not change; the labels did.

What was sold as a 70-pint dehumidifier is now sold as roughly a 50-pint. Anything written before 2019 — forum threads, older buying guides, charts on old boxes — that says to buy a "70-pint for a basement" is describing what is now labelled a 50-pint.

Old rating (pre-2019, 80°F)Approx. 2019 DOE rating (65°F)Typical basement use
70 pints/day50 pints/dayMost basements; the standard choice
50 pints/day35 pints/daySmall or finished basements
30–35 pints/day20–22 pints/daySingle rooms; too small for most basements

If you see a "70-pint" listing today, check which standard it uses; some still quote the old figure because it looks bigger. And since the largest portables are now labelled 50 pints, a chart recommending a 70-pint portable means "the largest portable available".

These are 2019 DOE pints for a reasonably airtight basement. Size up for a leaky or indoor-venting dryer, an uncovered sump pit (it is open water), laundry drying on a line, a basement shower, never-sealed block walls, or a basement open to a vented crawlspace. Size down slightly if the space has an air-conditioning supply, which removes some moisture in summer.

When torn between two sizes, buy the larger. An oversized dehumidifier reaches its set point and switches off; an undersized one runs continuously, never gets there, and wears out sooner.

💡 Measure before you buy. Leave a digital hygrometer in the basement for three or four days with the windows shut. Below 55% with no smell, you may not need a dehumidifier. Between 60% and 70%, the table applies. Above 75%, or if the reading barely moves after a week of dehumidifying, you are almost certainly dealing with a water source, not just humid air.

Cold Basements and Low-Temperature Performance

A compressor dehumidifier blows room air across a refrigerated coil, where water condenses and drips away. The colder the room, the closer the coil runs to freezing, and below roughly 65°F it starts to accumulate frost. Frost insulates the coil, capacity drops further, and the unit has to pause to melt it off.

Almost every current portable has automatic defrost, which prevents damage, but every minute defrosting is a minute not removing water. At 60°F a standard portable still works, below its label; in the low 50s°F many spend so much time defrosting that real output becomes modest. The minimum operating temperature most list, around 41°F, is where they will run, not where they work well.

What Works Better in the Cold

Low-temperature compressor units. Whole-house and LGR (low-grain refrigerant) units use larger coils, tighter refrigerant control and more effective defrost — often hot gas from the compressor rather than just the fan — so they keep working into the 40s and 50s°F.

Desiccant dehumidifiers. These pass air over a rotating wheel of moisture-absorbing material, then use a heater to drive the water off. With no cold coil, cold does not stall them. They are smaller in capacity, use more electricity per pint in warm conditions, and warm the room by several degrees — often welcome in a cold basement.

A little heat. Raising the basement to 62–65°F brings an ordinary portable back into its effective range. Many cold basements are only humid from late spring to early autumn, when they are warmest anyway, so watch a hygrometer for a year before buying a specialist unit.

Drainage: Hose, Pump or Bucket

A 50-pint dehumidifier working hard removes about six gallons of water a day, and most portable buckets hold well under two. In a damp basement a bucket-only setup means emptying it three or four times a day at first, and the unit stops every time it fills. That is why drainage matters more than almost any other feature.

Gravity hose. Most units have a garden-hose connection. The hose must fall continuously to a floor drain, sump pit or utility sink below the outlet, with no loops or upward sections where water can sit and back up. Because the outlet is low on the unit, this usually means a nearby floor drain or raising the unit on a platform. With no moving parts, it is the most reliable option.

Built-in pump. A built-in pump pushes water through a thin tube up to around 15–16 feet vertically — enough to reach a laundry sink, run along the joists to a drain, or exit through the rim joist. The pump is also the most common part to fail, so check it when you clean the filter, keep the tube free of kinks, and make sure an outside discharge cannot freeze.

External pump or bucket. A unit without a pump can feed an external condensate pump, ideally one with an overflow switch that cuts power to the dehumidifier. The bucket is a fallback, acceptable only in a small, mildly damp space.

What Humidity to Aim For

The EPA recommends keeping indoor humidity between 30% and 50%, and below 60% in all cases to limit mould. For a basement, around 50% is the practical target: low enough that mould, mildew and dust mites struggle, without running the unit hard to chase a lower figure. Cold walls and slabs sit at a higher surface humidity than the room air, and holding the air near 50% keeps them out of the danger zone.

Do not trust the unit's own display too far. Built-in humidistats measure air at the intake and are often several points off. Put a separate hygrometer away from the unit and adjust the set point until the hygrometer reads around 50%.

Whole-House and Ducted Units

Whole-house dehumidifiers from makers such as Santa Fe (Therma-Stor), Aprilaire and Honeywell Home are a different class of machine: built for continuous duty, rated for low-temperature operation, more efficient per pint, and fitted with pleated filters rather than a mesh screen. Capacity typically runs from about 70 to more than 130 pints a day. Manufacturers sometimes quote capacity at hotter, wetter conditions than the portable test, so compare like with like.

Installed standalone, the unit sits in the basement or crawlspace and needs only an outlet and a drain. Ducted, it connects to the air handler or its own ductwork, treats the whole house, and can hide in a utility room.

Expect a ducted installation to cost roughly $1,500–$3,500 including the unit. It makes sense for large or chronically humid basements, sealed crawlspaces (building codes recognise dehumidification as one way to condition them), humid upper floors, or when the alternative is two or three portables running all summer. For an ordinary basement, a good portable is still the better value.

Energy Use and Running Cost

Dehumidifier efficiency is stated as the Integrated Energy Factor (IEF), in litres removed per kilowatt-hour; higher is better. Federal standards set a minimum for each size class and Energy Star models must beat it by a meaningful margin. A unit at 2.0 L/kWh uses 20% less electricity per pint than one at 1.6.

Estimating Your Own Cost

A typical 50-pint portable draws around 450–650 watts with the compressor running. Take 550 watts and $0.17 per kilowatt-hour, close to the recent US residential average:

  • First two to four weeks, running around the clock: 0.55 kW × 24 h = 13.2 kWh a day, about $2.25 a day.
  • Steady state, about 10 hours a day: 0.55 kW × 10 h = 5.5 kWh, about $0.94 a day or $28 a month.
  • A five-month humid season at steady state: about $140, plus the initial drawdown.

Substitute the rate from your own bill; in high-cost states the same hours can cost double. A unit with an IEF a quarter higher costs roughly a quarter less to run, which over a few summers usually covers the premium for an Energy Star model.

To cut the cost, keep windows and the door upstairs shut, lid the sump pit, and stop seepage — every gallon that comes in is a gallon you pay to remove.

Placement, Noise and Maintenance

Placement. Put the unit as near the centre of the damp area as the drain allows, with at least a foot of clear space around it. Tucked into a corner behind boxes, it dries the corner and little else. In a long basement, a small fan at the far end helps move air towards it. Plug it straight into a grounded, GFCI-protected outlet, not an extension cord.

Noise. A portable is roughly as loud as a window air conditioner on low. In a finished basement used as living space, prefer a quiet low fan setting or an inverter model, stand it on a rubber mat to stop vibration carrying through the floor, and keep it off the wall shared with a bedroom. A ducted unit in a utility closet is the quietest option.

Maintenance. Basements are dusty and the washable mesh filter clogs quickly: check it every two to four weeks in the humid season and rinse it under the tap. A clogged filter cuts airflow and capacity and makes the compressor work harder. Once a year, unplugged, brush dust from the coil fins, wipe out the bucket and flush the drain line.

Lifespan. Portables commonly last a few years to perhaps ten, less if undersized. Register the warranty and check the CPSC recall database occasionally — dehumidifiers have been recalled over overheating.

When a Dehumidifier Is the Wrong Fix

A dehumidifier removes water vapour from the air. It does nothing about liquid water entering through the foundation. Treat any of these as a water problem first:

  • Puddles or wet patches on the floor after rain
  • Damp bands along the base of walls, or water at the joint where wall meets floor
  • White, powdery efflorescence on block or poured concrete — salts left as water passes through
  • Peeling paint or bubbling coatings on foundation walls
  • A humidity reading that barely moves after a week of continuous running

In each case water is moving through the masonry and the dehumidifier is evaporating it from the inside face, at your expense, while the wall stays wet. Start outside: overflowing gutters dumping water against the foundation are the most common cause, which is why keeping gutters clear is cheap basement protection. Grading should slope away and downspouts discharge several feet out.

Inside, our guide on how to waterproof basement walls covers diagnosis and the repair sequence, from hydraulic cement for active leaks to coatings for general dampness. For seepage through sound walls, a basement wall sealer is the usual next step, and hollow block foundations, which wick water readily, are covered in our cinder block sealer guide. If the slab itself is damp, any floor finish needs to be breathable — see the concrete floor paint guide.

💡 Think about power cuts. If your basement relies on a sump pump, the pump is the critical load in a storm, not the dehumidifier. A battery backup pump or a generator sized for outages protects the basement far more, and auto-restart on the dehumidifier takes care of the rest when power returns.

The Short Version

Buy a 50-pint dehumidifier rated under the 2019 DOE standard, Energy Star certified, with a built-in pump, auto-defrost and auto-restart. If a floor drain sits where the unit will go, skip the pump and drain by gravity. Drop to 35 pints only for a small or finished basement; go to two units or a whole-house model for large or very wet spaces, and to a low-temperature or desiccant unit if the basement sits in the 50s°F.

Ignore advice to buy a "70-pint" — that is the pre-2019 label for today's 50-pint. Set it to about 50%, verify with a separate hygrometer, clean the filter every few weeks, and if water is coming through the walls, fix that first.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size dehumidifier do I need for a basement?

For most basements, a 50-pint unit under the 2019 Department of Energy rating, which handles a moderately to very damp basement up to roughly 1,500–2,000 square feet. A small or finished basement under about 800 square feet with a mild musty smell can manage with 35 pints. Basements over 2,000 square feet, or any with condensation on the walls and damp slab patches, usually need two 50-pint units or a whole-house dehumidifier.

Is a 70-pint dehumidifier the same as a 50-pint?

Broadly, yes. In 2019 the Department of Energy changed the test conditions for portable dehumidifiers from 80°F to 65°F at 60% relative humidity. Cooler air gives up less water, so the same machines now carry lower ratings: an old 70-pint unit is labelled roughly 50 pints today, and an old 50-pint about 35. Some listings still quote the older figure, so check which standard applies.

Do I need a dehumidifier with a pump for my basement?

Usually, unless a floor drain or sump pit is close to where the unit will sit. A 50-pint unit in a damp basement can remove several gallons a day while buckets hold under two, so bucket-only means emptying it several times daily and the unit stops whenever it fills. A built-in pump lifts water around 15–16 feet to a sink, a drain line or outside; a gravity hose works only if it can fall continuously to a drain.

What humidity should a basement be?

Around 50%. The EPA recommends 30–50% indoors and below 60% in all cases to limit mould. Holding basement air near 50% keeps the cooler wall and slab surfaces below the level where mould and mildew thrive, without running the dehumidifier hard for a lower figure. Check with a separate hygrometer, because built-in humidistats are often several points off.

Will a dehumidifier work in a cold basement?

A standard compressor unit will run, but below about 65°F its coil begins to frost, capacity falls and it spends time defrosting. In the low 50s°F many portables deliver only a fraction of their rating, even though most list a minimum operating temperature around 41°F. For a basement in the 50s, choose a whole-house or LGR unit specified for low-temperature operation, or a desiccant dehumidifier, which has no coil to freeze.

How much does it cost to run a basement dehumidifier?

Typically $20–$40 a month in the humid season for a 50-pint portable, more in the first few weeks. A unit drawing about 550 watts costs roughly $2.25 a day running around the clock at $0.17 per kilowatt-hour, which is common during the initial drawdown. Once it cycles about ten hours a day, that falls below $1 a day.

Should a basement dehumidifier run all the time?

Leave it switched on through the humid season with the humidistat at about 50%, and it will cycle off when it reaches the target. Running nonstop is normal for the first few weeks while it draws moisture out of the concrete and framing. If it still runs around the clock after a month, it is undersized, the filter is clogged, the basement is too cold for it, or water is getting in through the walls or floor.

Can a dehumidifier fix a wet basement?

No. A dehumidifier removes water vapour from the air; it cannot stop liquid water coming through the foundation. Puddles after rain, damp bands at the base of walls, white efflorescence, or a humidity reading that will not drop all mean water is passing through the masonry. Fix the source first — gutters, downspouts and grading outside, then crack repair and a masonry waterproofer — and the dehumidifier handles the remaining humid air far more cheaply.
