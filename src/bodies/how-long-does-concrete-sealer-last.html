How Long Does Concrete Sealer Last?

Anywhere from one year to the life of the slab. The answer depends almost entirely on whether the sealer sits on top of the concrete or inside it — and on what the surface has to put up with.

A concrete sealer lasts anywhere from one year to the life of the slab, and the single biggest factor is not brand or price but where the sealer ends up. Film-forming sealers — acrylics, polyurethanes and epoxies — sit on the surface and wear away under tyres, feet and sunlight, so they need recoating on a cycle measured in years. Penetrating sealers — silanes, siloxanes and densifiers — soak into the pores and react inside the concrete, where nothing can scuff them off, so they last far longer.

Within each type, the location does the rest. The same acrylic that lasts three years on a shaded patio can be gone from the wheel tracks of a north-facing driveway in a single salted winter. This guide gives realistic ranges for each sealer type, shows how they shift by location, and explains how to tell when yours has stopped working — which, for the longest-lasting sealers, is something you cannot see.

⚡ Typical service life by type: Acrylic (solvent- or water-based) 1–3 years. Polyurethane 3–5+ years. Epoxy, indoors 5–10 years. Penetrating silane/siloxane 5–10+ years. Densifiers (lithium, sodium or potassium silicate) effectively permanent — they become part of the concrete. Exterior driveways sit at the low end of every range; interior floors and covered patios at the high end.

Acrylic Sealers: 1–3 Years

Acrylics are the most common sealer on residential driveways, patios and stamped concrete because they are cheap, easy to roll or spray, and give the colour-enhancing "wet look" people want on decorative work. They are also the shortest-lived, because the film is thin — typically a couple of mils — and it sits entirely on the surface where traffic, sunlight and weather attack it every day.

Solvent-based acrylics generally penetrate a little further, build a slightly tougher film and hold their gloss longer than water-based versions, so they tend toward the upper end of the range. They also have a maintenance advantage covered in the reapplication section: the solvent in a fresh coat softens and re-dissolves the old one, so the layers fuse rather than stack. Water-based acrylics are lower in odour and VOCs and are the only option in some states with strict VOC rules, but in exterior use they commonly need recoating every one to two years.

Higher solids content helps. A 25–30% solids acrylic leaves noticeably more film behind than a 15% product, and will outlast it, though going too heavy on a single coat causes its own problems — trapped solvent and whitening, which we cover in our guide to why concrete sealer turns white.

Polyurethane Sealers: 3–5+ Years

Polyurethane forms a harder, more abrasion- and chemical-resistant film than acrylic at a similar thickness, which is why it lasts roughly twice as long. On a garage floor it is most often used as a clear topcoat over epoxy, where it takes the wear and protects the epoxy underneath. Outdoors, only aliphatic polyurethanes are suitable, because they resist UV yellowing; aromatic versions amber in sunlight much as epoxy does.

The trade-offs are that polyurethane is less breathable than acrylic, so it is more vulnerable to moisture pushing up from below, and it is harder to recoat. A fresh coat will not bond to cured polyurethane unless the surface is scuff-sanded first.

Epoxy: 5–10 Years Indoors

Epoxy builds the thickest film of any common sealer — often 10 mils or more for high-solids products — and that thickness is why it lasts. It is the standard for garage and basement floors. It is not an exterior sealer: sunlight yellows and chalks it, and because it is effectively impermeable it traps moisture in outdoor slabs, which leads to blistering and delamination. Indoors, with a polyurethane or polyaspartic topcoat over it, a well-prepared epoxy floor can run well past ten years.

Penetrating Silane and Siloxane: 5–10+ Years

Silanes and siloxanes soak into the concrete and chemically bond to the inside of the pores, leaving a water-repellent lining rather than a surface film. Because there is no film, there is nothing for tyres to scuff, nothing for UV to degrade at the surface and nothing to peel. The concrete looks and feels essentially unchanged, and water vapour can still escape — which matters a great deal for exterior slabs in freeze–thaw climates.

Life depends on penetration depth and how much of the surface wears away. Silanes have smaller molecules and penetrate deeper into dense concrete; siloxanes are larger and work better on more porous surfaces; blends aim for both. Higher-concentration products (40% silane and up) tend to last longest, and some manufacturers rate their products for ten years or more on vertical surfaces. Horizontal driveways see more abrasion, so five to seven years is a more typical real-world figure there. Our silane vs siloxane comparison covers which suits which slab.

Densifiers: Effectively Permanent

Lithium, sodium and potassium silicate densifiers react with the free calcium hydroxide in concrete to form additional calcium silicate hydrate — the same binder that holds concrete together. The result is a harder, denser, more dust-resistant surface. Because the product becomes concrete rather than coating it, it does not wear off any faster than the concrete itself.

The catch is that a densifier on its own is not a water or stain repellent. It reduces porosity but does not stop water and oil soaking in. That is why densifiers are usually paired with a penetrating repellent or a light guard coat, and why "permanent" applies to the hardening, not to protection against salt and stains.

Driveways

The harshest residential location. A driveway takes direct sun, tyre abrasion concentrated in two narrow lanes, turning tyres that scrub the surface, de-icing salt, freeze–thaw cycling and standing water. Film sealers wear first in the wheel tracks and at the apron where cars turn in, so a driveway can look sealed at the edges and bare where it matters. This is the strongest case for a penetrating sealer, and why most of the picks in our concrete driveway sealer reviews are silane or siloxane products. If you are sealing for the first time, our step-by-step guide to sealing a concrete driveway covers preparation and application.

Patios

Patios get UV and weather but only foot traffic and furniture, so film sealers last noticeably longer than on driveways. Covered patios and pool surrounds in shade can push acrylics to three years or beyond. Pool decks are a special case: chlorinated splash-out and constant wet–dry cycling are hard on films, and a slick wet-look acrylic is a slip hazard, so a penetrating sealer or an acrylic with a grit additive is usually the better choice.

Garage Floors

Garage floors avoid UV but get hot tyres, oil, brake fluid, salt-laden slush and dragged equipment. Acrylics are vulnerable to hot-tyre pickup, where warm tyres soften the film and lift it when the car pulls out, leaving tread-shaped bare patches. Epoxy with a polyurethane or polyaspartic topcoat is the long-life option here. A penetrating sealer protects the slab from salt damage but will not stop oil from staining, because oil is not repelled by a silane the way water is.

Basement Floors

No UV, little abrasion and steady temperatures should make basements the easiest environment, and they are — on a dry slab. The threat is moisture vapour rising from below. A slab poured without a vapour barrier can push water up hard enough to whiten, blister or lift an impermeable film. Run a moisture test before sealing: tape a two-foot square of plastic tightly to the slab for 24–48 hours and check for condensation underneath. If it fails, choose a breathable product — a penetrating sealer or a breathable acrylic rather than epoxy.

Countertops

Countertops are the outlier. Wear is light but the demands are different: hot pans, acidic food, wine, oil and daily wiping. Most concrete countertop systems use a topical sealer plus a sacrificial wax or finishing polish that is renewed every few weeks to months, and the sealer itself typically needs attention every one to three years in a busy kitchen. Countertop-specific products are formulated for food contact once cured; do not use a driveway sealer indoors on a food surface. Our concrete countertop sealer guide covers the options.

What Shortens Sealer Life

When a sealer fails early, it is almost always one of five things — and the first four are within your control.

1. Poor Surface Preparation

The leading cause of early failure by a wide margin. A film sealer bonds only as well as the surface allows: dirt, efflorescence, oil, old curing compound or a previous incompatible sealer all prevent adhesion, and the new coat peels within months. Penetrating sealers fail in a different way — they simply cannot get in. A slab that has not been cleaned thoroughly, or that still carries a film from an old acrylic, will absorb little or nothing, and the "sealed" surface is protected only for as long as the product sitting on top lasts. Pressure washing, degreasing and allowing the slab to dry completely (typically 24–48 hours) are the difference between the low and high end of every range on this page.

2. UV Exposure

Sunlight breaks down the polymer chains in film sealers, which is why south-facing driveways lose gloss first and why epoxy is unsuitable outdoors. Acrylics chalk and dull; aromatic polyurethanes and epoxies yellow. Penetrating sealers are largely unaffected because the active material sits below the surface.

3. Traffic and Abrasion

Tyres, especially turning tyres, grind away film sealers fastest. Snow shovels and plough blades scrape them. Even foot traffic wears a visible path through a gloss acrylic on a busy patio within a couple of years. For penetrating sealers, abrasion matters only to the extent that it erodes the concrete surface itself — slow on good concrete, faster on a weak, dusty or poorly finished slab.

4. De-Icing Salt and Chemicals

Salt brine sits on the surface, attacks acrylic films and, where it gets into the concrete, drives the freeze–thaw scaling that sealing is meant to prevent. Calcium chloride and magnesium chloride are harder on both concrete and sealers than rock salt, and ammonium-based de-icers are worse still. If you must use something, our guide to the best ice melt for concrete explains which are gentlest. Petrol, brake fluid and harsh cleaners attack many films too.

5. Moisture From Below

Water vapour moving up through a slab — common in basements, slabs on wet ground, and any concrete without a vapour barrier — pushes against an impermeable film. Over time this causes whitening, blistering and peeling. Breathable penetrating sealers let vapour escape and are unaffected. Applying a film sealer to concrete that is still damp from washing or rain does the same damage on a shorter timeline.

💡 New concrete: Most sealer manufacturers want new concrete cured for around 28 days before application, so moisture and alkalinity have dropped. Some penetrating sealers and cure-and-seal products are designed for earlier use — follow the label. Sealing a green slab with a standard film sealer is a reliable way to cut its life in half.

Signs Your Sealer Is Worn Out

The Water Test

The most reliable check, and the only one that works on penetrating sealers, takes a minute. Sprinkle or pour a cup of water on several spots — wheel tracks, the apron, the edges and a low-traffic area for comparison.

  • Water beads up or sits on the surface without darkening it: the sealer is still working.
  • The concrete darkens slowly over several minutes: protection is fading. Plan to reseal within the next season.
  • The concrete darkens within a minute and the water soaks in: the sealer is gone in that area. Reseal.

Expect results to vary across the slab. Wheel tracks and the turning area of a driveway usually fail a year or two before the edges. That is normal, and it tells you where to concentrate your attention.

Visual Signs on Film Sealers

Acrylics and other films announce their decline. Look for:

  • Loss of gloss or colour depth, especially in traffic lanes — the wet look fades to a dull, patchy finish.
  • Uneven appearance, where some areas are still glossy and others look bare.
  • Peeling or flaking, usually a sign of poor adhesion or moisture rather than normal wear.
  • White or cloudy patches, which usually mean trapped moisture or too heavy an application rather than age.
  • Stains soaking in quickly where they used to wipe off.

Signs on Penetrating Sealers

There are few. A silane or siloxane leaves no visible film, so you cannot see it wearing. The water test is the indicator. Secondary signs are concrete that stays dark for a long time after rain, new surface scaling in winter, or efflorescence appearing where it had not before — all signs that water is getting in again.

How to Make Sealer Last Longer

Prepare thoroughly. Everything else is secondary. Clean, degrease, remove failed old coatings, and let the slab dry fully before sealing. A sealer applied to a properly prepared surface can last twice as long as the same product applied to a dirty one.

Apply at the right rate. For film sealers, two thin coats outlast one heavy one: thin coats release their solvent or water properly and cure clear. For penetrating sealers the reverse logic applies — the concrete needs enough product to saturate the pores. Follow the label coverage rate and apply until the surface stays wet for a few minutes without puddling.

Seal in the right conditions. Most products want temperatures between roughly 50°F and 90°F, no rain for 24 hours, and no direct midday sun on a hot slab, which flashes off solvent before the sealer can level or penetrate.

Keep it clean. Sweep grit off film sealers, because grit under tyres and shoes acts like sandpaper. Wash periodically with a pH-neutral cleaner and avoid harsh acids or strong solvents.

Go easy on de-icers. Shovel early so less ice forms, use sand for grip where you can, and avoid chloride and ammonium de-icers on concrete less than a year old. Rinse salt residue off in spring.

Protect garage floors from hot tyres. Let an acrylic cure fully — often several days — before driving on it, and consider a hot-tyre-resistant topcoat or a different system if pickup is a recurring problem.

Reseal before failure, not after. Recoating a film sealer while some of it remains is far easier than starting from bare concrete — and for a driveway that sees salt, a lapsed penetrating sealer means winters of unprotected concrete.

Reapplying: What to Know First

Recoating Acrylics: Match the Chemistry

The general rule is to recoat with the same chemistry that is already on the slab. A solvent-based acrylic over an existing solvent-based acrylic fuses well, because the solvent re-dissolves the top of the old film. That is also why a dull or lightly blushed solvent acrylic can sometimes be revived with a thin recoat, or even a pass of xylene, rather than being stripped.

Mixing chemistries is where trouble starts. A water-based acrylic generally does not bond well over a solvent-based film, and a solvent-based product can soften, wrinkle or lift a water-based one. If you do not know what was used before, test a small inconspicuous area: apply the new product, let it cure for 24–48 hours, then check for wrinkling, whitening or poor adhesion with a piece of tape pressed firmly and pulled off. If in doubt, strip back to bare concrete.

Clean the old film thoroughly before recoating, and apply thinly. Building coat upon coat over the years leads to a thick film that traps moisture and turns white or peels, so occasional stripping and a fresh start is normal maintenance on decorative concrete.

Polyurethane and Epoxy: Scuff First

Cured polyurethane and epoxy do not re-dissolve, so a new coat has nothing to grab unless the old surface is abraded. Clean, degrease, scuff-sand or lightly screen the existing coating, vacuum, and then apply. Most manufacturers specify a recoat window — if you are within it, the next coat bonds chemically without sanding; outside it, abrasion is required.

Penetrating Sealers Need Clean, Bare Concrete

Silanes and siloxanes only work if they can get into the pores. They cannot penetrate through an acrylic, polyurethane or epoxy film, and they cannot penetrate through dirt, oil or a curing compound. If a slab has ever had a film sealer, it must be stripped completely before a penetrating sealer will do anything.

Reapplying a penetrating sealer over an older application of the same type is straightforward once the old one has worn: clean the slab, let it dry, and apply. If the water test still shows strong beading, there is no point reapplying — the product will sit on the surface rather than soak in, because the pores are still treated. That is a useful built-in check: reapply when the concrete starts absorbing water again, not on a calendar.

You can, however, put a compatible film sealer over a penetrating one in some cases — certain systems are designed for it — but you cannot reliably do the reverse. If you are switching from acrylic to penetrating for a longer-lasting driveway, budget for stripping. The best concrete sealer guide notes which products are designed to work together.

A Realistic Resealing Schedule

Rather than a fixed calendar, use the water test every spring and reseal where it fails. As a starting point:

  • Acrylic on a driveway: check every spring; most need recoating every one to two years, sooner in heavy salt areas.
  • Acrylic on a patio or covered area: every two to three years.
  • Polyurethane topcoat on a garage floor: inspect the traffic lanes yearly; recoat every four to seven years before wear reaches the base coat.
  • Silane/siloxane on a driveway: water-test every year from year three; reseal typically at five to seven years.
  • Silane/siloxane on a patio or wall: water-test from year five; many last ten years or more.
  • Densifier: never needs reapplication, but the repellent or guard paired with it follows its own schedule.

Cost per year often favours the longer-lasting option. A penetrating sealer may cost two to three times as much per gallon as an acrylic, but if it lasts three or four times as long and avoids the labour of repeated recoats and occasional stripping, it is cheaper over a decade. The Short Version

If you want the longest gap between applications on exterior concrete, choose a penetrating silane or siloxane and expect five to ten years, with driveways at the lower end. If you want the wet look on stamped or decorative concrete, accept that acrylic means recoating every one to three years and budget for occasional stripping. For garage and basement floors, epoxy with a polyurethane or polyaspartic topcoat gives the longest life indoors.

Whatever you use, preparation determines whether you reach the top or the bottom of the range, and the water test — not the calendar — tells you when it is time to reseal.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should you seal concrete?

It depends on the sealer. Acrylic sealers on driveways typically need recoating every one to two years, and every two to three on patios and covered areas. Polyurethane lasts three to five years or more. Penetrating silane and siloxane sealers usually last five to ten years, with driveways nearer five to seven because of tyre abrasion and salt. Densifiers never need reapplying, though the water repellent often paired with them does. The most reliable approach is a yearly water test: if water soaks in and darkens the concrete within a minute, it is time to reseal that area.

What is the longest-lasting concrete sealer?

Densifiers are permanent because they react with the concrete to form more of its own binder, but they are hardeners rather than water repellents. Among sealers that actually repel water, penetrating silanes and siloxanes last longest — typically five to ten years and sometimes more, especially on vertical or low-traffic surfaces. High-concentration silanes (40% and up) generally penetrate deepest. Indoors, epoxy with a polyurethane or polyaspartic topcoat can last well over ten years. Film sealers such as acrylics are the shortest-lived at one to three years.

How do I know if my concrete sealer is still working?

Do the water test. Sprinkle water on several areas, including wheel tracks and low-traffic spots. If it beads or sits on the surface without darkening the concrete, the sealer is working. If the concrete darkens slowly, protection is fading. If it darkens within a minute and the water soaks in, the sealer has worn off there. On film sealers you may also see loss of gloss, patchy wear in traffic lanes, peeling or white haze. Penetrating sealers leave no visible film, so the water test is the only practical way to tell.

Can I put new sealer over old sealer?

Often yes, if the chemistries match. Solvent-based acrylic over solvent-based acrylic bonds well because the new solvent softens and fuses with the old film. Mixing water-based and solvent-based acrylics can cause poor adhesion, wrinkling or whitening, so test a small area first if you are not sure what is there. Polyurethane and epoxy need scuff-sanding before recoating unless you are within the manufacturer's recoat window. Penetrating sealers cannot go over any film sealer at all — the old film must be stripped to bare concrete first.

Does concrete sealer wear off?

Film-forming sealers do. Acrylic, polyurethane and epoxy sit on the surface, and tyres, foot traffic, shovels, UV and salt erode them, usually first in wheel tracks and walkways. Penetrating silanes and siloxanes do not wear off in the same way because they are inside the pores rather than on top; they decline slowly as the concrete surface itself erodes and the repellent chemistry ages. Densifiers do not wear off at all, because they become part of the concrete.

Is it bad to seal concrete too often?

With film sealers, yes. Recoating an acrylic every year whether it needs it or not builds up a thick film that traps moisture, turns cloudy or white, and eventually peels, at which point the whole lot has to be stripped. Recoat only when the water test or visible wear shows it is needed, and keep coats thin. With penetrating sealers, over-application is mostly wasted money: if the old treatment is still working, the new product will not soak in and simply sits on the surface, where it can leave a residue.

How long does sealer last on a concrete driveway specifically?

Typically one to two years for an acrylic and five to seven years for a good penetrating silane or siloxane. Driveways are the toughest residential location: direct sun, concentrated tyre wear in two lanes, scrubbing at the turning apron, de-icing salt and freeze–thaw cycles. In mild climates with little salt, acrylics may reach three years and penetrating sealers ten. In northern states with heavy salting, expect the lower end of each range and check the wheel tracks every spring.

Do densifiers replace a concrete sealer?

Not entirely. A lithium, sodium or potassium silicate densifier hardens the concrete permanently, reduces dusting and improves abrasion resistance, but it does not make the surface water- or stain-repellent. Water, salt brine and oil can still soak in. That is why densifiers are usually followed by a penetrating silane or siloxane on exterior concrete, or a guard coat on interior floors. The densifier never needs reapplying; the repellent or guard over it does, on its own schedule.
