Drive-Maxx vs Latex-ite: Which Driveway Sealer Is Better?

Short answer: on paper the Latex-ite pails go further per coat and the top Latex-ite tier cures in colder weather, while Black Jack Drive-Maxx is cheaper per pail but requires two coats. Here is how the two brands compare tier by tier, using each manufacturer's own technical data sheets.

"Drive-Maxx vs Latex-ite" is one of the most common questions people ask in the driveway sealer aisle, and for good reason: the two brands sit side by side at Home Depot, Lowe's, Ace and independent hardware stores, the pails look almost identical, both are water-based asphalt emulsions, and both are sold in tiers with year ratings printed on the label. Picking between them on the shelf usually comes down to price and the number on the pail. That is a reasonable start, but it misses the differences that actually decide how many pails you need, what weekend you can apply them, and how long the driveway stays closed.

This comparison is built from research, not a hands-on test. We did not seal driveways with these products. Instead we worked from each manufacturer's published technical data sheets and label directions — Black Jack's sheets for the Drive-Maxx 500, 700 and 1000, and Latex-ite's sheets for Ultra Shield and Airport Grade — plus the general chemistry of asphalt-emulsion sealers. Where a figure comes from an older revision of a sheet, or where we could not confirm it, we say so. The label on the pail you actually buy is always the final word.

⚡ Quick Answer: Both brands make competent water-based asphalt sealers. Black Jack Drive-Maxx requires two thin coats at about 300–350 sq ft per 4.75-gallon pail per coat, so one pail covers roughly 150–175 sq ft finished. Latex-ite's sheets list 300–500 sq ft per pail and treat the second coat as optional. For a cold-season job, Latex-ite Ultra Shield (40°F and rising on its 2019 sheet) and Drive-Maxx 1000 (45°F) are the two to look at; the other tiers need 55°F.

The Short Answer

If you want one sentence: buy the top tier of whichever brand your store stocks, apply it in the right weather, and follow its coat instructions exactly — the gap between brands at the same tier is smaller than the gap between a well-applied and a badly applied sealer. But the brands are not interchangeable, and three differences are worth knowing before you load the cart.

1. Coats and coverage. Every Drive-Maxx data sheet we read says the same thing in capitals: two thin coats are required, applied perpendicular to each other. Coverage is about 350 square feet per coat on smooth driveways and 300 on rough or worn ones, per 4.75-gallon pail. Latex-ite's Ultra Shield and Airport Grade sheets list 400–500 square feet per pail on smooth or previously sealed driveways and 300–400 on rough or unsealed surfaces, and describe the second coat as something you can add ("if needed" on Airport Grade). In practice many homeowners do two coats of either brand, but the labels set different expectations, and that changes how many pails you buy.

2. Cold-weather tolerance. Standard Drive-Maxx (500 and 700) and Latex-ite Airport Grade all call for 55°F and rising. Drive-Maxx 1000 adds what Black Jack calls ClimaSeal technology and allows application at 45°F and rising. Latex-ite's 2019 Ultra Shield sheet lists ThermaSEAL technology and application at 40°F and rising (an earlier revision listed 55°F, so check your pail). If you are sealing in late October, that matters more than anything else on this page.

3. Speed. Drive-Maxx is built around fast recoat times: the second coat goes on after roughly 3–6 hours (2–4 hours for the 1000), and the 1000 is marketed as ready for light foot traffic in one hour in good drying conditions. Latex-ite's sheets list a second coat 24 hours after the first. Both brands say to keep vehicles off for at least 24 hours, and Latex-ite lists 24–48 hours of cure time at 70°F and 50% humidity. If you want the whole job done in one day, Drive-Maxx's directions are written for that; Latex-ite's are written for a two-day job if you do two coats.

Who Makes Each Sealer

Black Jack is a brand of Gardner-Gibson, based in Tampa, Florida, which also makes roof coatings and patching products. The Drive-Maxx line is its main range of blacktop filler-sealers, and the model number tracks the rated life on the pail: the 500 is sold as a 5-year product, the 700 as a 7-year product and the 1000 as a 10-year product. Black Jack also sells lower tiers (a Drive-Maxx 300 and a basic 2-year filler-sealer) and a separate crack filler and patch range — Speed-Fill and Speed-Patch — that its sealer sheets tell you to use first.

Latex-ite is made by Dalton Enterprises of Cheshire, Connecticut. The name refers to the latex rubber polymers added to its asphalt emulsions. Its best-known sealers are Ultra Shield, Airport Grade (now sold as Airport Grade+), and Optimum, alongside crack fillers such as Pli-Stix and a cleaner/degreaser and oil spot primer that its sealer sheets call for in prep. We cover the Optimum and Airport Grade in more depth in our Latex-ite driveway sealer reviews, and Ultra Shield against Airport Grade in Latexite Ultrashield vs Airport Grade.

Chemically, the two brands are the same kind of product. Both are asphalt emulsions: tiny droplets of asphalt suspended in water with emulsifiers, clay and fillers, modified with polymers (latex in both cases, and urethane in Drive-Maxx 1000) to make the cured film more flexible. Both dry by evaporation, both clean up with soap and water while wet, both list very low VOC content, and neither is a coal tar sealer. If you are choosing between asphalt emulsion and other sealer chemistries in general, see coal tar vs asphalt emulsion sealer and oil-based vs water-based driveway sealer.

Matching the Tiers: 500, 700, 1000 vs Airport Grade and Ultra Shield

Most "Drive-Maxx vs Latex-ite" comparisons go wrong by comparing a top-tier pail from one brand with a mid-tier pail from the other. The fairer way is to line them up by what each manufacturer says about the product's rated life and features.

  • 10-year tier: Black Jack Drive-Maxx 1000 vs Latex-ite Ultra Shield. Both carry a 10-year rating (Drive-Maxx 1000 is sold as a 10-year filler-sealer; Latex-ite's Ultra Shield data sheet lists a 10-year warranty). Both are gel formulas with UV protection claims, and both are the cold-weather options in their range.
  • Mid tier: Black Jack Drive-Maxx 700 (7-year) and Drive-Maxx 500 (5-year) vs Latex-ite Airport Grade. Latex-ite's 2016 Airport Grade data sheet lists a 4-year warranty; the current Airport Grade+ pail is widely described by shoppers and some retailers as a 5-year product, but we could not confirm that from a current Latex-ite sheet, so read the label.
  • Entry tier: Drive-Maxx 300 and Black Jack's basic filler-sealers vs Latex-ite Optimum and the brand's lower-priced lines. We do not have a current data sheet for every entry product, so we focus on the tiers above.

A note on the year ratings, because they cause more confusion than anything else on these pails. A "10-year" rating is a manufacturer's limited warranty or durability claim under ideal prep, application and conditions. It is not a promise that you will not need to reseal for a decade. Thin-film asphalt emulsions wear fastest in tire paths and turning areas and in strong sun, and the pavement-maintenance industry generally recommends resealing when the surface turns gray and the aggregate begins to show. Our guide to how long driveway sealer lasts goes into why, and most homeowners end up resealing these products every two to four years regardless of the number on the label. Higher tiers do generally mean more polymer and better film properties, so the ratings are a useful ranking within a brand — just not a calendar.

Coverage and the Two-Coat Rule

Coverage is where the two brands look most different on paper, and it is the part most likely to change your shopping list. Take a typical two-car driveway of about 600 square feet that is smooth and has been sealed before.

With Drive-Maxx, the sheet says 350 square feet per coat on a smooth driveway, and two coats are required. That is 1,200 square feet of coverage needed in total (600 × 2), divided by 350 = about 3.4 pails. Buy four. On a rough or worn driveway at 300 square feet per coat, that becomes 4 pails exactly — buy five to be safe, because porous asphalt drinks the first coat.

With Latex-ite Ultra Shield or Airport Grade, the sheet says 400–500 square feet per pail on a smooth, previously sealed driveway. For one coat that is 1.2–1.5 pails; buy two. If you choose to do a second coat, which is common on worn driveways, you are at 2.4–3 pails; buy three. On rough or unsealed asphalt at 300–400 square feet per pail, a single coat takes 1.5–2 pails and two coats take 3–4.

So is Latex-ite really twice as thrifty? Not necessarily. Coverage figures are not measured the same way by different manufacturers, and the amount of sealer that actually lands on the driveway depends on your squeegee pressure, the porosity of the asphalt, and how thin you keep each coat. A Latex-ite coat spread at 500 square feet per pail is a thinner film than a Drive-Maxx coat at 350. What the labels really tell you is that Black Jack expects two thin coats every time and Latex-ite expects you to judge whether one is enough. If your driveway is worn, gray and porous, plan on two coats of either brand — see how many coats of driveway sealer for how to decide.

💡 Price per square foot, not per pail: Drive-Maxx pails are often a few dollars cheaper than the matching Latex-ite tier, but because Black Jack's directions call for two coats at 300–350 sq ft each, the cost per finished square foot can end up similar or higher. Divide the pail price by the finished square feet the label promises before you compare.

Temperature, Rain and Dry Times

Asphalt emulsions cure by evaporation: the water leaves, the asphalt droplets coalesce, and the polymers knit the film together. Anything that slows evaporation — cold, humidity, shade, a low autumn sun — slows the cure and raises the risk of tracking and wash-off. Both manufacturers write their directions around that.

Temperature. Drive-Maxx 500 and 700 need 55°F and rising during application but below 90°F, and the sheets add that you should not apply if temperatures below 50°F are expected overnight within 36 hours. Drive-Maxx 1000 widens the window to 45–99°F and says not to apply if temperatures below 45°F are expected overnight within 36 hours. Latex-ite Airport Grade needs 55°F and rising for both ground and air. Latex-ite Ultra Shield's 2019 sheet allows 40°F and rising thanks to ThermaSEAL; an earlier Ultra Shield sheet listed 55°F, so if your pail says 55°F, follow the pail. Latex-ite also warns against applying very late in the day, and notes that early spring or late fall application, when the sun is at a low angle, can stop the sealer curing properly and cause tracking. Our guide to what temperature to seal a driveway covers why air temperature alone is not enough.

Rain. Both brands ask for a 36-hour rain-free window. That is longer than the 24 hours many people assume. If a shower arrives inside that window, read what to do if it rains after sealing a driveway.

Dry and cure times. Drive-Maxx 500 and 700: second coat after about 3–6 hours, foot traffic after 2–4 hours, vehicles after 24–48 hours. Drive-Maxx 1000: second coat after about 2–4 hours, light foot traffic in 1–2 hours (one hour in what Black Jack defines as good conditions: above 75°F, clear sunny skies and humidity below 35%), and a minimum of 24 hours before vehicles. Latex-ite Ultra Shield and Airport Grade: second coat 24 hours after the first, and a cure time of 24–48 hours at 70°F and 50% relative humidity before the driveway is back in service. All of these lengthen in humid, cloudy or cool weather. Our main guide to how long driveway sealer takes to dry explains why dry-to-touch is not the same as ready for a car.

Application Differences That Matter

Beyond coverage and weather, the two brands' directions differ in a few small ways that are easy to miss and can affect the result.

Damp vs dry pavement. Black Jack tells you to clean and rinse the driveway, remove puddles, and apply the sealer to a slightly damp (not wet) surface. Latex-ite tells you to let the cleaned driveway dry completely — 24 hours is its recommendation — and to mist the surface lightly only when the pavement is very hot, leaving no standing water. Follow the directions for the brand you buy rather than mixing habits.

Squeegee only. Both brands want a squeegee. Black Jack says a squeegee must be used to spread the product and achieve the proper application rate, with an optional brush follow-up for texture. Latex-ite goes further: its sheets say a roller is the wrong applicator and voids the warranty, and tell you to drag the squeegee back and forth rather than push it.

Mixing and batches. Both say to flip the pails upside down the day before (Latex-ite says at least 24 hours) so settled solids redistribute. Black Jack recommends matching batch codes and blending the end of one pail into the start of the next to avoid color shading; Latex-ite recommends "boxing" pails — mixing them back and forth between buckets — for the same reason. Both say to finish the whole driveway in one continuous session.

Prep products. Black Jack's sheets point you to Speed-Fill Elastic Crack Filler for cracks, Speed-Patch for cracks and holes over 1 inch, and a permanent pothole patch that needs six weeks to cure before sealing. Latex-ite's sheets point you to its Cleaner/Degreaser and Oil Spot Primer (allow the primer half an hour to dry) and say to let crack fillers cure 24 hours, or until firm, before sealing. Either way, fill cracks wider than the sealer can handle first — our guide to the best asphalt crack filler covers the options, and how long driveway crack filler takes to dry covers the wait.

New asphalt and slopes. Black Jack says new asphalt must cure for at least six months or a full summer season; Latex-ite says a minimum of one full season. Black Jack also states that Drive-Maxx is not recommended or warranted on slopes greater than 20 degrees, which is worth knowing if your driveway is steep.

Which One Should You Buy?

Choose Latex-ite Ultra Shield if you want the most coverage per pail, the option of a single coat on a driveway that is in good shape, or a cooler-weather application window (where your pail lists 40°F). It also suits people who would rather spread the job over two days than rush coats.

Choose Black Jack Drive-Maxx 1000 if you want a two-coat job finished in one day, need a 45°F window, or your store stocks Black Jack and not Latex-ite. Budget for more pails.

Choose Drive-Maxx 500 or 700, or Latex-ite Airport Grade, if you are sealing in warm weather, the driveway is in reasonable condition, and price matters more than the rating on the pail. Compare price per finished square foot, not per pail.

Don't choose either if the driveway has alligator cracking, potholes, or base failure. No sealer fixes structural problems; see how to fix a crumbling asphalt driveway first. And if your driveway is concrete, neither brand is the right product — these are asphalt sealers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Drive-Maxx or Latex-ite better?

At the same tier, neither brand is clearly better on chemistry — both are polymer-modified asphalt emulsions. The real differences are in the directions. Drive-Maxx requires two coats at 300–350 square feet per coat and allows a fast recoat (2–6 hours depending on the tier). Latex-ite's Ultra Shield and Airport Grade list 300–500 square feet per pail with an optional second coat after 24 hours. For cold-weather work, Latex-ite Ultra Shield (40°F on its 2019 sheet) and Drive-Maxx 1000 (45°F) are the two to consider.

Can I put Latex-ite over Black Jack Drive-Maxx (or the other way round)?

Both are water-based asphalt emulsions, so a fully cured coat of one is generally a sound base for a later application of the other — for example, resealing with Latex-ite a few years after a Drive-Maxx job, once the old film is clean, sound and well worn. Mixing brands within the same job is a different matter: Black Jack's sheets say not to mix or blend Drive-Maxx with any other driveway product, and each brand's warranty assumes its own coat schedule. For a two-coat job, use the same product for both coats.

Which is better in hot weather?

Drive-Maxx 1000 lists the widest hot-weather range, up to 99°F; the 500 and 700 should be applied below 90°F. Latex-ite's sheets do not give a maximum but say to mist very hot pavement lightly before sealing, leaving no puddles. In any brand, avoid the hottest part of the afternoon, when the surface can skin over before the film underneath has cured.

Which holds up better in snow and freeze-thaw?

Neither manufacturer publishes freeze-thaw test results we could verify, so we can't rank them. Higher tiers in both brands use more flexible polymer systems, which helps a film ride out expansion and contraction, and both top tiers carry 10-year ratings. What matters most in snowy climates is applying in warm, dry weather with enough time to cure before the first freeze, and keeping metal plow and shovel blades off the surface.

What does the number on Drive-Maxx mean?

It tracks the rated life: Drive-Maxx 500 is sold as a 5-year product, 700 as 7-year and 1000 as 10-year. See our Drive-Maxx review for how the three differ.

How many pails do I need for a 600 sq ft driveway?

With Drive-Maxx (two required coats at about 350 sq ft per coat on a smooth driveway): about four pails, five on rough asphalt. With Latex-ite Ultra Shield or Airport Grade on a smooth, previously sealed driveway: two pails for one coat, three for two coats. Rough, unsealed asphalt needs more of either.
