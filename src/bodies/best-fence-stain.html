The 6 Best Fence Stains of 2026 — Picked for Spraying, Both Sides and Long Runs

A fence is not a vertical deck. It sheds water, takes no foot traffic and gets sprayed rather than rolled — which changes which stain is the right one, how much you need and how long it lasts.

The best fence stain for most people is a penetrating, oil-based semi-transparent stain that can be sprayed on without leaving lap marks — because a fence is hundreds of square feet of vertical boards, both sides, and the product that goes on fastest and fails most gracefully wins. On a fence, a semi-transparent stain that would last two or three years on a deck typically lasts three to five, since vertical boards shed water and nobody walks on them.

That changes the shopping list. Forgiveness during application matters more than ultimate toughness, the right sprayer matters more than the right brush, and the quantity is about double what most people estimate because they forget the second side. Below are six stains that suit fences specifically, followed by the coverage math, the preparation that decides whether any of them works, and realistic maintenance cycles.

⚡ Quick Answer: For a cedar or pressure-treated pine fence, Ready Seal Exterior Stain and Sealer for Wood (Natural Cedar) is the easiest stain to get a good result with — oil-based, sprayable, and formulated not to lap or streak. In hot, sunny climates, Defy Extreme Semi-Transparent Exterior Wood Stain holds colour better. On an old, grey fence you want to hide rather than show, use a solid colour stain. Budget roughly 1 gallon per 125–200 sq ft of fence face, and remember a fence has two faces: a 150-ft run of 6-ft privacy fence is about 1,800 sq ft both sides.

Why Fences Are Different From Decks

Most stain advice is written for decks, and most of it transfers. But five differences change which product makes sense on a fence and how you should apply it.

Vertical boards shed water. On a deck, rain sits on the boards, soaks into the end grain at every joint and stands in the cupped centre of each plank. On a fence it runs off within minutes. Less time wet means less mildew, less checking and slower breakdown of the finish. The same semi-transparent stain that needs recoating every two to three years on a sunny deck routinely lasts three to five on a fence.

No foot traffic. A deck finish is worn away mechanically as well as by weather — grit under shoes, chair legs, dogs. A fence finish only weathers. That means durability claims built around abrasion resistance matter far less, and it means film-forming products, which peel on decks partly because traffic breaks the film, behave noticeably better on fences.

You will spray it. A deck is a horizontal surface you can roll or pad in an afternoon. A fence is a long vertical surface full of gaps, rails and pickets, and brushing a 150-foot privacy fence on both sides is several weekends. Nearly everyone sprays, which makes a product's tolerance for overlaps, drips and uneven spray patterns the single most practical feature on the label.

Both sides. The neighbour's side weathers just as fast as yours, and staining only one face lets the bare side absorb and release moisture unevenly, which encourages cupping and warping. Plan materials and time for two faces, and talk to the neighbour before overspray reaches their car.

Ground contact at the posts. The part of a fence that actually fails is the bottom: the post at grade and the lower edge of boards that sit in mulch or soil. These stay damp and rot first. Stain cannot fix a post that is not rated for ground contact, but generously coating the bottom six inches, the end grain of pickets and the post faces near grade buys real years. Keep boards an inch or two clear of soil where you can.

Cedar, Pressure-Treated Pine and Redwood

Western Red Cedar

Cedar is the most common premium fence wood, and it is naturally rot- and insect-resistant thanks to oils and tannins in the heartwood. Those same tannins are the main thing to plan around: water-based stains can draw them to the surface as brownish streaks, called tannin bleed, particularly on new boards. Oil-based penetrating stains avoid the problem and soak readily into cedar's open, often rough-sawn surface. That is why most cedar fences are best served by an oil, and why our guide to stains for cedar leans the same way. Cedar is also soft, so pressure-wash it gently or not at all.

Pressure-Treated Pine

Most fences in North America are pressure-treated southern yellow pine, or spruce/pine pickets on treated posts and rails. Treated pine arrives saturated with water from the treatment process, and staining it before it dries is the most common reason a fence stain fails within a year — the stain simply cannot get in. Once dry, treated pine takes semi-transparent and solid stains well; it has little natural colour of its own, so most people pick a warmer tint such as a cedar tone. The green or brown tinge from the copper preservative shows through lighter stains, which is another argument for some pigment rather than a clear. The pressure-treated stain guide covers this in more depth.

Redwood

Redwood is the most dimensionally stable of the three and has a beautiful colour that greys quickly in sun if left bare. Like cedar it is tannin-rich, so oil-based translucent finishes are the natural fit, and an oxalic-acid brightener after cleaning restores much of the original colour before staining. Heavy pigment defeats the purpose of buying redwood; a translucent oil or a light semi-transparent shows it best. See our redwood sealer roundup for clear and lightly tinted options.

💡 Spruce pickets. Many budget privacy fences use untreated spruce or whitewood pickets on treated framing. Spruce has little natural rot resistance, so staining it promptly — once it is dry — matters more than on cedar. It also absorbs unevenly, so a semi-transparent or solid colour gives a more even result than a toner.

Transparency vs. Lifespan

The more pigment a stain carries, the longer it lasts. Pigment is what blocks UV, and UV is what breaks down both the wood surface and the binder holding the stain in it. The trade is how much of the wood you can see.

Clear products are the one category to avoid on a fence you care about. A clear exterior wood sealer keeps water out, but without pigment it does almost nothing to stop UV greying, so the wood turns silver underneath a perfectly intact water-repellent finish within a year or two. If you want a natural look, a lightly pigmented toner or a semi-transparent in a cedar or natural shade is the honest compromise.

Semi-transparent is the sweet spot for most fences. It shows the grain, it lasts long enough on vertical wood that recoating is a twice-a-decade job rather than an annual one, and it fails by fading rather than peeling — so the next coat needs only cleaning, not stripping.

Oil vs. Water-Based

Oil-based penetrating stains soak into the wood and leave little or no surface film. On fences they have three practical advantages: they are more forgiving when sprayed, because wet edges blend instead of lapping; they penetrate rough-sawn boards readily; and they weather by fading, so recoating is simple. The downsides are solvent odour, mineral-spirits cleanup, longer dry times and VOC rules — some formulas are not sold in California and other low-VOC states. Oil-soaked rags are also a genuine fire risk and should be laid flat to dry outdoors or stored in a sealed, water-filled metal can.

Water-based stains have improved substantially. They dry fast — often allowing both sides of a fence in one day — clean up with water and have little smell. The best use UV blockers that hold colour well in hard sun. Their weaknesses on fences are that they are less forgiving of uneven spraying, they can sit on the surface if over-applied or put on unprepared wood, and they can draw tannins out of new cedar and redwood.

Switching between them is where people get caught. Oil over a weathered oil stain is fine. Water-based over a recent oil stain usually is not, because it cannot bond to the oily surface; the old finish needs to weather off or be stripped first. Solid stain over semi-transparent is possible on clean, weathered wood; going the other way requires stripping. When in doubt, stay in the same family you used last time.

How Much Stain a Fence Needs

Fence quantity is the calculation most people get wrong, and it is almost always the same mistake: forgetting the second side. The formula is simple:

Linear feet × height × 2 sides = square feet of fence face. Then divide by the product's coverage on your wood and add 10–15% for posts, rails, end grain and waste.

Coverage depends heavily on the wood. Smooth, planed boards may take a stain at 200–250 square feet per gallon. Rough-sawn cedar and dry, weathered pine soak up far more — 100–150 square feet per gallon is realistic, and the first coat on very dry wood can be lower still. Spraying also uses more product than brushing, because some is lost to overspray and wind. When in doubt, plan around 150 square feet per gallon and buy one extra gallon; unopened stain is easy to return and running out halfway down a run leaves a visible change in colour.

Spraying vs. Rolling and Back-Brushing

Pump Sprayer

A one- or two-gallon garden pump sprayer is the default tool for penetrating oil stains on a fence, and it costs very little. Use one dedicated to stain (or a new one), fit an adjustable fan tip, and spray two or three boards at a time from top to bottom, overlapping the wet edge. Thin penetrating stains such as Ready Seal and TWP are made for this. It is slow compared with an airless, but there is almost no overspray, no setup and no cleaning of a pump between sessions.

Airless Sprayer

For more than about 150 feet of fence, an airless sprayer earns its rental fee — typically a half-day to day rental from a tool hire shop. It puts stain on very quickly and handles thicker semi-solid and solid stains that a pump sprayer struggles with. Use a small tip, roughly in the .011–.015 range for stains, and keep the gun moving at a steady distance. The costs are setup and cleanup time, a lot of overspray, and the need to mask anything within several feet: siding, cars, plants and the neighbour's patio furniture. Never spray in wind.

Rolling and Back-Brushing

Back-brushing means spraying (or rolling) the stain on and then immediately working it into the wood with a brush. It evens out heavy and light spots, pushes stain into rough grain and checks, and catches drips before they dry. On rough-sawn cedar and with water-based stains it noticeably improves penetration and evenness. A wide, thick-nap roller works on flat board-on-board fences, but on pickets and rails a brush is faster. Many people spray, then have a second person follow with a brush; it is the best compromise between speed and finish quality.

💡 Order of work. Do the posts, rails and bottom edges first, then the boards — so drips from the framing get covered rather than drying on finished faces. Work in shade or on an overcast day, top to bottom, and finish a whole section between posts before stopping so there is no lap mark mid-board.

Preparation

Stain choice matters less than preparation. A good stain over dirty, grey or wet wood will fail on schedule regardless of the brand.

Let new pressure-treated wood dry. Freshly treated lumber needs weeks to months to dry depending on climate and season. Use the sprinkle test: splash water on several boards. If it beads, the wood is still too wet or too new; if it soaks in within a few minutes, it is ready. Kiln-dried-after-treatment (KDAT) lumber can usually be stained much sooner. New cedar can generally be stained sooner too, but should still be cleaned to remove mill glaze.

Clean. An oxygen-bleach (sodium percarbonate) wood cleaner removes grey weathered fibres, dirt and mildew. Avoid chlorine bleach, which damages wood fibres and does not remove grey weathering effectively. Apply, let it dwell, scrub where needed and rinse.

Brighten. An oxalic-acid brightener after cleaning neutralises the cleaner, removes tannin stains and restores the wood's colour — especially dramatic on cedar and redwood. It also opens the surface so the stain penetrates evenly.

Pressure-wash carefully, if at all. A pressure washer speeds rinsing, but too much pressure furs soft cedar and pine, raising fibres that soak up stain unevenly. Use a wide fan tip, keep it moving, and stay low — roughly 500–1,200 psi for soft woods. A garden hose is enough for rinsing cleaner and brightener in most cases.

Let it dry, then watch the weather. Give cleaned wood 24–48 hours of dry weather before staining. Most stains want air and surface temperatures roughly between 50°F and 90°F, no rain for at least a day after, and no direct midday sun on the boards, which flashes off the stain before it penetrates.

Maintenance Cycles and Cost

The sign that a fence needs restaining is not a date but the wood: when water stops beading and soaks in, or when the sunny side has visibly faded toward grey, it is time. On most fences, the south- and west-facing side reaches that point a year or more before the other.

With a penetrating semi-transparent, maintenance is a light clean and a single fresh coat, often needing less product than the first time because the wood is already partly sealed. Doing it before the wood greys is far cheaper than after, because a fence that has gone fully grey needs heavier cleaning and brightening — and may need two coats to look even again.

The Short Version

For most fences, buy a penetrating oil-based semi-transparent stain such as Ready Seal and spray it with a pump sprayer, back-brushing rough spots. In strong sun or where solvent stains are restricted, use a good water-based stain like Defy Extreme and prep thoroughly. On a damp, shady fence line, a mildew-resistant preservative stain like TWP is the better fit. Show off cedar or redwood with a translucent oil, and hide an old grey fence under a solid colour.

Whatever you buy, calculate linear feet × height × 2, let new pressure-treated wood dry, clean and brighten before staining, and plan on recoating every three to five years. If you are also doing a deck, our deck stain guide covers what changes when the boards are horizontal.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best stain for a wood fence?

For most fences, a penetrating oil-based semi-transparent stain. It shows the grain, lasts three to five years on vertical boards, fails by fading rather than peeling, and is the most forgiving type to spray over a long run. Ready Seal in Natural Cedar is the easiest to get a good result with. In very sunny climates or low-VOC states, a quality water-based semi-transparent such as Defy Extreme is a strong alternative. On an old, grey or patched fence that you want to look uniform, a solid colour stain is the better choice and lasts longest of all.

How long does fence stain last?

Longer than on a deck, because vertical boards shed water and take no foot traffic. Typical fence lifespans are one to two years for clear sealers, two to three for translucent toners, three to five for semi-transparent stains and five to eight or more for solid colour stains. The sunny south and west faces wear fastest — often a year or more ahead of the shaded side. Recoat when water stops beading and soaks into the wood, rather than on a fixed schedule, and before the wood turns grey, which keeps the recoat to a light clean and one coat.

How much stain do I need for a fence?

Multiply linear feet by height by two sides, divide by the stain's coverage, and add 10–15%. A 150-foot run of 6-foot privacy fence is 900 square feet per side, or 1,800 square feet in total. Smooth boards may cover 200–250 square feet per gallon, but rough-sawn cedar and dry weathered pine are closer to 100–150, so that fence needs roughly 12–14 gallons at a typical 150 square feet per gallon. Shadowbox and board-on-board styles need 15–20% more again. Buy the whole job at once to avoid colour variation between batches.

Is it better to spray or brush fence stain?

Spray, then back-brush where needed. Brushing an entire privacy fence on both sides is several weekends of work; a pump sprayer handles thin penetrating stains well with little overspray, and an airless sprayer is worth renting beyond about 150 linear feet or for solid stains. Back-brushing — working the stain in with a brush right after spraying — evens out heavy spots, pushes stain into rough grain and catches drips, and noticeably improves results on rough-sawn cedar and with water-based stains. Spray in calm weather and mask anything nearby.

How long should I wait to stain a new pressure-treated fence?

Until the wood is dry enough to absorb stain, which can take anywhere from a few weeks to several months depending on climate, season and how wet the lumber was when installed. Use the sprinkle test: splash water on several boards, and if it beads up, wait; if it soaks in within a few minutes, the wood is ready. Kiln-dried-after-treatment lumber can usually be stained much sooner. Staining wet treated pine is the most common reason fence stain fails in its first year, because the stain cannot penetrate wood already full of water.

Should I stain both sides of my fence?

Yes, if at all possible. The unstained side weathers, greys and absorbs and releases moisture differently from the stained side, which encourages cupping, warping and checking in the boards. It also shortens the life of the fence overall, since rot and mildew work on bare wood regardless of which side faces your yard. If the neighbour's side is on their property, ask before going over, and agree on colour if they will see it. Many neighbours are happy to split the cost of materials.

Is oil-based or water-based stain better for a fence?

Oil-based is better for most fences, especially cedar, redwood and rough-sawn boards. It penetrates readily, sprays without lap marks and fades gracefully, so recoating is simple. Water-based stains dry faster, have little odour, clean up with water and hold colour well in strong sun when they use good UV blockers — but they need thorough preparation, are less forgiving of uneven spraying and can draw tannins out of new cedar. Avoid putting water-based over a recent oil-based stain; it will not bond until the old finish weathers off or is stripped.

How much does it cost to stain a fence?

For a 150-foot, 6-foot privacy fence stained on both sides — about 1,800 square feet — expect roughly $400–$1,000 doing it yourself, covering stain, cleaner and brightener, a sprayer and sundries. Professional staining of the same fence typically runs about $1,800–$4,500, or roughly $1 to $2.50 per square foot stained including preparation. Mainstream semi-transparent stain costs about $25–$45 a gallon and premium penetrating stains about $40–$70, with five-gallon pails cheaper per gallon. Recoats cost less than the first job because preparation is lighter and the wood absorbs less.
