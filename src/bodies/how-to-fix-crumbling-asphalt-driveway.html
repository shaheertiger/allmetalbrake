How to Fix a Crumbling Asphalt Driveway

Ravelling, cracking, alligatoring and potholes are four different failures needing four different repairs. Diagnose first — sealcoating a base failure wastes the money and the weekend.

A crumbling asphalt driveway is asphalt that has lost its binder. The stone aggregate is still there — that is the loose gravel you sweep up — but the black petroleum binder that held it together has oxidised and washed away, so the surface is coming apart grain by grain. The trade term is ravelling, and understanding it is what tells you whether your driveway needs a $30 bag of patch or a $7,000 replacement.

The good news is that most crumbling driveways are repairable, and the repair is well within DIY range. The bad news is that a driveway crumbling because its base has failed cannot be fixed from the top, and doing so wastes both money and a weekend.

⚡ Quick Answer: Surface ravelling — loose stone, a rough sandy texture, no depressions — is repairable: clean, patch any holes, and sealcoat. Alligator cracking (interconnected cracks in a pattern like reptile skin) means the base has failed under that area, and it needs a full-depth patch, not a surface repair. Widespread alligatoring or depressions that hold water means the driveway needs replacing or resurfacing. Diagnose before you buy anything.

First: Diagnose What You Actually Have

Four distinct failures get described as "crumbling," and they need completely different responses. Walk the driveway and identify which you have — this is the most valuable ten minutes of the whole project.

1. Ravelling (Surface Only)

The surface feels rough and sandy, loose stone collects at the edges and in the gutter, and the colour has gone from black to grey. Press a screwdriver into it and it stops within a few millimetres — the asphalt underneath is still solid.

Cause: UV oxidation and water washing the binder out of the top layer, usually because the driveway has never been sealed or has not been sealed in a decade.

Fix: Clean and sealcoat. This is the easy case and by far the most common.

2. Cracking (Linear)

Individual cracks running along or across the driveway, or in irregular lines. They may be hairline or up to an inch wide.

Cause: Thermal expansion and contraction, edge settlement, tree roots, or shrinkage. Normal ageing.

Fix: Crack filler, then sealcoat. Urgent, because an open crack lets water into the base and that is how a surface problem becomes a structural one.

3. Alligator Cracking (Base Failure)

Interconnected cracks forming a pattern of small polygons, like reptile skin or dried mud. Often accompanied by a soft or springy feel underfoot, and frequently in the wheel tracks.

Cause: The base beneath has failed — either it was never thick enough or well enough compacted, or water has got in and undermined it. The asphalt is flexing under load because it has nothing solid to bear on.

Fix: Full-depth patch. Cut out the failed area, excavate and replace the base, and repave that section. Sealcoating alligator cracking is money thrown away — the surface will re-crack in the same pattern within one season, because the cause is underneath.

4. Potholes and Depressions

Holes with defined edges, or dish-shaped areas that hold water after rain.

Cause: Alligator cracking that has progressed, or base settlement.

Fix: Full-depth patch, and if the depression is because the base settled, fix the drainage that caused it or it will settle again.

🔍 The Screwdriver Test: Push a long screwdriver into the affected area with firm hand pressure. If it stops in the first few millimetres, the problem is surface-only and a surface repair will work. If it sinks an inch or more, or the asphalt flexes as you press, the base has failed and no amount of surface treatment will help.

This is worth understanding because it explains most premature failures, and because it tells you what to specify if you end up replacing.

That last row is the reason so many driveways alligator within five years. Two inches of asphalt over four inches of base flexes under a vehicle, and repeated flexing fatigues the asphalt until it cracks. It is not a materials failure; it is a specification failure, and it is invisible in a quote unless you ask for depths in writing.

New asphalt driveway problems that show up in the first two or three years — alligatoring in the wheel tracks, edges crumbling away, depressions where vehicles park — are nearly always thin base, poor compaction, or inadequate edge support rather than bad asphalt.

How to Fix a Crumbling (Ravelled) Driveway

This is the repair most people need. Budget a weekend and roughly $150–$400 in materials for a typical two-car driveway.

Step 1 — Clear Everything Loose

Sweep hard with a stiff broom, then blow the surface with a leaf blower. Every loose stone left in place is a stone the sealer will bond to instead of the driveway. On badly ravelled surfaces this takes longer than you expect — expect to fill several buckets.

Step 2 — Kill and Remove Vegetation

Pull weeds from cracks, and treat the roots. Anything left will grow back through the repair within a season.

Step 3 — Degrease Oil Spots

Sealer will not bond to oil, and untreated oil spots become the first places the new sealcoat fails. Use a dedicated asphalt degreaser or an oil-spot primer, scrub, and rinse. If the oil has soaked deep enough to soften the asphalt, that area needs cutting out and patching instead.

Step 4 — Wash

Pressure wash at a moderate setting with a fan tip, or scrub with a driveway cleaner. Do not use a turbo tip on ravelled asphalt — you will strip more aggregate loose than you clean.

Step 5 — Fill Cracks

Cracks up to about half an inch take a pourable or squeeze-bottle rubberised crack filler. Cracks wider than that take a cold-patch or an asphalt repair caulk with backer rod underneath. Clean the crack out first with a screwdriver and the blower — filler over debris does not adhere.

Let crack filler cure for the time the label states, usually 24 hours, before sealcoating over it.

Step 6 — Patch Any Holes

See the section below. Patch before sealing, and let patches cure.

Step 7 — Sealcoat

Two thin coats beat one thick one — thick sealer cures slowly, stays soft, and tracks into the house on shoes and tyres. Apply with a squeegee-brush applicator, working in sections, and cut in the edges with a brush first.

Conditions matter: 50–85°F and rising, no rain forecast for 24 hours, and not in blazing direct sun, which skins the surface before the sealer bonds. Keep off for 24 hours and keep vehicles off for 48.

⚠️ On Badly Ravelled Asphalt: If the surface has lost so much binder that it is essentially loose aggregate, sealcoat will not save it — the sealer bonds to loose stone that then comes away. In that case the answer is a resurfacing overlay: a fresh 1.5–2 inch lift of asphalt over the existing surface, which costs roughly half a full replacement and reuses the base.

How to Patch Asphalt — Potholes and Full-Depth Repairs

Cold Patch (Small Holes, Temporary to Semi-Permanent)

Cold patch asphalt comes in a bag, needs no heating, and works down to fairly low temperatures. It is the right product for a pothole up to about a foot across where the base underneath is still sound.

  1. Square up the hole with a masonry chisel or a circular saw with a diamond blade — vertical edges, not tapered. Patch material cannot key into a sloping edge.
  2. Remove all loose material and any standing water.
  3. Fill in lifts no more than 2 inches deep, tamping each one hard with a hand tamper or the end of a 4x4.
  4. Overfill by about a quarter of the depth, because it will compact down.
  5. Compact the final lift thoroughly — drive over it slowly with a car several times if you have nothing better, laying a board over it first.

Cold patch is often described as temporary. Done properly, with squared edges and real compaction, it lasts years. Done badly — shovelled into a round hole and tamped with a boot — it lasts weeks.

Full-Depth Patch (Alligator Cracking and Base Failure)

This is the repair that actually fixes a failed area, and it is more work.

  1. Cut out the failed area in a rectangle, extending 12 inches beyond the visible damage in every direction — the base has failed further than the surface shows.
  2. Excavate the asphalt and the failed base material down to firm subgrade.
  3. Compact the subgrade.
  4. Replace with 6–8 inches of crushed aggregate base in 2–3 inch lifts, compacting each one.
  5. Fill with hot mix or cold patch asphalt in 2 inch lifts, compacting each.
  6. Seal the joint between old and new asphalt with crack filler.

For anything over a few square yards, hiring this out is reasonable — the compaction is what makes it work, and a plate compactor or a jumping jack does it far better than hand tools.

Crumbling Concrete Is a Different Problem

Concrete does not ravel; it spalls and scales. The surface flakes off in sheets or chips, exposing the aggregate underneath. The causes are different and so are the fixes.

Cause: De-icing salt driving chloride into the surface, freeze-thaw cycling on a slab that was not air-entrained, or a slab that was over-watered or over-troweled when placed, leaving a weak surface layer.

Fix for light scaling: A concrete resurfacer — a polymer-modified cementitious overlay applied 1/8 to 1/4 inch thick over a clean, sound slab. It bonds well and restores a uniform surface, and it is a genuinely good DIY product.

Fix for deep spalling: Cut out the damaged area to sound concrete, key the edges, and patch with a polymer-modified repair mortar. Match the finish as closely as you can, and accept that it will not match the colour.

Prevention, which matters more: Seal the concrete with a penetrating silane/siloxane, and stop using chloride de-icers. Sand gives traction and does no damage.

The economics are worth stating plainly. Sealcoating a 600 sq ft driveway costs $90–$180 in materials. A resurfacing overlay costs roughly $2,500–$4,500. A full replacement costs $4,200–$7,800. Spending $150 on sealer for a driveway that needs replacing wastes $150 and a weekend; spending $6,000 replacing one that needed sealing wastes considerably more.

Preventing It Next Time

Sealcoat every three to five years. Not annually — over-sealing builds a thick, brittle layer that cracks on its own. Every three to five years, once the surface has greyed but before it starts shedding stone.

Fill cracks as soon as they appear. An open crack is a funnel delivering water into the base. Crack filler is cheap and this is the single highest-value maintenance action there is.

Keep water off and away. Make sure the driveway sheds water, that downspouts do not discharge onto it, and that the edges are supported with soil or gravel rather than eroding away.

Support the edges. Unsupported asphalt edges crumble under a wheel. Build up soil or gravel flush with the edge.

Do not park in the same spot in high heat if you can avoid it, particularly with trailer jacks and motorcycle kickstands. Use a plate under any point load.

Wait before sealing new asphalt. New asphalt needs 90 days to cure and off-gas before its first sealcoat. Sealing sooner traps volatiles and produces a soft surface.

The Bottom Line

Most crumbling asphalt driveways are ravelled surfaces on sound bases, and the fix is a weekend of cleaning, crack filling, and sealcoating for a couple of hundred dollars. That is the common case and it is worth doing.

The failure to avoid is treating a base problem as a surface problem. If the driveway alligators, feels springy underfoot, or has depressions that hold water, sealer will hide it for one season and then it will look exactly the same. Do the screwdriver test, look for the alligator pattern, and if you find it, patch full-depth or plan for replacement.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you fix a crumbling asphalt driveway?

First identify whether the problem is the surface or the base. Crumbling that is loose stone and a rough, sandy grey texture with no depressions is ravelling — the binder has oxidised out of the top layer — and the fix is to sweep and blow the surface completely clean, kill weeds, degrease oil spots, wash, fill cracks with rubberised filler, patch any holes, and apply two thin coats of sealcoat. Budget a weekend and $150–$400 for a two-car driveway. But if you see interconnected cracks in a pattern like reptile skin, or the surface feels springy underfoot, the base has failed and no surface treatment will work — that needs a full-depth patch. Test it by pushing a screwdriver in: if it sinks an inch or more, the problem is underneath.

How thick should an asphalt driveway be?

For passenger cars on good soil, 3 inches of compacted asphalt over 6 inches of compacted aggregate base — 9 inches total. In deep-frost regions or on clay, go to 4 inches of asphalt over 8 inches of base. For trucks, an RV, or a boat trailer, 4 inches of asphalt over 8–10 inches of base. A typical low-bid residential job is 1.5–2 inches of asphalt over 4 inches of base, and that is why so many driveways develop alligator cracking within five years: the surface flexes under vehicle load because there is not enough structure beneath it, and repeated flexing fatigues the asphalt until it cracks. Ask any contractor to state base depth, number of compaction lifts, and finished asphalt thickness in writing.

What causes alligator cracking and can sealcoating fix it?

Alligator cracking — interconnected cracks forming small polygons like reptile skin — means the base beneath has failed. Either it was never thick enough or well enough compacted, or water has got in and undermined it, and the asphalt is now flexing under load because it has nothing solid to bear on. Sealcoating it is money thrown away: the sealer covers the pattern for one season and it reappears identically, because the cause is entirely underneath the surface you treated. The correct repair is a full-depth patch — cut out the failed area extending 12 inches beyond the visible damage, excavate to firm subgrade, rebuild 6–8 inches of compacted base in lifts, then repave in 2-inch lifts. If alligatoring covers more than about 25% of the driveway, replacement is the economic choice.

How do you patch a pothole in asphalt?

Square up the hole first with a masonry chisel or a circular saw with a diamond blade, cutting vertical edges rather than leaving them tapered — patch material cannot key into a sloping edge, and this is the step most people skip and most repairs fail on. Remove all loose material and any standing water. Fill with cold patch asphalt in lifts no deeper than 2 inches, tamping each one hard with a hand tamper or the end of a 4x4. Overfill the final lift by about a quarter of the depth because it will compact down, then compact thoroughly — laying a board over it and driving across slowly several times works if you have nothing better. Cold patch is often called temporary, but done with squared edges and real compaction it lasts years.

What is the difference between crumbling asphalt and crumbling concrete?

Different materials failing in different ways. Asphalt ravels: the petroleum binder oxidises and washes out, releasing the stone aggregate grain by grain, leaving a rough sandy surface and loose gravel. The fix is cleaning and sealcoating, or a resurfacing overlay if it has gone too far. Concrete spalls and scales: the surface flakes off in sheets or chips, exposing aggregate. That is usually caused by de-icing salt driving chloride into the surface, freeze-thaw cycling on a slab that was not air-entrained, or a slab that was over-watered or over-troweled when placed. Light scaling is fixed with a polymer-modified concrete resurfacer applied 1/8 to 1/4 inch thick; deeper spalling needs cutting out and patching with repair mortar.

When should you replace an asphalt driveway instead of repairing it?

Replace when alligator cracking covers more than about 25% of the area, when there are depressions that hold water after rain (which means base settlement), or when the driveway is past twenty years old with widespread failure. Resurface — a fresh 1.5–2 inch overlay reusing the existing base, at roughly half the cost of replacement — when the surface is worn or badly ravelled but the base is still sound and the driveway drains properly. Repair when the problem is confined to surface ravelling, linear cracks, or isolated potholes over a sound base. The economics matter: sealcoating a 600 sq ft driveway costs $90–$180 in materials, an overlay $2,500–$4,500, and a full replacement $4,200–$7,800.

Why is my new asphalt driveway already crumbling?

Almost always a specification or workmanship problem rather than bad asphalt. The most common cause is insufficient depth — 1.5 to 2 inches of asphalt over 4 inches of base is a common low-bid specification, and it flexes under vehicle load until the asphalt fatigues and alligators, usually in the wheel tracks first. Poor compaction is the second cause: base dumped to full depth and compacted once leaves the lower half loose, and it consolidates later under traffic. Unsupported edges are the third — asphalt edges with no soil or gravel built up flush to them crumble under a wheel. Sealing too soon is a fourth: new asphalt needs 90 days to cure and off-gas, and sealing earlier traps volatiles and leaves a soft surface.

How often should you sealcoat an asphalt driveway?

Every three to five years, not annually. Sealcoat protects the binder from UV oxidation and from fuel and oil, both of which dissolve it, and skipping it entirely is what turns a 20-year driveway into a 12-year one. But over-sealing is its own problem: applying sealer every year builds a thick layer that becomes brittle and cracks independently of the asphalt beneath it. The right timing is once the surface has greyed but before it starts shedding aggregate. Two thin coats beat one thick one — heavy sealer cures slowly, stays soft, and tracks into the house. Apply at 50–85°F with no rain forecast for 24 hours, and wait 90 days after new asphalt is laid before the first application.
