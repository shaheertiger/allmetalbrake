Do Pavers Need to Be Sealed? An Honest Answer by Material and Location

Pavers are built to survive outdoors without any coating at all. Sealing is an upgrade, not a requirement — worth it in some places, a waste of money in others, and actively harmful if done at the wrong time.

No — pavers do not strictly need to be sealed. Concrete and clay pavers are manufactured to be far denser and stronger than a poured slab, and an unsealed paver patio or driveway installed on a proper base will last decades. Plenty of commercial plazas and municipal sidewalks have never seen a drop of sealer. What sealing does is make pavers easier to live with: it slows stains from oil, grease, food and rust, deepens or preserves color, locks joint sand in place, and reduces the water and salt that cause surface wear in freeze-thaw climates.

So the useful question is not whether pavers need sealing but whether your pavers, in your location, gain enough from it to justify the cost and the recurring upkeep. For a driveway that collects oil drips, a grill patio, a salt-water pool deck or a stone that stains easily, the answer is usually yes. For a clay brick walkway or a lightly used concrete patio you are happy with as it is, it is usually no.

⚡ The direct answer: Sealing pavers is optional. Seal when you want stain resistance (driveways, grills, outdoor kitchens), color enhancement or a wet look, joint sand stabilization (washout, weeds, ants), or protection from deicing salt and freeze-thaw. Skip it on clay brick pavers, low-use patios you like the look of, and any surface that is still showing efflorescence or sits on a damp base. If you do seal, expect to repeat it every 2–5 years depending on the product.

Why Unsealed Pavers Hold Up Fine

The case for sealing is often made as though bare pavers were fragile. They are not, and understanding why helps put the decision in proportion.

Concrete pavers are dense by design. Interlocking concrete pavers made to the ASTM C936 standard must reach an average compressive strength of around 8,000 psi and absorb no more than about 5% of their weight in water. A typical poured driveway slab is closer to 3,000–4,000 psi and considerably more porous. Pavers are made from a stiff, low-water mix compacted under vibration and pressure, which is exactly what gives them their strength and their resistance to freeze-thaw damage. The standard's freeze-thaw durability requirements apply to the paver as made, with no sealer, because that is how pavers are expected to perform.

Clay pavers are denser still. Fired clay brick pavers get their color from the clay and the firing process rather than from added pigment, so the color goes all the way through and does not fade in sunlight. Their surface is hard and low-absorption, and the brick industry's general guidance is that clay pavers do not need a sealer at all.

The system is flexible. A segmental paver surface is not a single rigid slab. Each unit can move slightly with the base, and if one cracks or stains badly, you lift it and set a new one. That repairability is one of the main reasons people choose pavers, and it reduces the stakes of leaving them unprotected: a ruined paver is a ten-minute fix, not a resurfacing job.

What actually happens to unsealed pavers over time is mostly cosmetic. Concrete pavers lose a little of their surface cement paste to weather and traffic, which exposes the sand and aggregate underneath and makes the color look lighter and flatter — often described as fading, though the pigment itself is stable. They pick up stains where things get spilled. Joint sand washes out and gets replaced by organic debris that weeds root in. None of these threaten the structure, but all of them are things a sealer can slow.

Good Reasons to Seal Anyway

1. Stain Resistance — the Strongest Case

Concrete pavers are porous enough to wick in oil, grease and pigmented liquids within minutes. Motor oil from a leaking car, grease spatter around a grill, red wine, berries, leaf tannins, and rust from patio furniture feet or fertilizer granules all leave marks that can be very difficult to remove completely from bare concrete. A good sealer does not make pavers stain-proof, but it slows absorption long enough that a spill wiped up within an hour or so usually leaves nothing behind.

This is the main reason sealing makes sense on driveways and outdoor kitchen areas. If oil drips are already a problem, clean them before sealing, because a sealer will lock an existing stain in just as effectively as it keeps new ones out.

2. Color Enhancement

Film-forming sealers darken and saturate paver color, ranging from a subtle "damp" look to a high-gloss wet look. If your concrete pavers have weathered pale and chalky, an enhancing sealer brings much of the original richness back. Penetrating sealers, by contrast, are chosen precisely because they do not change the look; our guide to the best paver sealers for a natural look covers that side of the range. This benefit is purely aesthetic, but for many homeowners it is the reason they seal at all.

3. Joint Sand Stabilization

Ordinary joint sand washes out under heavy rain, downspout discharge, pressure washing and sweeping. As it goes, pavers lose some of their interlock, weeds and moss establish in the gaps, and ants excavate the joints. Joint-stabilizing sealers are designed to soak down into the sand and bind the top portion of it together, so it stays put. This is an alternative to, or a complement to, polymeric sand — our polymeric sand pros and cons breakdown explains the trade-offs between the two approaches.

4. Salt and Freeze-Thaw Areas

Pavers are freeze-thaw resistant, but not immune. In cold climates where deicing salt is used, the combination of saturated pavers, repeated freezing and salt-laden water can cause surface scaling — small flakes of the top layer popping off over several winters. Salt also leaves white residue and accelerates the loss of surface paste. A penetrating silane or siloxane sealer reduces how much water and dissolved salt the paver absorbs without forming a surface film, which is why it is the usual recommendation for driveways and walkways in snow country. The chemistry is explained in our silane vs siloxane sealer comparison.

5. Easier Cleaning and Less Organic Growth

A sealed surface sheds dirt more readily and dries faster after rain, which gives moss, algae and mildew less opportunity on shaded patios. It does not eliminate them, and it will not stop weeds that germinate in the joints from seeds blown in on top, but it does reduce how often a shaded area needs scrubbing.

The Downsides Nobody Mentions

Sealer marketing rarely dwells on these, but each one is a real reason some people decide not to seal — and a reason to choose the type carefully if you do.

  • It is a recurring commitment. Film-forming acrylic sealers typically need recoating every 2–3 years, and penetrating sealers every 3–5 or more. Once a surface has been sealed with a film, letting it lapse produces a patchy, half-worn look that is worse than never sealing. Our guide to how often to seal pavers covers realistic intervals by product type and exposure.
  • Cost adds up over time. DIY sealing usually runs a few hundred dollars for a typical patio once cleaning products and sealer are included; professional cleaning, re-sanding and sealing costs considerably more. Multiplied by a recoat every few years, it becomes a real line item. See paver sealing cost for current DIY and professional figures.
  • Slipperiness. Glossy film sealers reduce surface texture and can be noticeably slick when wet, which matters on steps, slopes and especially pool decks. Anti-slip grit additives help, but they slightly dull the finish and need to be mixed in consistently.
  • Haze and blushing. Solvent-based acrylics in particular can turn milky white if applied over a damp surface, in high humidity, or too thickly. Fixing it means re-dissolving the film with solvent or xylene, or stripping it — neither is pleasant.
  • Trapped moisture. A film that is too heavy or not breathable enough can trap water vapor rising from the base, causing white clouding under the film and, in the worst cases, peeling. This is why breathable water-based and penetrating products are preferred on pavers over sand-set bases.
  • It changes the look permanently-ish. A wet-look sealer can only be removed with chemical strippers and a lot of rinsing. If you later decide you prefer the natural matte finish, getting back to it is a significant job.
  • A bad application is worse than none. Streaks, lap marks, puddled gloss in low spots and sealer pooled on joint sand are all common DIY outcomes, and all are more noticeable than ordinary weathering.
💡 The commitment test. Before sealing for the first time, ask whether you are prepared to do it again in three years, and again three years after that. If the honest answer is no, a penetrating sealer — which fades invisibly rather than peeling — or no sealer at all is the better choice.

Concrete Pavers

The paver type where sealing is most genuinely optional and most genuinely useful at the same time. Concrete pavers survive unsealed for decades, but they stain more readily than clay or dense stone, and their surface weathers to expose aggregate over the years. If you care about keeping the original color and keeping oil and grease out, sealing pays off. If you are relaxed about a weathered patina, it is not needed.

Most concrete paver manufacturers now point owners toward breathable, water-based sealers. Our roundup of the best water-based paver sealers covers products in that category, which suit most residential patios and driveways and are easier for a DIYer to apply cleanly than solvent-based acrylics.

Clay Brick Pavers

Clay pavers are the clearest case for not sealing. Their color is permanent, they absorb little, and their dense fired surface gives a film-forming sealer very little to grip — which is how sealed clay pavers end up with peeling, cloudy or blotchy coatings. Brick industry guidance generally advises against coating clay pavers unless there is a specific problem to solve. The exceptions are an outdoor kitchen or grill area where grease is a concern, and heavily trafficked joints that keep losing sand; in those cases a penetrating or joint-stabilizing product is the safer choice than a gloss film.

Natural Stone Pavers

Stone is where generalizations break down, because "natural stone" covers everything from near-impervious granite to sandstone that soaks up water like a sponge. Soft, porous sedimentary stones — limestone, sandstone and travertine — benefit most from a penetrating impregnating sealer, which reduces staining and organic growth without changing the surface texture or making it slippery. Dense stones such as granite rarely need anything outdoors. A simple test tells you which you have: sprinkle water on a dry paver. If it darkens within a minute or two, the stone is absorbent enough that a penetrating sealer will help; if the water beads and sits for ten minutes, sealing will achieve little.

With stone, avoid high-gloss acrylic films unless the stone manufacturer specifically recommends them. They tend to look artificial on natural material, are slippery, and can trap moisture in stone that needs to breathe.

Driveways vs Patios vs Pool Decks

Where the pavers are installed changes the case for sealing almost as much as what they are made of.

Driveways

The strongest case for sealing concrete pavers. Driveways collect motor oil, transmission fluid, brake dust, tire marks and, in cold climates, deicing salt — exactly the things a sealer is good at holding off. Vehicle traffic also grinds dirt across the surface, accelerating wear on unsealed concrete pavers. The usual approach is a penetrating silane/siloxane sealer in freeze-thaw regions, or a durable water-based film sealer in milder climates where color enhancement is wanted. Avoid thick high-gloss coatings, which show tire marks and scuffing and can become slippery on a sloped driveway.

Patios

Patios are where the decision is most personal. A patio used mainly for sitting, with no grill and little shade, may never need sealing. A patio with an outdoor kitchen, a dining table, potted plants that leak rust-colored runoff, or overhanging trees that drop berries and leaves is a different story. If spills and tannin stains are frequent, a sealer saves real cleaning effort. Patios are also where people most often seal purely for appearance, and that is a perfectly legitimate reason — as long as you account for the recoating.

Pool Decks

Pool decks combine constant wetting, splashed pool chemicals, sunscreen, and — on salt-water pools — salt crystallization in the paver surface. Sealing is generally worth it here, but the product choice matters more than anywhere else, because slip resistance is a safety issue rather than a preference. Choose a breathable water-based or penetrating product with low gloss, and add anti-slip grit if the product is a film. Salt-water pool owners in particular benefit from a penetrating sealer that reduces salt absorption. Our guide to the best paver sealers for pool decks focuses on that trade-off between protection and grip.

Walkways and Garden Paths

Low traffic, little exposure to oil or food, and usually not worth sealing unless they carry deicing salt in winter or the joints keep washing out. A joint-stabilizing sealer can make sense on a sloped path where heavy rain washes sand downhill.

When Not to Seal

Sealing at the wrong time causes most of the problems people blame on sealers themselves. Do not seal in these situations.

New Pavers Still Showing Efflorescence

Efflorescence is the white, powdery haze that appears on many new concrete pavers. It is caused by calcium compounds in the cement migrating to the surface with moisture and reacting with carbon dioxide. It is harmless and usually works itself out over the first months to a year as the pavers weather — but if you seal over it, the haze gets trapped under the film and becomes far harder to remove. Many paver manufacturers recommend waiting at least 60–90 days after installation, and longer if efflorescence is still visible. When you do seal, clean the surface with an efflorescence remover first if any white bloom remains.

Damp Pavers or a Wet Base

Sealer needs a dry surface and, for film-forming products, reasonably dry pavers through their thickness. Applying over damp pavers is the leading cause of blushing (white clouding) in acrylic sealers. As a rule of thumb, allow at least 24–48 hours of dry weather after rain, washing or cleaning, longer in cool or humid conditions and in shaded areas. If the base has a chronic drainage problem — standing water, pavers that stay dark long after rain, persistent moss — fix the drainage first. Sealing a constantly wet installation traps moisture and almost guarantees a failed coating.

Fresh Polymeric Sand

Polymeric sand needs time to cure fully before it is sealed over, and some products specify a waiting period. Check the bag; sealing too soon can cause the joints to haze or the sand to fail to harden properly.

Wrong Weather

Most paver sealers need surface and air temperatures roughly between 50°F and 90°F, no rain forecast for at least 24 hours, and no direct midday sun beating on a hot surface, which can cause bubbling and lap marks. Product labels vary, so follow the one on the can.

Over an Existing, Incompatible Sealer

Applying a water-based product over a solvent-based acrylic, or vice versa, without checking compatibility can cause the new coat to fail to bond or the old one to cloud. If you do not know what was used before, test a small area or strip it first.

Stained or Dirty Pavers

Sealing locks in whatever is on the surface. Oil spots, rust marks, algae and ground-in dirt should all be cleaned out first, and the joints re-sanded where needed. Our step-by-step guide to how to seal pavers covers the cleaning and preparation sequence in detail.

The Short Version

Pavers do not need to be sealed to last. Concrete and clay pavers are made to perform unsealed for decades, and the structure of a well-built paver surface does not depend on any coating.

Seal concrete pavers where stains, salt or appearance matter — driveways, grill areas, pool decks and patios you want to keep looking new. Choose a breathable water-based or penetrating sealer unless you specifically want the wet look and accept the upkeep that comes with it.

Leave clay brick pavers and dense stone bare unless there is a specific problem to solve, and treat porous stone such as limestone, sandstone and travertine with a penetrating impregnator.

Never seal over efflorescence, damp pavers or dirt. Most sealer failures come from the timing and preparation, not the product. And before sealing for the first time, make sure you are willing to do it again in a few years — because a lapsed film sealer looks worse than pavers that were never sealed at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do pavers need to be sealed?

No. Concrete pavers made to ASTM C936 reach roughly 8,000 psi and absorb no more than about 5% of their weight in water, which makes them far denser and more durable than a poured slab, and clay pavers are denser still with color that does not fade. Both last decades unsealed. Sealing is an optional upgrade that improves stain resistance against oil, grease, food and rust, enhances color, stabilizes joint sand, and reduces water and salt absorption in freeze-thaw climates. It is most worthwhile on concrete paver driveways, grill and outdoor kitchen areas, pool decks and porous natural stone, and least worthwhile on clay brick and low-use patios.

What happens if you don't seal pavers?

Structurally, nothing — the pavers will keep doing their job. Cosmetically, unsealed concrete pavers gradually lose some surface cement paste, which exposes aggregate and makes them look lighter and flatter. They stain more readily from oil, grease, tannins and rust, and the joint sand washes out faster, letting weeds and ants move in. In cold climates with deicing salt, unsealed concrete pavers may show some surface scaling over many winters. All of these are slow, manageable changes, and individual damaged pavers can be lifted and replaced.

How long should you wait to seal new pavers?

Many manufacturers recommend waiting at least 60–90 days after installation, and longer if white efflorescence is still appearing on the surface. Efflorescence is a natural process in new concrete pavers that usually works itself out over the first months to a year, and sealing over it traps the white haze under the film. If you used polymeric sand, also check its required curing period before sealing. When you are ready, clean the surface thoroughly — with an efflorescence remover if any bloom remains — and let it dry for at least 24–48 hours before applying sealer.

Does sealing pavers make them slippery?

It can. Glossy film-forming sealers, especially solvent-based wet-look acrylics, smooth the surface texture and can be noticeably slick when wet. Penetrating silane and siloxane sealers do not form a film and have little effect on traction. If you want a film sealer on steps, slopes or a pool deck, choose a low-gloss product and mix in an anti-slip grit additive, applied consistently across the whole area. On pool decks, slip resistance should take priority over appearance.

Should clay brick pavers be sealed?

Usually not. Clay pavers get their color from the clay and firing process, so it goes all the way through and does not fade, and their dense fired surface absorbs very little water. Brick industry guidance generally advises against coating them unless there is a particular problem. Film-forming sealers often bond poorly to clay and can peel or cloud. The exceptions are areas exposed to grease, such as around a grill, and joints that keep washing out — in those cases a penetrating or joint-stabilizing product is a safer choice than a gloss coating.

Is it worth sealing a paver driveway?

For concrete paver driveways, usually yes. Driveways are exposed to the things sealers are best at holding off: motor oil and other vehicle fluids, tire marks, and deicing salt in cold climates. A penetrating silane or siloxane sealer is the low-maintenance choice where salt and freeze-thaw are the main concern, while a durable water-based film suits milder climates where color enhancement is wanted. Clean existing oil stains before sealing, because the sealer will lock them in. Avoid thick high-gloss coatings on driveways, which show scuffing and tire marks.

How often do pavers need to be resealed?

Film-forming acrylic sealers typically need a fresh coat every 2–3 years, polyurethane-based sealers every 3–5 years, and penetrating silane/siloxane sealers every 3–5 years or more. Traffic, sun exposure, pool chemicals and snow removal all shorten those intervals. A simple test is to sprinkle water on the pavers: if it soaks in and darkens the surface quickly rather than beading, the protection has worn and it is time to reseal. Driveway traffic lanes and pool surrounds usually wear first.

Can sealing pavers trap moisture?

Yes, if the wrong product is used or it is applied at the wrong time. A heavy or non-breathable film over damp pavers, or over a base with poor drainage, can trap water vapor and cause white clouding (blushing) under the film or eventual peeling. Using breathable water-based or penetrating sealers, applying thin coats, waiting for at least 24–48 hours of dry weather, and fixing drainage problems before sealing all prevent it. If an installation stays wet long after rain or grows persistent moss, deal with the drainage first.
