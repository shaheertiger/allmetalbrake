Why Is My Deck Stain Peeling? Causes and Fixes (2026)

Peeling is almost never a bad can of stain. It is a film that could not hold on — to wet wood, to glazed boards, to old coatings, or to a horizontal surface that was never going to keep it. Here is how to tell which, and what actually fixes it.

Deck stain peels when it forms a film on top of the wood instead of soaking into it, and that film then loses its grip. The usual reasons are a solid or heavily built-up stain on horizontal boards that take foot traffic and standing water, stain applied over wood that was still wet or glazed from the mill, too many coats stacked over the years, a new product put over an old one it does not bond to, or a surface that was never properly cleaned. Moisture coming up from underneath a low, poorly ventilated deck makes every one of those worse.

The fix depends on how widespread the peeling is and which stain you used. Small, isolated patches on an otherwise sound coating can be sanded, feathered and touched up. Peeling across most of the deck means the coating has failed, and the only lasting repair is to strip it back to bare wood, clean and brighten, let it dry, and restart — ideally with a penetrating stain that wears away gradually rather than lifting off in sheets.

⚡ The quick answer: Peeling is an adhesion failure, and it happens almost exclusively with film-forming stains — solid colour, many semi-solids, and anything applied too thick. The top causes are wet or new pressure-treated wood, too many coats, stain over an incompatible old finish, skipped cleaning and application in direct sun. If less than about 10–15% of the deck is peeling and the rest passes a tape test, spot sand and touch up. If it is peeling in traffic lanes or across whole boards, strip to bare wood and switch to a penetrating semi-transparent stain, which fades instead of peeling.

Why Deck Stain Peels at All

Deck stains fall into two broad families, and only one of them can peel in the usual sense.

Penetrating stains — transparent, toners and most semi-transparent oils — soak into the top layer of the wood fibres and leave little or nothing sitting on the surface. When they wear out, they fade and grey. There is no film to lift, so there is nothing to peel. At worst you get blotchy wear in traffic lanes.

Film-forming stains — solid colour stains, many semi-solids, and the thick “resurfacer” coatings sold for worn decks — behave much more like paint. They sit on top of the wood as a continuous skin, and that skin depends entirely on its bond to whatever is underneath. When the bond fails, the film lifts, cracks and flakes.

A deck is about the hardest place on a house to keep a film bonded. Horizontal boards hold standing water and snow, take direct overhead sun, expand and shrink across their width with every wet-dry cycle, and get ground by grit under shoes and chair legs. The same solid stain that lasts eight to ten years on a vertical fence or siding commonly starts peeling within two to four years on deck boards. That is not a defect in the product; it is physics. It is also why the choice between solid and semi-transparent stain matters far more on the deck floor than on the railings.

So the first question when a deck peels is really two questions: was the stain a film-former, and if so, what weakened its bond? Most peeling decks have one primary cause and one or two contributing ones.

The Eight Common Causes of Peeling Deck Stain

1. A film-forming stain on horizontal boards

This is the root cause behind most peeling decks, even when something else triggered it. Solid stain on the walking surface is fighting water pooling on top, water wicking in through end grain and cracks, and boards moving under it. Once a hairline crack opens in the film, water gets underneath, has nowhere to evaporate to, and pushes the coating off from below. The peeling then spreads outward from every crack, nail head and board end.

Solid stain on railings, posts, fascia and skirting is a perfectly reasonable choice. Solid stain on the floor boards is a choice to accept recoating every few years and eventually stripping.

2. Too many coats and years of build-up

Every recoat adds thickness. A film that started at a sensible thickness becomes, after three or four maintenance coats, a thick, rigid layer that cannot flex with the wood. Thick films crack more readily, and when they let go, they take every layer with them. Build-up also happens within a single job: semi-transparent stain is designed to be absorbed, and a second heavy coat on dense or already-saturated wood has nowhere to go, so it dries as a soft surface film that scuffs and peels within the first season.

The rule of thumb is that a penetrating stain should be applied only as heavily as the wood will drink in roughly 10–20 minutes, with any excess back-brushed or wiped off before it skins over.

3. New stain over an old, incompatible finish

Stains do not all bond to one another. The common failures:

  • A penetrating stain over a solid or film stain. It cannot soak in through the old film, so it sits on top and peels or stays tacky.
  • An oil-based product over an intact water-based acrylic film. The oil has no porous surface to grip and frequently fails.
  • Any product over a coating that is already failing. The new stain may bond beautifully to the old one, but the old one is still letting go of the wood — and now it takes the fresh coat with it.
  • A water-based product over oil that has not weathered. Fresh oil residue repels water-based coatings; many manufacturers require removal or a season of weathering first.

If you do not know what is on the deck, assume it is incompatible and test a small area before committing.

4. Wet wood and new pressure-treated lumber

Stain needs dry wood to penetrate or bond. Most wood stains want moisture content below about 15%, and some labels ask for 12%. New pressure-treated lumber typically arrives far wetter than that — the treatment process forces preservative solution deep into the boards, and lumber that is not kiln-dried after treatment can sit well above 20% for weeks or months. Stain applied over it cannot soak in, and as the boards dry, the water leaving the wood pushes against any surface film.

Wood that is wet from a recent cleaning, rain or heavy dew causes the same problem on a smaller scale. That includes boards that look dry on top but are still damp in the gaps and on the underside. Our guide to staining pressure-treated wood covers how long new boards typically need, and the water test below tells you when yours are ready.

5. Mill glaze on new boards

New lumber, especially smooth-planed softwood, can come with a compressed, slightly shiny surface layer from the planer blades and from resins drawn to the surface during drying. This “mill glaze” is debated among wood scientists as to how much it really matters, but most stain manufacturers treat it as real and so should you: it reduces how much stain the surface can absorb, and a film stain bonded to a glazed surface has a weaker grip. A deck cleaner or brightener, light sanding with 60–80 grit, or simply letting new boards weather for a few months opens the surface back up.

6. No cleaning or brightening before staining

Weathered deck wood is covered in a grey layer of UV-damaged fibre, dirt, pollen, mildew and chalked old stain. Stain applied over that bonds to the debris, not the wood. The tell-tale sign is grey fuzz or loose wood fibres stuck to the back of the peeled flakes. Pressure washing alone does not reliably fix it; a proper deck cleaner (usually percarbonate or sodium hydroxide based) followed by an oxalic or citric acid brightener removes the damaged layer and neutralises the cleaner, leaving the wood able to absorb stain evenly.

7. Applied in direct sun, in heat, or too thick

A sun-baked deck board can be 30–50°F hotter than the air around it. Stain applied to it flash-dries on the surface before it has time to penetrate, leaving a thin skin over unstained wood — which then peels or wears away in the first season. Heat also traps solvent or water under a skin that has already formed, which shows as bubbles and blisters. The same thing happens with thick application: a puddled coat dries on top first and never bonds properly underneath. Our guide to the right temperature to stain a deck covers surface temperature in detail; the short version is to stain in shade or on an overcast day, with the air between about 50°F and 90°F.

Not leaving enough time between coats, or walking on the deck before it has properly dried, causes related failures. See our guide to deck stain drying time for the recoat and foot-traffic windows by stain type.

8. Moisture from below and poor ventilation

Ground-level and low decks — anything with less than roughly 18–24 inches of clearance, or enclosed by solid skirting — sit over a damp microclimate. Moisture from the soil evaporates upward into the underside of the boards, which are usually unstained. With a film stain on top, that moisture has no way out except through the coating, and it lifts it. The pattern is distinctive: peeling worst over bare soil, in shaded corners, close to the house and along board ends, with the boards often cupping as well.

Clogged gaps between boards make it worse by holding water and debris against the edges. On these decks, a breathable penetrating stain is not just a better choice; it is close to the only choice that will last.

💡 The water drop test. Sprinkle a few drops of water on several boards, including in the shade. If the water soaks in within about 10 minutes, the wood is dry and open enough to take stain. If it beads up or sits on the surface, the boards are still too wet, glazed, or sealed by an old coating — and stain applied now is likely to peel.

How to Diagnose Your Deck

You can usually identify the cause in ten minutes by looking at two things: where the peeling is, and what is on the back of the flakes. Peel off a loose piece and turn it over.

  • Back of flake is clean, wood underneath is clean and smooth: the stain never bonded to the wood. Suspect mill glaze, wet wood, or application in hot sun.
  • Grey fuzz or wood fibres stuck to the back of the flake: the stain bonded to weathered, UV-damaged surface fibres that then tore away. Suspect skipped cleaning and brightening.
  • An older layer of stain is still on the wood under the flake: the new coat failed to bond to the old one. Suspect incompatible products or a glossy, unprepared old film.
  • Several layers come off together, down to bare wood: build-up from repeated coats, or an old base coat that was already failing.

The tape test

Before deciding on a repair, check how well the coating that is not peeling is holding. Press a strip of strong duct tape or masking tape firmly onto an intact area, rub it down, then rip it off sharply. Do this in several spots, including traffic lanes and shaded areas. If the tape comes away clean or with only a few specks, the remaining coating is sound and a spot repair is reasonable. If it pulls up flakes of stain, the whole coating is losing adhesion and anything you put on top will fail with it.

This is the decision that determines whether you fix the deck once or keep fixing it every spring.

Spot repair is tempting because it is quicker, but it only works when the problem is genuinely local — a few board ends, a spot under a downspout, a patch where a planter sat. If the cause was the stain type, the wood condition at application, or an incompatible old coat, the rest of the deck is failing on the same timeline, just more slowly. Patching it buys a season.

Stripping is more work, but it is the only way to change stain type, remove build-up, or recover from a coating that never bonded. On a typical 300 square foot deck it is a long weekend of labour plus drying time. Professional strip-and-restain jobs commonly run somewhere around $2–$5 per square foot depending on region, deck condition and how many layers are on it, so the DIY saving is real.

Stripping a Peeling Deck, Step by Step

Our full guide to how to strip deck stain goes deeper on products and technique, but the sequence for a peeling deck is:

1. Scrape the loose material first. A stiff paint scraper or a long-handled floor scraper removes the flakes that are already lifting. This takes most of a morning but makes the chemical stripper far more effective, because it can reach the coating that is still bonded.

2. Apply a deck stain stripper. Most deck strippers are sodium hydroxide based, which softens oil, acrylic and solid stains. Wet the surrounding plants and cover them, wet the deck, and apply the stripper with a pump sprayer or roller, working in sections. Keep it wet for the dwell time on the label — often 10–20 minutes — and never let it dry on the wood. Solid stains and multiple layers usually need a second application.

3. Scrub and rinse. Agitate with a stiff synthetic brush, then rinse. A pressure washer helps, but keep it gentle: on softwoods like pine and cedar, roughly 500–1,200 PSI with a 25° or 40° fan tip held 12 inches or more from the surface. Higher pressure or a narrow tip gouges the soft earlywood and raises fuzz that will take stain unevenly.

4. Brighten. Stripper leaves the wood alkaline and darkened. An oxalic or citric acid deck brightener neutralises it, restores the wood's colour and opens the grain. This step is easy to skip and is one of the most common reasons a freshly stripped deck peels again.

5. Sand what the stripper missed. Stubborn patches of solid stain, especially in board edges and checks, often survive chemical stripping. Once the deck is dry, sand them with 60–80 grit using a random orbital sander or a rented floor sander. Do not finish finer than 80 grit; sanding deck boards too smooth closes the surface and reduces how much stain it can absorb.

6. Let it dry. Give the deck at least 48 hours of dry weather after the final rinse, longer in cool or humid conditions, then do the water drop test before staining. A moisture meter, which costs less than a gallon of good stain, takes the guesswork out.

💡 Do not sand instead of stripping on a thick film. Sanding a thick solid or resurfacer coating clogs paper in minutes, heats and smears the coating into the grain, and on soft boards removes more wood than stain. Strip first, then sand only the remnants.

Spot Sanding and Touch-Up

When the tape test shows the rest of the coating is sound and the peeling is limited, a targeted repair is reasonable.

  1. Remove everything loose. Scrape all lifting stain until the edges of the remaining coating are firmly bonded and cannot be lifted with a fingernail or putty knife.
  2. Feather the edges. Sand the transition between bare wood and intact coating with 80 grit until the step is smooth. A hard edge will show through the touch-up and gives water a place to get under the film again.
  3. Clean the repair area. Use deck cleaner on the bare patches, rinse well, and brighten. Bare wood that has weathered grey must be cleaned back to fresh fibre, or the touch-up will peel exactly as the original did.
  4. Fix the local cause. Seal exposed board ends with stain, redirect a downspout, move the planter, clear the board gaps, or replace a split board.
  5. Touch up with the same product. Apply thin coats, ideally across whole boards end to end rather than in patches, to avoid a visible repair. With semi-transparent stains, touch-ups usually blend after a few weeks of weathering; with solid stains, expect to recoat whole boards or sections for an even colour.

Spot repairs make the most sense on penetrating stains, where new stain blends naturally into faded areas, and on vertical parts of the deck. On a solid-stained floor, frequent spot repairs are usually a sign it is time to plan a full strip.

Switching to a Penetrating Stain

If the deck floor has peeled once, the strongest single change you can make is to stop using a film-forming product on it. A penetrating oil or hybrid semi-transparent stain soaks into the wood, wears by gradual fading, and can be refreshed by simply cleaning and applying another coat — no scraping, no stripping. The trade-off is that it needs recoating more often on horizontal surfaces, typically every one to three years depending on sun exposure and traffic, and it will not hide stains, patches or mismatched boards the way solid colour does.

You cannot switch without removing the old film. A semi-transparent stain applied over remnants of solid stain cannot soak in, and it will look blotchy and peel from those patches. That means stripping and sanding back to genuinely bare wood. If the deck has been solid-stained for a decade and the boards are badly worn, it can be more practical to replace the worst boards, or to accept another solid stain on the floor knowing it will need maintenance. Our deck stain comparison covers penetrating options by base and opacity, and the best deck sealants guide covers clear water repellents for those who want the natural wood look.

If you prefer an opaque look and are willing to maintain it, a dedicated deck paint or porch-and-floor coating is designed to be a film and is formulated for foot traffic. It will still need careful preparation and periodic recoating, but it is a more honest choice than a solid stain stretched to do a paint's job.

A practical compromise

Many decks look best and last longest with a split system: a penetrating semi-transparent stain on the floor boards and stair treads, and a solid stain in a coordinating colour on the rails, posts, fascia and skirting. The vertical surfaces keep their colour for years, and the horizontal surfaces get easy maintenance coats without ever peeling.

How to Avoid Peeling Next Time

Almost every peeling deck traces back to preparation and product choice rather than bad luck. Before the next coat goes on:

  • Choose penetrating stain for horizontal boards. Keep solid stain and paint for vertical surfaces unless you are prepared to maintain a film on the floor.
  • Let new pressure-treated wood dry. Wait until the water drop test passes, which is often several weeks to a few months for wet-treated lumber. Kiln-dried-after-treatment boards can usually be stained much sooner.
  • Clean and brighten every time. Even for a maintenance coat on a sound penetrating stain, a deck cleaner and rinse remove the dirt and mildew the new coat would otherwise bond to.
  • Check compatibility. Recoat with the same product line where possible. If you are changing brands or bases, confirm on the label what it can go over, or strip first.
  • Apply thin. One coat of penetrating stain, or two wet-on-wet if the label calls for it, with excess back-brushed or wiped off. Never put on a second coat of semi-transparent stain once the first has dried unless the wood is still visibly thirsty.
  • Stain in shade or overcast conditions. Follow the sun around the deck, work in the early morning or late afternoon, and keep the boards out of direct midday sun while the stain penetrates.
  • Watch the forecast. Plan for a dry day before staining, the day of, and the day after — rain or heavy dew on fresh stain causes its own failures.
  • Improve ventilation under low decks. Open up or replace solid skirting with lattice or spaced boards, clear debris from under the deck, and keep the gaps between boards open with a putty knife each season.
  • Seal end grain and cut ends. Board ends take in water many times faster than the face. Give them an extra coat of stain or an end-grain sealer when boards are cut or replaced.
  • Recoat before it fails. A penetrating stain that is refreshed while it still has some colour needs only cleaning and one coat. Waiting until it is grey and bare means brightening, possibly sanding, and more stain.

The Short Version

Peeling deck stain is a film losing its bond. On a deck floor, the most common underlying reason is simply that a solid or film-forming stain was used on a horizontal surface, and the most common triggers are wet or new wood, build-up from too many coats, an incompatible old finish, skipped cleaning, and application in hot sun.

Diagnose it by checking where the peeling is and what is on the back of the flakes, then run a tape test on the coating that has not peeled yet. Limited, local peeling on a sound coating can be scraped, feathered and touched up. Widespread peeling means stripping to bare wood, brightening, letting it dry, and restaining — preferably with a penetrating semi-transparent stain on the floor, which fades gradually rather than lifting.

Do the preparation properly once and the next maintenance coat is a clean-and-recoat afternoon, not another weekend with a scraper.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my deck stain peeling after one year?

Peeling within the first year almost always points to a preparation or timing problem rather than normal wear. The most likely causes are staining new pressure-treated lumber before it dried, staining over mill glaze on new boards, skipping the cleaning and brightening step on weathered wood, or applying the stain in direct sun or too thick so it skinned over without penetrating. A new coat over an old, incompatible finish also fails quickly. Peel off a flake and look at the back: clean wood underneath suggests wet or glazed wood, grey fibres suggest skipped cleaning, and an older coat underneath suggests incompatibility. In nearly every first-year case, the lasting fix is to strip and restart with proper preparation.

Can I just stain over peeling deck stain?

No. Any new coat bonds to whatever is underneath, and if that layer is already letting go of the wood, it will take the new stain with it — usually within a season. At a minimum, scrape off everything loose, feather the edges, clean the bare spots and touch up only where the remaining coating passes a tape test. If the peeling is widespread or the tape test pulls up stain from areas that look intact, the whole coating has failed and needs to be stripped back to bare wood before restaining.

Does semi-transparent deck stain peel?

A true penetrating semi-transparent stain rarely peels, because it soaks into the wood rather than forming a surface film. It wears by fading. It can peel, however, if it was applied too heavily or in too many coats, leaving excess stain drying on the surface; if it was put on dense or wet wood that could not absorb it; or if it was applied over an old film stain that blocked penetration. Some products labelled semi-transparent are acrylic film-formers, and those behave more like a thin solid stain. Applying only as much as the wood absorbs and wiping off the excess prevents nearly all of these failures.

Should I sand or strip peeling deck stain?

Strip first, then sand. A chemical deck stripper removes most of a solid or film stain far faster than sanding, and sanding a thick coating clogs paper quickly and smears softened stain into the grain. After stripping, rinsing, brightening and drying, sand any stubborn patches with 60–80 grit. For small, local peeling on an otherwise sound coating, scraping and sanding alone is fine. Avoid sanding deck boards finer than 80 grit, because a very smooth surface absorbs less stain.

How long should new pressure-treated wood dry before staining?

Until it passes the water drop test, which for typical wet-treated lumber is often anywhere from several weeks to a few months depending on climate, sun exposure and how wet the boards were when installed. Sprinkle water on the boards: if it soaks in within about ten minutes, the wood is ready; if it beads, wait. A moisture meter reading below about 15% is a good confirmation. Lumber stamped KDAT, kiln-dried after treatment, is much drier on delivery and can usually be stained far sooner, after a cleaning to remove mill glaze and dirt.

Why is my deck stain peeling only in some areas?

Location tells you the cause. Peeling concentrated in traffic lanes and on steps is wear on a film stain. Peeling on board ends and around fasteners is water entering through end grain. Peeling in shaded areas, low sections and near the house usually means moisture from below or poor ventilation. Peeling on the sunniest section, often with blisters, suggests the stain was applied on hot boards. If only the floor peels while the rails and posts are fine, the stain is a film-former that simply cannot survive horizontal exposure. Local causes can be spot-repaired; a stain-type problem needs the floor stripped.

How much does it cost to fix a peeling deck?

DIY, a full strip and restain on a 300 square foot deck typically means a couple of gallons of stripper, brightener, two or more gallons of stain, sanding discs and possibly a pressure washer or sander rental — usually a few hundred dollars in materials and a long weekend of work, plus drying time. Professional strip-and-restain work commonly runs roughly $2–$5 per square foot depending on region, number of coating layers and deck condition. Spot repair of a small area is far cheaper, but only worthwhile if the rest of the coating is genuinely sound.

What is the best stain to stop deck peeling?

For horizontal boards, a penetrating oil-based or hybrid semi-transparent stain is the most reliable way to avoid peeling, because it has no surface film to lift. Transparent toners and clear penetrating sealers also cannot peel, but they need more frequent recoating. If you want an opaque colour on the floor, a coating designed as a deck or porch paint handles foot traffic better than a solid stain, though it still needs careful preparation and periodic maintenance. Whatever you choose, proper cleaning, brightening, dry wood and thin application matter more than the brand.
