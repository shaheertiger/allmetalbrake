Magnetic, edge-finding, center-finding and multi-sensor stud finders all work — on the right wall. The type of wall you have matters more than the brand on the tool, and every reading still needs confirming before you drill.

If you live in an older house with plaster and lath walls, skip the electronics entirely and buy a rare-earth magnetic finder — it costs a fraction as much and is the one type that reliably works through plaster.

The Four Types of Stud Finder

Every stud finder on the market works on one of two principles. Magnetic finders locate the steel fasteners holding the wall covering to the framing. Electronic finders measure changes in the wall's density by sensing capacitance — a stud behind the drywall changes the electrical field the sensor plate produces, and the tool reads that change. Within the electronic group, the differences are about how many sensors there are and how the tool interprets what they see.

1. Magnetic Stud Finders

A magnetic finder is a pair of rare-earth (neodymium) magnets in a plastic housing. Slide it across the wall and it grabs when it passes over a drywall screw or nail; because those fasteners run in a vertical line every 8 to 16 inches up a stud, a column of hits is a stud. It needs no batteries, does not care about wall density, and works on plaster, paneling and textured finishes where electronics struggle.

The drawbacks are speed and precision. You are searching for a fastener head roughly a quarter-inch across, so finding the first one takes some sweeping. And a magnet tells you where the screw is, not where the stud's edges are — screws can sit anywhere across the 1-1/2-inch face of a stud, so you know you are on the stud, not that you are in its center.

2. Edge-Finding Electronic

The classic design. You hold the tool on the wall, press a button to calibrate it to the wall's baseline density, then slide it sideways until it signals a change — that is the first edge of the stud. Repeat from the other side to find the second edge, and the center is halfway between your two marks. These are the cheapest electronic finders and they work well on standard drywall, but they depend entirely on calibrating over a hollow bay. Calibrate over a stud and the tool treats the stud as "normal wall" and will not find it.

3. Center-Finding Electronic

A center-finding tool uses more than one sensor plate and some processing to locate both edges in a single pass, then indicates the center directly — usually with an arrow, a target on a display, or a light. It still calibrates when you place it on the wall, but it is faster and more forgiving than a single-plate edge finder, and most center-finders are packaged as multi-scanners with AC and metal modes.

4. Multi-Sensor and Radar-Style

Multi-sensor finders, popularized by Franklin Sensors, use a row of independent sensor pads across a wide base. Each pad compares itself to its neighbours rather than to a calibration baseline, so there is no calibration step: you place the tool anywhere, including directly over a stud, and a row of LEDs lights up across the full width of whatever is behind the wall. That one change eliminates the most common user error in the category.

Radar-style imagers go further. The Walabot DIY, for example, clips to a smartphone and uses radio-frequency imaging to draw a picture of what is in the wall — studs, pipes, wiring — on the phone screen, with the manufacturer quoting a depth of up to about four inches. Professional wall scanners from Bosch and Hilti work on similar principles at several times the price. They are genuinely impressive, but they are slower to use than a stud finder, they require learning to interpret the image, and for locating studs alone they offer little over a good multi-sensor tool.

Franklin Sensors ProSensor 710
🏆 Best Overall

The ProSensor 710 is the tool that made multi-sensor stud finding mainstream, and it remains the easiest stud finder for a non-specialist to get a correct answer from. Matching the Tool to the Wall

The single biggest predictor of whether a stud finder works is the wall, not the tool. Capacitive sensors are designed around a thin, uniform sheet of gypsum over an air gap; the further a wall departs from that, the worse they perform.

Drywall

Standard 1/2-inch drywall on walls and 5/8-inch on ceilings and fire-rated assemblies is the ideal case. Any of the electronic tools above will work, with multi-sensor and center-finding models the most forgiving. Two layers of drywall — common on shared garage walls and in some sound-rated rooms — push the total thickness past an inch, and a basic edge-finder needs its deep-scan mode to see through it.

Plaster and Lath

Houses built before roughly the 1950s usually have plaster over wood lath — horizontal strips of wood nailed to every stud with small gaps between them, covered by three-quarters of an inch or more of dense plaster. Every part of this defeats a capacitive tool. The plaster is thick and varies in density, and the lath itself is a continuous layer of wood across the whole wall, so the tool sees "wood" everywhere and studs barely register against it. Some plaster walls are applied over expanded metal lath instead, which blocks capacitive tools completely and makes a magnet stick everywhere.

On wood lath, a magnet is the right tool. The lath nails sit at every stud, every couple of inches vertically, one or two where each lath strip crosses. Find one, then confirm two more directly above and below it, and you have a stud. Plaster patches cleanly afterwards — see our guide to spackle vs. putty for which filler suits which hole.

Textured Walls and Ceilings

Heavy knockdown, orange-peel or popcorn texture lifts the tool off the surface in places. A capacitive sensor reads that air gap as a change in density and throws false readings, or rocks enough to recalibrate itself mid-pass. The workaround is to slide the tool over a sheet of thin card or paper laid on the wall, which gives it a smooth path without adding meaningful thickness. A magnet is unaffected by texture. Ceilings are the same story with joists at 16 or 24 inches — and while you are up there, our guide to the best paint for ceilings covers how to touch up the test holes invisibly.

Tile

Tile is the hardest case for any consumer stud finder. In newer bathrooms, tile sits on cement board or a foam backer board fastened with screws to the studs; in older ones it may be set in a thick mortar bed over metal lath. Tile, thinset and cement board together can easily run an inch or more, with uneven density. A capacitive tool is mostly guessing. A strong magnet can sometimes find the backer board screws, which run at roughly 8-inch spacing along each stud, and a metal-mode scanner like the GMS120 does better. Radar imagers are the one category that genuinely handles tile well. The more reliable method is to find studs on an untiled wall nearby, measure across, and confirm through a grout line with a small masonry bit.

Concrete and Block

There are no studs in a concrete or block wall, and a stud finder cannot help — except to find wood furring strips if the wall has been finished over. On bare masonry, use masonry anchors directly into the block or poured wall. If you are finishing a basement, sort out the wall's moisture first; our guide to the best basement wall sealers covers that before you add any framing.

AC Detection and Metal vs. Wood

What AC Detection Actually Detects

AC detection senses the alternating electric field around an energized, unshielded conductor. That is useful, but it has limits you need to know before you rely on it. It does not detect a cable that is switched off — the cable feeding a light fixture is invisible to the tool when the light is off. It does not see through metal: armored cable, MC cable and conduit shield the field. It does not detect low-voltage wiring such as doorbell, thermostat, speaker or network cable. And sensitivity depends on how close the cable is to the surface and how humid the wall is.

Treat an AC alert as a reason to stop and think, and the absence of one as no guarantee. Before drilling anywhere near an outlet, switch or fixture, turn on every switch in the room so switched cables are energized, and remember that cable often runs straight up or down from a box and horizontally through studs at roughly outlet height. The National Electrical Code requires steel nail plates where a cable passes through a stud within 1-1/4 inches of its face, precisely because a screw that length can reach it — but older wiring was not always installed that carefully.

Metal Studs

Steel studs are common in commercial buildings, condominium towers and some basement remodels. A capacitive stud finder will usually locate them in its normal mode, and a magnet sticks to them directly along the entire length, which is the fastest way to confirm what you are dealing with. The fixing method is what changes: a wood screw in a light-gauge steel stud has very little holding power. Use snap toggles or toggle bolts through the stud face, or self-drilling screws rated for the gauge, and follow the TV mount maker's instructions for steel framing.

Metal Mode for Pipes

A metal-scan mode helps in kitchens and bathrooms, where copper supply lines and steel gas pipe share stud bays with the framing you want. It will not see PEX or plastic drain pipe, which are increasingly common. If a stud reading falls exactly where plumbing would logically run — in the wall behind a sink, toilet or tub, or the wall below an upstairs bathroom — treat it with suspicion until you confirm it is wood.

🔧 Before any large hole: Turn on all the switches, scan in AC mode, scan in metal mode, then drill a small pilot first. A 1/8-inch pilot that hits something unexpected costs a dab of spackle. A 2-inch hole saw that hits a cable or a pipe costs an electrician or a plumber.

How to Use One Properly

Most stud finder failures are technique failures. These rules apply to every calibrating electronic tool; multi-sensor tools skip the calibration step but benefit from the rest.

  1. Fresh battery. A weak battery produces erratic readings well before the tool warns you. If the readings start making no sense, change the battery before blaming the tool or the wall.
  2. Calibrate over a hollow bay. Place the tool flat on the wall at a spot you believe is between studs, then press and hold the button until it signals it is ready. If you are not sure, calibrate in two places a few inches apart and compare. A tool calibrated over a stud will skip that stud and may report every stud on the wall as weaker than it is.
  3. Keep it flat and in contact. Rocking or lifting the tool changes the reading. Most tools need recalibrating if they leave the wall.
  4. Move slowly and steadily, horizontally, with no pressure changes. Rushing past a stud is the second most common error after poor calibration.
  5. Keep your other hand off the wall. Your body is a large capacitor, and a hand flat on the wall near the sensor can change the reading.
  6. Scan from both directions. With an edge-finder, mark the edge approaching from the left, then from the right. The two marks should be about 1-1/2 inches apart for a single stud; the center is between them. Much wider than that suggests a doubled stud, a pipe or a header.
  7. Scan at two heights. Repeat a foot above or below. A stud gives you two marks that line up vertically; a horizontal pipe or a fire block will not.

False Positives and How to Confirm

Everything in a wall that is denser than an empty bay can register as a stud. The usual culprits are copper pipe, the edges of drywall sheets where the joint compound is thick, metal corner bead near outside corners, horizontal fire blocking, electrical boxes, heating ducts, and patches of dampness. On an exterior wall, insulation that is compressed or foil-faced can confuse the reading too.

None of that matters if you confirm the stud before relying on it. Three methods, from quickest to most certain:

Measure the Layout

Framing follows a pattern. Most walls are framed with studs every 16 inches on center; some newer houses, garages and walls built with 2x6 studs use 24 inches. Once you have one confirmed stud, measure 16 inches each way and the next one should be there. If the stud finder reports something at 11 inches, be suspicious. Corners, door and window openings break the pattern, because they carry extra studs — a king stud and a jack stud on each side of an opening, and two or three studs at a corner.

Use the Outlet Box

Most electrical boxes in wood framing are nailed to the side of a stud. Turn off the circuit, remove the cover plate, and look or probe at the side of the box — you can usually see or feel which side the stud is on. That gives you an edge you can measure from, and it is often the fastest way to establish the 16-inch grid across a room. Baseboard nails are a similar clue: trim carpenters nail into studs, so the small filled nail holes along the baseboard often line up with them.

The Finish Nail Test

The definitive check. Drive a thin finish nail — a 4d or 6d — through the drywall at your mark, in a spot the fixture will cover. Solid resistance after the first half inch means wood. If it pushes straight through, you are in the bay; move 3/4 inch across and try again. Probing a couple of times either side also tells you exactly where the edges are, which matters when you are putting a lag bolt into the center of a stud. The holes are tiny and fill with a thumb of spackle.

For heavy items — TVs, cabinets, grab bars — a small pilot bit in a drill does the same job and tells you even more: wood produces pale shavings, while drywall produces white powder and then nothing. What a Stud Finder Can't Tell You

A stud finder answers one question: is there something denser than an empty bay behind this spot? It cannot tell you:

  • What the object is. A stud, a pipe, a doubled drywall seam and a fire block can all look the same. Only confirmation tells you.
  • How deep it is or how far it extends. Capacitive tools do not measure depth. You do not know how thick the wall covering is or how far back a pipe sits behind a stud.
  • Whether a wire is safe. No AC alert does not mean no cable; it may be switched off, shielded or low-voltage.
  • The condition of the stud. Rot, old water damage, termite damage or a stud notched almost through for plumbing all read the same as a sound stud.
  • Whether the wall can carry the load. A stud will hold a TV mount comfortably; a cabinet run full of dishes deserves screws into several studs and a ledger. That is engineering, not detection.
  • What is behind tile, masonry or metal lath with any reliability, unless you step up to radar imaging.

The tool shortens the search. The confirmation is still your job.

Which One to Buy

Most homes with drywall: the Franklin Sensors ProSensor 710. It is the most reliable for non-specialists because it removes the calibration step entirely and shows you the whole stud.

Frequent drilling, renovations, or older wiring you do not trust: the Zircon MultiScanner i520 for an all-round multi-scanner, or the Bosch GMS120 if finding metal and pipes matters more than finding wood.

Plaster and lath: the CH Hanson 03040. Do not waste money on electronics for these walls.

Tight budget: the StudBuddy for magnetic, or the Zircon StudSensor e50 if you want a wire warning.

The best toolbox setup, and the one we would recommend to anyone who hangs things regularly, is an electronic finder plus a cheap magnet. The electronic tool finds candidates quickly; the magnet confirms them by finding the fasteners; a finish nail confirms the edges. Together they cost less than a single premium scanner, and they work on almost any wall. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most accurate type of stud finder?

On drywall, multi-sensor stud finders such as the Franklin Sensors ProSensor 710 are the most accurate for typical users, because they use a row of sensors that compare themselves to each other rather than to a calibration baseline. That removes the most common error — calibrating over a stud — and the LED array shows both edges and the full width of the stud at once. On plaster and lath, a rare-earth magnetic finder is more accurate than any capacitive tool, because it detects the lath nails directly rather than trying to read density through thick, uneven plaster. Whatever the tool, confirm with a finish nail before relying on it.

Why does my stud finder find studs everywhere?

Almost always because it was calibrated over a stud or something dense, because the battery is weak, or because the wall is plaster and lath. A calibrating stud finder sets its baseline wherever you press the button, so if that spot was dense, everything else reads oddly. Recalibrate over what you believe is a hollow bay, change the battery, and keep your other hand off the wall. On plaster and lath, the wood lath forms a continuous layer across the whole wall, so capacitive tools see wood everywhere. Switch to a magnetic finder on those walls.

Do magnetic stud finders actually work?

Yes. A rare-earth magnetic stud finder locates the steel screws or nails that hold the wall covering to the studs. Because those fasteners run in vertical lines up each stud, finding two or three in a vertical row confirms a stud with high certainty. They need no batteries or calibration and work on plaster, paneling and heavy texture where electronic finders struggle. The drawbacks are that they are slower to find the first fastener and they show a point on the stud rather than its edges. They also stick directly to steel studs, which makes metal framing easy to identify.

How far apart are studs in a wall?

Usually 16 inches from the center of one stud to the center of the next, which is the most common spacing for interior and exterior walls in houses. Some newer homes, garages and walls framed with 2x6 lumber use 24-inch spacing. Studs themselves are 1-1/2 inches thick on the face you drill into. The pattern breaks at corners and at door and window openings, which have extra studs, so measure from a confirmed stud in the middle of a wall rather than from a corner. If your stud finder reports something well off the 16- or 24-inch grid, check it before relying on it.

Can a stud finder detect live wires?

Many can, using AC detection that senses the electric field around an energized, unshielded cable. It has real limits: it cannot see a cable that is switched off, it cannot see through armored cable or metal conduit, and it does not detect low-voltage wiring such as doorbell or network cable. Turn on every switch in the room before scanning, and assume cable runs vertically above and below outlets and switches. Treat an alert as a warning and the absence of one as no guarantee.

Will a stud finder work through tile?

Not reliably. Tile, thinset and cement board together can exceed an inch of uneven, dense material, which defeats most capacitive stud finders. A strong magnet can sometimes locate the backer board screws, and a metal-mode scanner does better still, but the most reliable approach is to locate studs on an adjacent untiled wall and measure across using the 16-inch layout, then confirm by drilling a small pilot hole through a grout line with a masonry or tile bit. Radar imaging scanners are the one category that handles tile well, at a much higher price.

How do I find a stud without a stud finder?

Start at an outlet or switch box: with the power off and the cover removed, you can usually see or feel which side the stud is on, and measure 16 inches from there. Look at baseboard nail holes, which trim carpenters place into studs. Knock along the wall — a hollow bay sounds hollow, a stud sounds dull and solid, though this is imprecise. Then confirm with a thin finish nail driven at your mark in a spot that will be covered; solid resistance means wood. A strong fridge magnet run slowly across the wall can also find drywall screws in a pinch.

Is a more expensive stud finder worth it?

Only if the extra money buys something you need. Moving from a basic edge-finder to a multi-sensor or center-finding tool is worth it for most people, because it eliminates the calibration error behind most bad readings. Paying for a multi-scanner is worth it if you drill large holes often and want separate metal and AC modes. Beyond that, radar and imaging scanners are impressive but slow, and for finding studs on drywall they add little. Everyone, at every budget, should own a cheap magnetic finder alongside whatever electronic tool they choose.
