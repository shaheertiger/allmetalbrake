Can You Stain Pressure-Treated Wood? (2026 Guide)

Yes — but not the week it arrives from the yard. Most treated lumber is sold soaking wet, and stain applied before that water leaves is the single most common reason a new deck peels in its first year.

Yes, you can stain pressure-treated wood, and you should — treatment protects the lumber from rot and insects, but it does nothing to stop the sun graying it, the rain cracking it, or the surface splintering. What decides whether the stain lasts is timing. Most pressure-treated lumber leaves the yard saturated with the water used to carry the preservative into it, and stain cannot get into wood that is already full of water.

The practical rule: stain when the wood passes a simple water-drop test, or when a moisture meter reads around 15% or below (or whatever the stain label specifies). For standard wet-treated lumber that typically takes several weeks in hot, dry weather and two to four months or more in cool, humid conditions. Lumber stamped KDAT — kiln-dried after treatment — is already dry and can usually be stained within days of installation, once it has been cleaned.

⚡ The short answer: Yes, once it is dry enough. Sprinkle water on the boards — if it soaks in within about 10 minutes, the wood is ready; if it beads up or sits on the surface, wait. A pin moisture meter reading of roughly 15% or lower confirms it. Wet-treated lumber typically needs 4 weeks to 4 months depending on climate and exposure; KDAT lumber can usually be stained within a few days to two weeks. Clean the surface first, and use a penetrating semi-transparent stain rather than a film-forming finish.

How to Tell When Pressure-Treated Wood Is Ready

Calendar advice — "wait six months," "wait a year" — is a rough proxy for one thing: the moisture content of the boards. A deck built in July in Arizona and one built in November in Oregon dry at completely different rates, so measuring beats counting days.

1. The Water-Drop (Sprinkle) Test

Sprinkle or flick a small amount of water onto several boards — not just the sunniest one. Watch what it does over the next 10 minutes.

  • It soaks in and the wood darkens within a few minutes: the cells have room for liquid, and they have room for stain. You are ready.
  • It beads, pools or sits on top for more than about 10 minutes: the wood is still saturated, or the surface is sealed by mill glaze or a factory water repellent. Wait, or clean and retest.

Test in several places: boards in shade, boards near the house wall, the lower steps and the joist-side faces of the decking all dry more slowly than the top surface in full sun. The deck is ready when the slowest area passes, not the fastest.

2. A Pin Moisture Meter

A basic two-pin wood moisture meter costs $20–$50 and removes the guesswork. Push the pins in across the grain, a few inches from the board end (ends dry faster and read low), and take readings on at least six boards in different spots. Most stain manufacturers want wood at or below roughly 12–15% moisture content; some allow slightly higher for water-based products. When the label gives a number, the label wins.

For reference, freshly delivered wet-treated lumber commonly reads 30% or higher — often off the scale of a cheap meter — while seasoned outdoor wood settles somewhere around 12–16% depending on local humidity. If you are reading in the mid-twenties, you are weeks away, not days.

💡 Check the end tag before you build. The plastic or paper tag stapled to the board end tells you more than any salesperson. Look for KDAT (kiln-dried after treatment) or ADAT (air-dried after treatment) — either means the wood was dried after the preservative went in. No drying designation means it is wet-treated, and you should plan your staining for weeks or months after the build, not the weekend after.

Two things push the timeline out further than people expect. First, decking boards dry mainly from the top face; the underside sits over damp ground with little moving air, so a low deck can feel dry on top and still read wet a quarter-inch down. Second, rain resets the clock partially. A week of wet weather does not undo a month of drying, but it can add a week or two before the surface passes the test again.

Waiting too long is a smaller problem, but it is a problem. Bare treated wood in strong sun starts to gray within a few months as UV breaks down lignin at the surface, and after a year unprotected it often has loose gray fibers, small checks and mildew spots. None of that prevents staining — it just means more cleaning and brightening first. The sweet spot is to stain within the first season, as soon as the wood passes the test.

Wet-Treated vs KDAT Lumber

Pressure treatment works by putting lumber in a sealed cylinder, pulling a vacuum, then forcing a water-based preservative solution — today usually a copper compound such as ACQ, copper azole or micronized copper azole — deep into the wood under pressure. The boards come out carrying a large amount of water on top of whatever moisture they held before.

Wet-treated lumber is shipped in that state, which is why it is heavy, often visibly wet, and the cheapest treated wood on the rack. It will shrink as it dries — boards installed tight together will open gaps, and some cupping, twisting and checking is normal over the first season. This is the lumber that needs the long wait.

KDAT (kiln-dried after treatment) lumber goes through a kiln after the preservative is applied, bringing it down to a moisture content of roughly 19% or below. It costs more — often noticeably more per board — and is usually a special order at big-box stores, but it arrives lighter, straighter and dimensionally stable. Because most of its shrinkage has already happened, you install it with the gaps you actually want, and you can stain it almost immediately.

ADAT (air-dried after treatment) is the same idea using time instead of a kiln. Moisture content varies more, so treat it as "probably ready" and confirm with a meter.

Even KDAT needs a little attention before staining. The surface often has a slight glaze from the planer and may carry dust, handling grime and pencil marks from construction. A cleaning pass and a drop test — usually within a week or two of installation — is enough. If you are planning a deck and the schedule matters, KDAT is one of the few upgrades that saves you a season of waiting.

Why Staining Wet Wood Fails

Every deck stain is designed to soak into the wood's cells — or, in the case of solid stains and paints, to grip the surface fibers. Wet treated wood defeats both, in three predictable ways.

The Stain Cannot Penetrate

A penetrating stain works by wicking into the open cell structure of the wood. When those cells are already full of water, there is nowhere for the stain to go. Oil and water do not mix, so an oil-based stain on wet wood mostly sits on the surface, stays tacky far longer than the label says, and wears off within months because it never anchored into anything. Water-based stains tolerate damp wood a little better, but they still dilute and absorb unevenly.

It Peels

Whatever does dry on top of saturated wood forms a thin film. Over the following weeks the water inside the boards tries to escape upward, and the boards themselves shrink across their width as they dry. The film is caught between moisture pushing from underneath and a surface moving beneath it, and it loses. The result is the classic first-year failure: flaking and deck stain peeling in sheets, usually worst on the boards that were wettest. Fixing it means stripping the failed coat and starting again, which is far more work than waiting would have been.

It Blotches

Treated lumber never dries evenly. Sapwood and heartwood, sunny and shaded boards, board ends and centers all reach different moisture levels at different times. Stain applied while that patchwork exists absorbs heavily where the wood is drier and barely at all where it is wet, leaving a deck with dark and light stripes that do not even out. Mildew can also grow under a stain film that has trapped moisture beneath it, showing up as gray or black spots that cleaning cannot reach.

💡 What about "apply to new wood" products? Some water repellents and a few stains state on the label that they can go on new pressure-treated lumber. Those products are formulated thin and breathable precisely because they expect wet wood, and they typically offer modest color and UV protection in exchange. Follow the label of the product you actually bought: if it does not explicitly say new or damp treated wood is fine, assume it is not.

Prepping Pressure-Treated Wood for Stain

Dry is necessary but not sufficient. Most stain failures that are not moisture-related are preparation-related, and treated lumber has a few quirks of its own.

Remove Mill Glaze and Construction Grime

New lumber can have a smooth, slightly shiny surface where the planer knives compressed and burnished the fibers. That glazed layer absorbs stain poorly and unevenly. Add a few weeks of construction — boot traffic, sawdust, tree pollen, pencil lines — and the surface is in no state to take a finish. Scrub it with an oxygenated (sodium percarbonate) deck cleaner, the kind recommended in our best deck cleaner guide, using a stiff synthetic brush. Avoid chlorine bleach, which damages wood fibers and does little for dirt.

Brighten

Oxygen and alkaline cleaners raise the wood's pH and can darken it. A follow-up oxalic acid brightener restores the pH, lightens the surface, and opens it up to absorb stain evenly. Many cleaner and stain manufacturers sell the two as a pair for exactly this reason. Rinse thoroughly after each step.

Light Sanding (Where Needed)

Sanding the whole deck is rarely necessary on new lumber, but a light pass with 60–80 grit helps on boards with obvious glaze, raised grain after cleaning, or rough splintered spots. Do not sand finer than about 80 grit — a polished surface closes the pores and reduces absorption, which is the opposite of what you want. Sweep and blow off the dust before staining.

Pressure Washing — Carefully

A pressure washer helps rinse cleaner off, but treated pine is soft. Keep pressure modest (roughly 500–1,200 psi on the wood, with a wide fan tip), hold the wand a foot or more away and move with the grain. Gouges and furring from too much pressure show up immediately once stain goes on.

Then let it dry again. Cleaning puts water back into the wood. Give it at least 24–48 hours of dry weather after washing, and repeat the drop test before staining.

Penetrating Oil-Based Semi-Transparent: the Default Choice

A penetrating oil stain soaks into the wood rather than forming a layer on top, so there is nothing to peel. When it wears, it fades gradually, and recoating is usually just clean and reapply rather than strip and start over. The pigment provides UV protection, which is what actually keeps the surface from graying. On treated pine, which tends to absorb stain unevenly, oil's slower penetration also tends to give a more uniform color. For a first coat on new treated lumber, this is the safest bet.

Water-Based and Hybrid Options

Modern water-based and water-borne oil hybrid stains have improved a great deal. They dry quickly — often recoatable within a few hours — clean up with soap and water, and carry lower VOC levels, which matters in regions with VOC restrictions. Most form a thin, flexible film as well as penetrating, which can give good color hold, but the film means adhesion and dry wood matter even more. Several water-based lines are specifically marketed for treated lumber. Our deck stain drying time guide covers how the two chemistries behave after application.

Solid Stain: When You Want the Green Gone

Solid stains behave much like a thin paint, hiding the wood's color and grain completely. They are the answer when the green tint, mismatched boards or replacement planks bother you, and they generally last longer on vertical surfaces like railings and skirting than on walking surfaces. The trade-off is that a film can peel, and when it does, it usually needs scraping or stripping before recoating. Our solid vs semi-transparent deck stain comparison goes through the decision in detail.

What to Skip

Clear sealers on their own are not stain — without pigment they offer little defense against UV, and the deck will gray under them within a season or two. Paint and film-forming varnishes on horizontal decking trap moisture and tend to fail first where feet and water concentrate. And avoid any product that does not list exterior wood or decking use on the label.

What Color to Expect on Green-Tinted Wood

The green cast on most treated lumber comes from the copper in the preservative. It fades as the wood dries and weathers, often toward a tan or honey color, but it rarely disappears entirely, and it affects how stain looks in ways the color chip never shows.

  • Transparent and toner stains barely change the base color. On green wood, expect a green-brown result, especially at first.
  • Semi-transparent stains in warm tones — cedar, redwood, chestnut, medium brown — mask the green well, because red and brown pigments neutralize green visually. These are the most forgiving choices.
  • Grays and light neutrals can read as greenish or murky on treated pine. If you want gray, a semi-solid or solid stain is usually the more reliable route.
  • Solid stains hide the tint entirely and look the same as on any other wood.

Treated pine also absorbs unevenly: earlywood (the softer, lighter bands) takes more stain than latewood, so semi-transparent stains often look more striped and a shade darker than the sample. Always test on an offcut or a hidden board — after cleaning and brightening, once it is dry — and judge the color the next day, not while it is wet. Some lumber is sold with a brown dye added to the treatment; it accepts stain the same way but starts from a warmer base, so lighter stains look darker on it.

Applying Stain: Conditions and Technique

Once the wood is ready and prepped, the application itself is straightforward. Our step-by-step guide on how to stain a deck walks through it in full; the points that matter most for treated lumber are these.

Weather. Most stains want air and surface temperatures of roughly 50–90°F, no rain forecast for 24–48 hours, and no direct midday sun on the boards. Hot sun dries the surface before the stain penetrates, causing lap marks. Our guide to what temperature to stain a deck covers the details, including overnight lows and dew.

Thin coats. Treated pine often absorbs more than expected on the first coat. Apply a thin, even coat, working two or three boards at a time along their full length to avoid lap marks, then back-brush to work it in. Wipe off any stain that has not absorbed after the time on the label — puddles left on the surface stay sticky and wear unevenly.

Second coat, if the label calls for it. Many penetrating stains recommend two coats applied wet-on-wet or within a short window. Others say one coat only, because a second will not soak in and simply sits on top. Follow the specific product.

Ends and cut edges. Board ends absorb heavily and are where checking and rot start. Give them an extra brushful. Fresh cuts made after treatment should already have had an end-cut preservative applied during the build; if not, treat them before staining.

Quantity. Rough-sawn and freshly dried treated lumber is thirsty, so coverage is often at the low end of what the can states. Work out your quantity with our guide to how much deck stain you need, and buy all of it from the same batch to avoid color differences between cans.

Common Mistakes With Pressure-Treated Wood

Staining the weekend after the build. The most common and most expensive mistake. Wet wood plus stain equals peeling, and the fix is stripping the whole deck.

Testing only the sunny boards. The top step in full sun can pass the drop test weeks before the shaded corner by the house wall. Test everywhere, and wait for the slowest area.

Skipping the cleaning because the wood is "new." New wood has mill glaze, dust and construction grime. Clean and brighten before the first coat, not just before recoats.

Washing the deck and staining the same day. Cleaning soaks the boards again. Give them a day or two of dry weather and retest.

Applying too heavily. More stain is not more protection. Whatever does not soak in becomes a soft surface film that scuffs, gets sticky and peels.

Waiting years instead of months. Once the wood passes the test, stain it. A deck left bare for two or three years needs much heavier cleaning and often sanding to remove the degraded gray layer, and it will have more checks and splinters than it would have.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should you wait to stain pressure-treated wood?

Until it is dry, which for standard wet-treated lumber is typically 4–8 weeks in hot, dry summer weather and two to four months or more in cool or humid conditions. A deck built in late autumn is usually best stained the following spring. KDAT (kiln-dried after treatment) lumber is the exception: it can usually be stained within a few days to two weeks of installation, after cleaning. Rather than counting days, use the water-drop test — if water soaks in within about 10 minutes, the wood is ready — or a moisture meter reading of roughly 15% or below.

What happens if you stain pressure-treated wood too soon?

The stain cannot penetrate cells already full of water, so most of it dries as a thin layer on the surface. As the wood dries over the following weeks, moisture pushes up from beneath and the boards shrink, and that layer flakes and peels — often within the first year. Uneven drying also causes blotchy, striped color, and mildew can grow under the trapped film. The fix is stripping, cleaning, drying and starting again.

How do I know if my pressure-treated wood is dry enough to stain?

Sprinkle water on several boards in different locations, including shaded ones. If it soaks in and darkens the wood within about 10 minutes, the wood is ready; if it beads or pools, wait. For a precise answer, a $20–$50 pin moisture meter should read roughly 15% or lower (or the stain label's figure) across several boards, measured away from the board ends. The deck is ready when the slowest-drying area passes.

What does KDAT mean on pressure-treated lumber?

KDAT stands for kiln-dried after treatment. The lumber is pressure-treated with preservative in the usual way, then dried in a kiln to remove the water the treatment added, typically to around 19% moisture content or below. It costs more and is often a special order, but it arrives straighter, has done most of its shrinking, and can be stained shortly after installation. Check the end tag for the designation.

What is the best type of stain for pressure-treated wood?

For most new treated decks, a penetrating semi-transparent stain — oil-based or a quality water-based or hybrid formula. It soaks in rather than forming a film, so it fades instead of peeling, recoats easily, and its pigment blocks the UV that grays wood. Warm tones hide the green tint best. Choose solid stain to hide the wood entirely, accepting that a film can peel.

Do you need to clean new pressure-treated wood before staining?

Yes. New lumber often has a smooth mill glaze from the planer that resists absorption, plus dust, dirt and pencil marks from construction. Scrub with an oxygenated deck cleaner, follow with an oxalic acid brightener to restore the wood's pH and open the surface, and rinse thoroughly. Then let it dry 24–48 hours and retest before staining.

Will stain cover the green color of pressure-treated wood?

Solid stain covers it completely. Semi-transparent stains largely mask it, especially in warm tones such as cedar, redwood or medium brown, because red and brown pigments counteract green visually. Transparent stains and light grays tend to look greenish or murky. Test on a cleaned, dry offcut and judge the color the next day.

Can you wait too long to stain pressure-treated wood?

You can wait longer than ideal without ruining anything, but it costs you prep work. Bare treated wood in sun begins graying within a few months, and after a year or more unprotected it usually has loose gray fibers, checking, splinters and mildew. All of that can be dealt with by more thorough cleaning, brightening and sometimes sanding before staining. The best timing is to stain within the first season — as soon as the wood passes the water-drop test.
