Best Luxury Vinyl Plank Flooring — Matched to the Room, Not the Showroom

Two numbers decide most of what an LVP floor will do in your house: the core type and the wear layer in mils. Get those right for the room, then worry about colour.

The best luxury vinyl plank for most homes is a rigid-core click-lock plank with a 20 mil wear layer and an attached pad — COREtec Plus Enhanced if you want it warmer and quieter underfoot, Shaw Floorte Pro or Pergo Extreme if you want a denser, more dent-resistant SPC core. Below 12 mil is for bedrooms and closets; 20 mil is the sensible floor for kitchens, hallways and pets; 22 mil and above earns its premium only in genuinely hard-wearing spaces.

Everything else on the box — "waterproof", "pet-proof", the total thickness in millimetres — matters far less than those two specs and the condition of the subfloor underneath. A premium plank on an uneven floor will click, gap and break its locks; a mid-priced plank on a flat, dry subfloor will outlast it.

⚡ The quick answer: Choose SPC rigid core for basements, sunrooms and anywhere with temperature swings or heavy furniture. Choose WPC for upstairs living areas where comfort and noise matter. Choose glue-down for large open areas, commercial use or heavy rolling loads. Buy 20 mil for kitchens, hallways and homes with dogs; 12 mil is fine for bedrooms. Budget roughly $2.50–$6 per sq ft for materials and $4.50–$11 per sq ft installed, before subfloor prep.

SPC vs WPC vs Glue-Down

"Luxury vinyl plank" now covers three quite different constructions. They share a printed design film and a clear wear layer on top; the difference is what sits underneath.

ConstructionCoreTypical thicknessStrengthsWeaknesses
SPC (stone-plastic composite)PVC + limestone, dense4 – 8 mmDent resistance, dimensional stability, basementsHard, cold, louder footfall
WPC (wood/wood-plastic composite)Foamed PVC, lighter6 – 9 mmWarmer, quieter, softer underfootDents under point loads, moves more with heat
Flexible / glue-down (dryback)Thin flexible vinyl2 – 3 mmLong runs, rolling loads, cabinets on topNeeds a near-perfect subfloor, pro install

SPC is the default today because it is dense, stable and cost-efficient. Limestone filler makes the core stiff and heavy, which is why SPC planks shrug off chair legs and furniture and why they move very little when the room heats and cools. That stability is the reason to prefer it in basements, sunrooms with good blinds, and anywhere the thermostat gets turned down for weeks. The cost is comfort: SPC is hard underfoot and transmits footfall noise, which is why an attached pad matters more on SPC than on anything else.

WPC uses a foamed core — the "wood" in the name is largely historic, and most modern WPC cores are foamed PVC. The air in the foam makes it lighter, warmer to the touch and quieter, and it feels closer to engineered wood when you walk on it. The same softness lets a narrow heavy load leave a dent, and the core expands and contracts more with temperature. Upstairs living rooms and bedrooms are its natural territory.

Flexible glue-down plank is the original LVT and still what most commercial spaces use. It is thin, fully bonded and does not float, so it never needs the expansion breaks a floating floor requires and it tolerates rolling loads. It is unforgiving of the subfloor — and if you are refinishing an existing wooden floor instead, our guide to the best water-based polyurethane for floors covers that route.

Wear Layer: What the Mils Mean

The wear layer is the transparent film over the printed design, measured in mils — thousandths of an inch, not millimetres. It is the single best predictor of how long the floor will keep looking new, because once grit and traffic abrade through it, the print wears and the damage cannot be repaired except by replacing planks.

Wear layerIn millimetresSuitsAvoid in
6 – 8 mil≈ 0.15 – 0.2 mmClosets, guest rooms, short-term rentals on a budgetAnywhere with daily traffic
12 mil≈ 0.3 mmBedrooms, home offices, low-traffic living roomsKitchens, entries, large dogs
20 mil≈ 0.5 mmKitchens, hallways, family rooms, pets, basementsLittle — the sensible default
22 – 28+ mil≈ 0.55 – 0.7+ mmMudrooms, entries off a garage, light commercial, rentalsBedrooms — you are paying for durability you won't use

Two clarifications matter. First, the wear layer governs abrasion, not dents. A 22 mil plank on a soft WPC core still dents under a fridge foot; a 12 mil plank on dense SPC resists it. Dent resistance comes from the core. Second, the fine scratches that make a floor look tired mostly happen in the factory-applied urethane or ceramic-bead coating on top of the wear layer. Every brand has a version of this coating, and it is why gritty entries benefit from a good walk-off mat more than from any extra mil.

Kitchens need 20 mil: chair legs, dropped utensils and grit carried from the back door. Pets need 20 mil plus trimmed nails, and owners of large, active dogs should consider 22 mil and a lighter, mid-tone colour that disguises surface scuffing better than very dark or very light planks. Basements earn 20 mil if they are a family room or gym, and 12 mil is acceptable for a storage room or guest suite.

Total Thickness and Attached Pads

Total thickness — usually quoted as 5 mm, 6.5 mm, 8 mm and so on — is mostly core and pad. It is not a durability number, and a 9 mm plank with a 12 mil wear layer will wear out sooner than a 6 mm plank with 20 mil. What thickness does buy is a stiffer plank that bridges small subfloor dips, a more solid feel, and better sound behaviour. Above about 8 mm the returns diminish quickly.

Most rigid-core planks now come with an attached pad, typically 1–1.5 mm of IXPE foam or cork. It improves comfort and dampens the hollow sound floating floors are known for. The firm rule: do not add a second underlayment under a padded plank. A soft layer below lets the click joints flex every time someone steps on them, and that flex is what breaks the locking edges and opens gaps. Most manufacturers void the warranty if you do it.

An attached pad is not automatically a vapour barrier. Some manufacturers accept their attached pad as sufficient on concrete; others still require a 6-mil polyethylene sheet underneath. That is a manufacturer-specific instruction, and on a basement slab it is the one to look up before buying.

Waterproof vs Water-Resistant

"Waterproof" on an LVP box means the plank itself does not swell when wet. SPC and WPC cores contain no wood fibre to absorb water, so a spill left overnight, a leaking dishwasher or a wet dog will not damage the plank. That is a genuine advantage over laminate, which is usually marketed as water-resistant: the surface sheds water for a period, but water that reaches the fibreboard core at the edges causes swelling.

What "waterproof" does not mean is that the floor protects the house. A floating floor has joints; standing water seeps through them and around the perimeter to the subfloor below, and a plank floor sitting over a wet subfloor can trap moisture and grow mould. After a real leak, floating LVP is often unclicked, dried out and reinstalled — a nice advantage — but the subfloor needs to dry first. For bathrooms, seal the expansion gap around tubs and toilets with 100% silicone, and treat the planks as protecting themselves, not your joists.

Installation: Subfloor, Acclimation, Gaps

Click-lock floating installation is why LVP has become the most popular DIY floor. Planks lock together on their long and short edges and the whole floor floats over the subfloor, held down by its own weight and the trim around the edge. A reasonably handy homeowner can do a bedroom in a day with a tape, a utility knife, a tapping block and a pull bar.

Subfloor Flatness

The tolerance most rigid-core manufacturers publish is in the region of 3/16 inch over 10 feet (some also specify 1/8 inch over 6 feet). Lay a long straightedge across the floor in several directions and slide shims under the gaps. Dips get filled with patching compound or self-leveller; humps on concrete get ground and on wood get planed or sanded. A floor that is out of tolerance produces the most common LVP complaints: planks that click when walked on, joints that peak or open, and broken locking tongues. Glue-down planks need it flatter and smoother still.

Acclimation

Requirements vary. Many SPC lines say little or no acclimation is needed; WPC and glue-down products more often specify 24–48 hours in the room, boxes laid flat, at normal living temperature. The safe default is to bring the boxes into the room two days ahead with heating or cooling running as normal. Planks installed cold from a garage in winter expand when the house warms them, which is exactly the movement the expansion gap is supposed to absorb rather than add to.

Expansion Gaps and Transitions

Leave about 1/4 inch at every wall, pipe, door jamb and fixed object — more if the manufacturer says so. Baseboard or quarter round covers it. Undercut door casings so the plank slides beneath rather than being butted against them. Most floating floors also need a T-moulding transition at doorways and once a run exceeds the manufacturer's maximum, which on many lines is in the range of 40–50 feet in one direction.

Never pin a floating floor. Kitchen islands, built-in cabinets and heavy fixed shelving should sit on the subfloor with the plank cut around them, not on top of it. A floor that cannot move will buckle somewhere else, usually in the middle of a room in the first summer.

💡 Three checks before you buy a single box. What is the wear layer in mils for this exact colour? Does the manufacturer require a separate vapour barrier on concrete with this product? What is the flatness tolerance and maximum run before a transition? If the retailer or installer cannot answer all three from the spec sheet, read the installation guide yourself before ordering.

Basements and Concrete Slabs

LVP is the best mainstream finished floor for basements, because it is the only common option that does not mind occasional damp. But a waterproof plank on a wet slab is still a problem floor: moisture moving up through the concrete condenses under the floor, the subfloor stays damp, and mould and musty smells follow even when the planks look perfect.

Fix water first. Seepage through walls, wet corners after rain or efflorescence on block need dealing with before the floor goes in; our guide on how to waterproof basement walls covers the sequence, and a masonry sealer for basement walls is part of it on block foundations.

Then test the slab. The quick check is to tape a two-foot square of plastic sheet down on all four edges and leave it for 24–48 hours. Damp underneath means vapour is coming through. For a definitive number, manufacturers quote limits from in-slab relative humidity probes (ASTM F2170) or calcium chloride tests (ASTM F1869). Many floating LVP lines accept slabs up to roughly 85% RH, but the figure varies — check before relying on it.

Use the right barrier. On concrete, a 6-mil polyethylene sheet, seams overlapped and taped and turned up the wall behind the trim, is the usual requirement unless the manufacturer accepts its attached pad instead. Do not substitute a foam underlayment for a barrier on top of a padded plank.

Control humidity. A basement held at 45–55% relative humidity is better for the floor, the drywall and everything stored down there. A dedicated basement dehumidifier is the cheapest insurance in the whole finished-basement budget. Choose SPC over WPC for its dimensional stability. If the space is really a workshop or garage-style utility room, a coating is the better finish — compare options in our garage floor coating guide, or for a low-cost painted finish see the best concrete floor paint.

AC Ratings, Use Classes and VOCs

AC ratings are a laminate standard, not a vinyl one. AC1 to AC5 come from the abrasion and impact tests in EN 13329, the European laminate specification. You will occasionally see an AC rating on a vinyl listing, but it is borrowed terminology; for LVP the meaningful durability numbers are the wear layer in mils and the use class.

Use classes come from the European resilient flooring classification (EN ISO 10874): a 2 prefix is domestic and a 3 prefix is commercial, with the second digit indicating traffic intensity. Class 23 means heavy domestic use; class 33 means heavy commercial; class 32 or 33 on a residential plank suggests a robust wear layer. In North America, warranties are a practical proxy: a "lifetime residential, 10–15 years light commercial" warranty usually signals a 20 mil-class product.

Indoor air quality is the other thing to check. Look for FloorScore certification, administered by SCS Global Services, which shows the product meets the California Department of Public Health emission limits for VOCs. Major brands have moved to phthalate-free plasticisers in the last decade, and most now say so on the spec sheet. Expect a mild smell from newly unboxed planks; ventilate for a few days after installation, especially with glue-down adhesives.

The Short Version

Pick the core for the space: SPC for basements, sunrooms and heavy furniture; WPC for upstairs comfort; glue-down for large open plans, commercial use and kitchens with heavy islands.

Pick the wear layer for the traffic: 12 mil for bedrooms, 20 mil for kitchens, hallways, basements and anything with paws, 22 mil and above for entries and rentals.

Then spend the effort where floors actually fail: a subfloor flat to 3/16 inch in 10 feet, the right vapour barrier on concrete, a 1/4 inch gap at every wall, and nothing heavy pinning the floor. COREtec Plus Enhanced is the best all-rounder, LifeProof's 22 mil planks are the best value, and Shaw Floorte Pro is the pick for a basement slab.

Frequently Asked Questions

What wear layer do I need for luxury vinyl plank?

Match it to the traffic. A 12 mil wear layer is fine for bedrooms, home offices and other low-traffic rooms. A 20 mil wear layer is the sensible default for kitchens, hallways, family rooms, basements and any home with dogs, and it typically carries a light-commercial warranty as well as a residential one. 22 mil and above is worth paying for in mudrooms, entries from a garage, rentals and light commercial spaces. Below 12 mil — the 6 and 8 mil products — belongs only in closets and spare rooms. Remember that the wear layer governs abrasion; dent resistance comes from the core.

Is SPC or WPC better?

Neither across the board. SPC has a dense limestone-filled core that resists dents and stays dimensionally stable through temperature changes, which makes it the better choice for basements, sunny rooms, heavy furniture and home gyms. WPC has a foamed core that is warmer, quieter and softer underfoot, which suits upstairs living rooms and bedrooms where comfort matters. The trade-off is that WPC dents more readily under heavy point loads and moves more with heat. If you are unsure, SPC with an attached pad is the safer default; if comfort is the priority and the room is temperature-stable, WPC is more pleasant to live on.

Can you put luxury vinyl plank in a basement?

Yes — it is the best mainstream finished floor for basements, because a vinyl core does not swell when damp. But fix any water entry first and test the slab: tape plastic sheet down for 24–48 hours, or use the RH or calcium chloride test the manufacturer specifies. Install a 6-mil polyethylene vapour barrier on the concrete unless the manufacturer accepts its attached pad instead, choose SPC for stability, and keep the space at roughly 45–55% relative humidity with a dehumidifier. A waterproof plank over a slab pushing moisture through still traps damp underneath, which leads to mould and odours.

Is waterproof vinyl plank really waterproof?

The plank is; the floor is not. SPC and WPC cores contain no wood fibre, so spills, pet accidents and a leaking appliance will not swell or delaminate the planks. But a floating floor has joints and an expansion gap around the edge, and standing water will find its way through to the subfloor underneath. After a significant leak, click-lock planks can often be lifted, the subfloor dried, and the floor reinstalled. In bathrooms, seal the perimeter gap around the tub and toilet with silicone. Laminate, by contrast, is usually water-resistant: its fibreboard core swells if water reaches the edges.

Do I need underlayment under vinyl plank with an attached pad?

No, and adding one usually causes problems. A second soft layer lets the click joints flex under foot traffic, which breaks the locking edges and opens gaps, and most manufacturers void the warranty if you add extra cushioning. The exception is a vapour barrier: on concrete, many manufacturers still require a thin 6-mil polyethylene sheet under a padded plank, because the pad itself is not rated as a moisture barrier. That is not cushioning, and it does not cause the flex problem. Check the installation guide for your specific collection.

How flat does the subfloor need to be for LVP?

Most rigid-core manufacturers specify within about 3/16 inch over 10 feet, and some add 1/8 inch over 6 feet. Check with a long straightedge in several directions. Fill dips with floor patch or self-levelling compound, and grind or sand high spots. Glue-down vinyl needs a flatter, smoother surface still, because thin planks telegraph every ridge and nail head. An out-of-tolerance subfloor is the most common cause of LVP problems: clicking underfoot, peaked or gapping joints and broken locks — none of which are covered by warranty when the floor is outside the stated tolerance.

How much does luxury vinyl plank cost per square foot?

Materials run roughly $1.50–$3 per square foot for entry SPC with a 6–12 mil wear layer, $3–$5 for mid-range 20–22 mil rigid core with an attached pad, and $4.50–$7 for premium lines. Professional click-lock installation adds about $1.50–$4 per square foot, bringing installed costs to roughly $3.50–$11. Commercial glue-down runs $7–$12 or more installed. Budget separately for old floor removal, levelling compound, trim, transitions and stair nosing, and order 10% extra for waste. The mid-range 20 mil category is where most homeowners get the best value.

Does LVP have an AC rating?

Not properly. AC ratings (AC1–AC5) come from EN 13329, the European standard for laminate flooring, and describe abrasion and impact resistance for laminate's melamine surface. When an AC rating appears on a vinyl listing, it is borrowed terminology. For LVP, judge durability by the wear layer in mils, the EN ISO 10874 use class (23 is heavy domestic, 33 heavy commercial), and the warranty — a light-commercial warranty usually indicates a 20 mil-class product. For air quality, look for FloorScore certification and a stated phthalate-free formulation.
