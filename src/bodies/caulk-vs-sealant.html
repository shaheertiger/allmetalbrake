Caulk vs Sealant — The Difference Is How Much the Joint Moves

The shelf uses both words for the same tubes. What actually separates them is movement capability: how far the cured bead can stretch and squeeze, year after year, without tearing or letting go.

In the store, "caulk" and "sealant" are used almost interchangeably — the same aisle, the same 10-ounce cartridges, and labels that say "caulk," "sealant" or both. In practice, though, the words point at two different kinds of product. What people usually mean by caulk is a rigid or low-movement filler, typically acrylic latex, made to close gaps that barely move and then be painted. What people mean by sealant is an elastomeric material — silicone, polyurethane or a hybrid polymer — engineered to stretch and compress with a joint that opens and closes every day.

So the useful question is not what the tube calls itself but how much the joint will move and whether it needs paint. Interior trim gaps that never move take a cheap paintable caulk. Anything outdoors where two different materials meet — windows, siding, concrete, roofs, gutters — needs a true sealant, ideally one rated to ASTM C920.

⚡ The direct answer: "Caulk" generally means an acrylic latex or siliconized acrylic filler with low movement capability (often around ±5–12.5%), cheap, paintable and best indoors. "Sealant" generally means an elastomeric silicone, polyurethane or hybrid product rated to ASTM C920 at ±25%, ±35% or ±50% movement. Use caulk for interior trim, baseboards and drywall-to-wood gaps. Use sealant for exterior windows, siding, concrete joints, tubs and showers, gutters and roof flashing. The fastest check: if the tube lists ASTM C920 Class 25 or higher, it is a sealant no matter what the front says.

Why the Words Overlap

There is no legal rule about which word goes on a cartridge, and manufacturers use whichever sells. A water-based acrylic is often marketed as "sealant" to sound more capable, and a high-performance silicone is regularly shelved as "kitchen and bath caulk." "Caulk" also works as a verb: you caulk a joint, whatever you fill it with. That is why so many arguments about the difference go nowhere — people are comparing labels rather than materials.

The construction industry's technical vocabulary is more precise. In the standards world, "sealant" is the general term for a material that seals a joint and accommodates movement, and the older word "caulk" is associated with low-performance compounds — historically oil- or resin-based putties — used where little movement occurs. Commercial specifications almost never say "caulk"; they call for a sealant meeting a specific standard and movement class.

For a homeowner, the practical version of that distinction is simple. A tube that meets ASTM C834 (the standard for latex sealants) is what most people mean by caulk. A tube that meets ASTM C920 (the standard for elastomeric joint sealants) is what most people mean by sealant. Plenty of products carry neither standard, and those are the ones to be most cautious about outdoors. The deeper split between the two common chemistries is covered in our silicone vs latex caulk comparison.

The Real Difference: Movement Capability

Every joint on a house moves. Wood swells and shrinks with humidity. Aluminium and vinyl window frames expand and contract with temperature — vinyl especially, at several times the rate of wood. Concrete slabs shrink as they cure and then cycle with every season. A joint between two different materials moves the most, because each side moves at its own rate.

Movement capability is the percentage of the joint's width that a cured bead can repeatedly stretch and compress without tearing internally (cohesive failure) or peeling off one side (adhesive failure). It is not the same as the "elongation" figures some packaging advertises. A product may stretch 400% once before snapping in a lab pull; that says little about how it behaves after thousands of cycles, years of UV and a few freeze-thaw winters. Movement class is measured with cyclic testing on cured, aged samples, which is why it is the number worth trusting.

Here is what the percentages mean in real dimensions. A 1/2-inch joint sealed with a ±25% product can open or close by 1/8 inch either way and survive. The same joint with a ±50% product can move 1/4 inch either way. An acrylic latex caulk rated at roughly ±7.5% can handle under 1/25 of an inch on that joint — fine for a mitred casing indoors, not enough for the seam where vinyl siding meets a window frame in full sun.

That gap is the whole story. Low-movement caulk on a high-movement joint does not fail on day one. It looks perfect for a season, then hairline cracks appear along one edge, water gets behind the bead, and the bead begins to work loose. People blame the brand; the real cause is the movement mismatch.

Reading an ASTM C920 Rating

ASTM C920 is the standard most professional sealants are specified against, and the rating code on the back of a cartridge tells you more than anything on the front. A typical line reads something like "ASTM C920, Type S, Grade NS, Class 25, Use NT, M, A, G, O." Each piece means something.

What the Classes Mean in Practice

Class 12.5 is the bottom rung of C920 and roughly where the best acrylic caulks sit. It suits joints with little thermal movement: interior work, wood-to-wood trim outside on a shaded wall, nail holes in painted siding.

Class 25 is the practical minimum for most exterior joints — window and door perimeters, siding butt joints and corner boards, penetrations through the wall. Most mid-grade polyurethanes, hybrids and many premium elastomeric acrylics land here.

Class 35 adds headroom for joints between dissimilar materials in direct sun, or for longer runs where the substrate moves more. Several hybrid polymer sealants carry it.

Class 50 and Class 100/50 are mostly silicones and some high-end hybrids. They are what you want on dark-coloured vinyl or metal, on south- and west-facing walls, and on any joint where you have seen lesser products fail before.

If a tube carries no C920 class at all, assume it is not built for meaningful movement. That is not automatically a problem indoors, but outdoors it is the single most common reason a bead fails within a few years.

Acrylic latex is water-based, cleans up with a damp rag, tools beautifully and takes paint within an hour or two for fast formulas. It also shrinks as the water evaporates, so deep gaps need two passes, and it hardens and cracks with age and sun. "Siliconized" acrylic adds a little silicone for flexibility and adhesion; it remains, fundamentally, a latex caulk.

Polyurethane grips concrete, masonry and wood tenaciously, resists abrasion well enough for traffic joints, and is paintable once cured. It is sticky and unforgiving to tool, needs mineral spirits for cleanup, and its surface chalks under long UV exposure unless painted.

Hybrid polymers — silyl-terminated polyether or polyurethane, often called MS polymer — cure like polyurethane but with better UV stability, no isocyanates, low odour, and the ability to bond to slightly damp surfaces. Many are paintable within hours. They are the closest thing to an all-rounder for exterior work and cost the most per tube.

Silicone has the best movement capability and the best UV and weather resistance of anything on the shelf. Its drawbacks are equally specific: paint will not stick to it, it has a narrower range of substrates it bonds to without primer, and its residue contaminates a surface so that almost nothing else will adhere afterward.

Butyl rubber stays tacky and sticks hard to metal, which is why it shows up in gutter and flashing work, but it moves poorly, is stringy to apply, and does not take paint well.

Paintability and Adhesion

Paint

Paintability is the second deciding question after movement. Acrylic caulk takes paint fastest and best — the reason painters use it for every interior gap. Polyurethane and hybrids take paint well, though polyurethanes generally want to cure first and some hybrids specify a skin time before coating. Silicone does not take paint at all; latex paint beads up on it and peels off in strips. Products labelled "paintable silicone" exist, but most are actually acrylic or hybrid formulas with some silicone content rather than 100% silicone.

There is a subtler issue too. Paint is far less flexible than an elastomeric sealant. On a joint that moves a lot, a rigid paint film over a stretchy bead will crack — often in a crazed, alligator pattern — even while the sealant underneath is fine. Use a 100% acrylic latex paint over sealant and, on high-movement joints, consider leaving the bead in a colour-matched sealant rather than painting it.

Adhesion

A sealant is only as good as its bond to both sides of the joint. Polyurethane and hybrids have the broadest adhesion and bond well to concrete, masonry, wood, fibre cement, aluminium and most vinyls. Silicone bonds superbly to glass, glazed tile, porcelain and metals, and less reliably to porous masonry and some plastics without a primer. Acrylic latex sticks well to painted wood and drywall and weakly to non-porous surfaces such as glass, metal and vinyl.

Two practical notes. First, acetoxy-cure silicone (the vinegar-smelling kind) releases acetic acid as it cures, which can corrode bare metal and mark natural stone and some masonry; for those, use a neutral-cure silicone. Second, adhesion depends far more on preparation than on the product: old caulk fully removed, surfaces clean, dry and free of dust, and silicone residue removed with a solvent rather than just scraped.

Indoor vs Outdoor

Indoors, temperature is stable, there is no UV, and most joints barely move. That is why inexpensive acrylic caulk works so well for trim, baseboards, crown moulding, window casings and the gap between countertop backsplash and drywall. The exceptions indoors are wet areas — tubs, showers, sinks — where constant water and mould favour silicone, and any joint against a floor or wall that is known to move seasonally.

Outdoors, everything works against a low-movement caulk. Sun heats dark siding and frames well above air temperature, then night cools them; UV breaks down acrylic binders; rain and freeze-thaw push water into any crack. A bead on a south-facing wall may cycle through more movement in one summer week than an indoor joint sees in a decade. This is where a C920 sealant pays for itself. Our best exterior caulk guide sorts the outdoor options by exactly this kind of joint.

Application conditions differ too. Water-based caulks generally need temperatures of about 40°F and rising, with no rain for several hours, and they freeze in the tube. Many silicones can be applied well below freezing, and some polyurethanes and hybrids can go on damp surfaces or tolerate rain within an hour. Cure times vary just as much — see how long caulk takes to dry for the real numbers by chemistry.

Interior Trim Gaps

The classic caulk job. Gaps between casing and wall, baseboard and drywall, and mitred joints that have opened slightly are all low-movement, need paint, and benefit from a product that tools smoothly and cleans up with water. A painter's-grade acrylic or siliconized acrylic is the right choice. Spending more on a C920 sealant here buys nothing you will notice.

Exterior Windows and Doors

The perimeter between a window frame and the siding or trim is one of the hardest-working joints on the house: two materials, often vinyl against wood or fibre cement, in direct sun, at the exact point water tries to get in. Use a C920 Class 25 or better sealant — hybrid if you want to paint it, silicone if you do not. Leave weep holes open and do not seal the gap above head flashing, which is designed to drain. Our step-by-step guide to caulking exterior windows covers removal, backer rod and tooling in detail.

Siding

Butt joints in fibre cement and wood siding, corner boards, and trim-to-siding joints all move moderately and almost always get painted. A paintable C920 Class 25 product — a hybrid or a high-performance elastomeric acrylic — is the standard. Do not caulk the horizontal laps where one board overlaps the next; those gaps let water that gets behind the siding drain out, and sealing them traps it. Vinyl siding should generally not be caulked at all where it meets J-channel, since it needs to slide freely.

Concrete Joints

Driveway and sidewalk expansion joints, the gap between a slab and the foundation, and control joints that have opened all move substantially and take traffic. Use a self-levelling polyurethane or silicone rated C920 Grade P and Use T, installed over backer rod. Non-sag grades work on vertical faces such as the slab-to-wall joint. Random cracks across a slab are a different problem — see our best concrete crack filler guide for those.

Tubs and Showers

The joint between a tub or shower pan and the tile is a change-of-plane joint that moves every time someone steps in. It should be sealant, not grout. A 100% silicone with mildewcide is the usual choice because it bonds to glazed tile and acrylic or enamel, tolerates constant water and resists mould. Fill the tub with water before sealing so the joint is at its widest when the sealant cures. Seal the grout itself separately with a penetrating product — our best grout sealer guide covers that half of the job.

Gutters

Gutter seams, end caps and outlet joints sit in standing water and debris, on metal that expands and contracts with temperature. Purpose-made gutter sealants — typically butyl-based or hybrid formulas — stick to aluminium and steel and tolerate permanent wetting. Clean the seam to bare, dry metal first. General-purpose acrylic caulk fails here quickly, and silicone, while durable, can be hard to bond reliably to painted or oxidised gutters.

Roofs

Flashing edges, plumbing vent boots, exposed nail heads and skylight perimeters see the most heat and UV on the house, often with surface temperatures well above 150°F in summer. A polyurethane or hybrid roofing sealant is the usual choice; asphalt roofing cement is cheap but hardens and cracks over time and is better treated as a patch than a long-term sealant. Sealant on a roof is a backup to properly lapped flashing, never a substitute for it.

💡 The two-question rule. Does the joint move — outdoors, between different materials, or under traffic? Does it need paint? Moves and needs paint: hybrid or polyurethane. Moves, no paint: silicone. Barely moves and needs paint: acrylic latex caulk. Those two answers settle the product choice for almost every joint on a house.

Joint Design: The 2:1 Rule and Backer Rod

Even the best sealant fails in a badly shaped joint, and this is where most DIY exterior work goes wrong. The bead's movement rating assumes it is installed in a joint of sensible proportions, bonded to two sides only.

Width to Depth

The general guidance for elastomeric sealants is a width-to-depth ratio of about 2:1, with a minimum depth of around 1/4 inch and a maximum of about 1/2 inch. A 1/2-inch-wide joint should be about 1/4 inch deep; a 3/4-inch joint about 3/8 inch deep. For joints wider than an inch, depth is typically capped at 1/2 inch.

The reason is geometry. A thin, wide bead can stretch like a rubber band. A deep, narrow plug cannot — when the joint opens, the stress concentrates at the bond lines and the sealant tears away from the sides. Tooled properly, the finished bead has a slight hourglass profile, thinnest in the middle, which lets it flex without overloading the edges.

Backer Rod and Bond Breakers

Backer rod is a round foam cord pushed into the joint to set the sealant depth and give it something to tool against. It also prevents three-sided adhesion: if sealant bonds to the back of the joint as well as both sides, it cannot stretch, and it tears at the first significant movement.

  • Size: choose a rod about 25% wider than the joint so it stays in by compression — roughly 5/8-inch rod for a 1/2-inch joint.
  • Closed-cell rod is standard for most exterior and concrete joints. Do not puncture it; damaged cells can outgas and cause bubbles in the sealant.
  • Open-cell or bi-cellular rod compresses more easily into irregular joints and lets moisture-cure sealants cure from behind, at the cost of absorbing water if left exposed.
  • Bond-breaker tape replaces rod in joints too shallow for it — the bottom of the joint gets tape, so sealant bonds only to the sides.

Joints narrower than about 1/4 inch are too small to accommodate meaningful movement no matter what you fill them with, and gaps wider than about an inch usually need trim, flashing or a repair rather than a huge sealant bead.

How Long Each Lasts

Lifespans on cartridges are warranties, not predictions, and "35-year" or "lifetime" claims on acrylic caulk describe ideal indoor conditions. Outdoors, realistic service life depends on chemistry, sun exposure, joint design and preparation in roughly that order.

  • Acrylic latex: 2–5 years outdoors before cracking and shrinkage show, often 10+ years indoors where nothing moves.
  • Siliconized acrylic: about 5–10 years outdoors on low-movement, painted joints.
  • Polyurethane: 10–20 years, longer when painted to shield it from UV.
  • Hybrid polymer: 15–20+ years, with good UV resistance even unpainted.
  • Silicone: 20 years or more outdoors; indoors in showers it is usually mould or soap-scum discolouration, not failure, that prompts replacement after 5–10 years.

Cost per year makes the choice clearer. A $5 acrylic tube that needs redoing every three years on a window costs more in time — removing and replacing the bead — than a $12 hybrid that lasts fifteen. On a whole house, the labour of recaulking dwarfs the price difference between products, which is why buying the cheap tube for exterior work is false economy.

Mistakes That Cause Failures

Using interior caulk outside. The most common error, and usually invisible for the first year. Check the label for exterior rating and, ideally, a C920 class.

Caulking over old caulk. New sealant bonds to the old bead, not to the substrate, so it fails as soon as the old bead does. Remove it fully. Silicone in particular must be cut out and the residue cleaned with a solvent.

Ignoring joint depth. Filling a 1-inch-deep gap with sealant wastes material and creates a plug that cannot flex. Use backer rod.

Sealing gaps meant to drain. Siding laps, window weep holes, the gap above head flashing and the bottom edge of trim over flashing all exist to let water out. Sealing them traps moisture behind the cladding.

Painting silicone, or painting too soon. Paint will not hold on silicone, and painting a moisture-cure sealant before it is ready can leave the paint tacky or wrinkled.

Applying in the wrong conditions. Too cold, on a wet surface, or in direct midday sun on dark siding when the joint is at its narrowest. Mild, dry, overcast days give the best results.

The Short Version

Treat "caulk" and "sealant" as descriptions of performance, not labels. Caulk — acrylic latex or siliconized acrylic — is the right material for interior gaps that barely move and need paint. Sealant — silicone, polyurethane or hybrid, rated ASTM C920 Class 25 or higher — is the right material for anything outdoors, anything wet, and anything under traffic.

When in doubt, turn the tube over. A C920 class tells you how far the bead will move; a chemistry tells you whether it takes paint. Combine that with a properly sized joint, backer rod and clean surfaces, and the bead will outlast the paint around it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is caulk the same as sealant?

In retail, the words are used almost interchangeably, so the label alone tells you little. Technically, "caulk" usually refers to low-movement, rigid or semi-rigid fillers such as acrylic latex, meant for gaps that barely move and need paint. "Sealant" refers to elastomeric materials — silicone, polyurethane and hybrid polymers — designed to stretch and compress with a moving joint. The reliable way to tell is the back label: a product rated to ASTM C920 at Class 25 or higher is a true sealant, while one meeting only ASTM C834 is a latex caulk, whatever the front says.

Can I use caulk instead of sealant outside?

Only on low-movement, painted joints, such as wood-to-wood trim on a shaded wall or nail holes in siding. Exterior acrylic caulk handles roughly ±5–12.5% movement, which is not enough for window perimeters, joints between dissimilar materials, concrete or anything in strong sun. It will usually look fine for a season, then crack along an edge and let water behind it. For most exterior joints, use a sealant rated ASTM C920 Class 25 or higher — hybrid or polyurethane if you want to paint it, silicone if you do not.

What does ASTM C920 Class 25 mean?

It means the cured sealant can repeatedly stretch and compress by 25% of the joint's width without failing. On a 1/2-inch joint, that is 1/8 inch of opening or closing either way. Classes run 12.5, 25, 35, 50 and 100/50 (plus 100%, minus 50%). The same rating line also gives Type (S for single-component, M for multi-component), Grade (NS for non-sag, P for pourable), and Use codes such as T for traffic, NT for non-traffic, I for immersion, and substrates like M for masonry, G for glass and A for aluminium.

Which is better for a shower, caulk or sealant?

Sealant — specifically a 100% silicone with mildewcide, usually sold as kitchen and bath silicone. The joint between tub and tile moves every time the tub is loaded, is constantly wet, and sits against non-porous glazed surfaces where acrylic caulk bonds weakly. Silicone resists water, mould and movement far better. Fill the tub with water before sealing so the joint is at its widest when the bead cures, and seal the grout separately with a penetrating sealer. The one trade-off is that silicone cannot be painted, which rarely matters in a bathroom.

Can you paint over sealant?

It depends on the chemistry. Acrylic caulk takes paint quickly and easily. Polyurethane and hybrid polymer sealants are paintable, though polyurethane generally needs to cure first and each product lists its own wait time. 100% silicone cannot be painted — paint beads up and peels off. On joints that move a lot, even a paintable sealant can cause paint to crack, because the paint film is less flexible than the bead beneath it; use a 100% acrylic latex paint and accept that high-movement joints may show fine cracks in the paint over time.

What should I use to seal concrete expansion joints?

A self-levelling polyurethane or silicone sealant rated ASTM C920 Grade P (pourable) and Use T (traffic), installed over closed-cell backer rod. The rod sets the depth at roughly half the joint width, with about 1/4 to 1/2 inch of sealant, and prevents three-sided adhesion so the bead can stretch as the slab moves. Clean the joint of old filler, dirt and loose concrete, let it dry, and apply in mild weather. Acrylic caulk and asphalt-based fillers do not survive the movement and traffic of a driveway joint for long.

Why does my caulk keep cracking?

Almost always because the joint moves more than the product can handle, the joint is the wrong shape, or the bead was applied over old caulk. A low-movement acrylic on an exterior joint between dissimilar materials will crack within a season or two. A deep, narrow joint filled solid with sealant cannot flex and tears at the edges. A bead bonded to the back of the joint as well as both sides — three-sided adhesion — also tears. Remove the old material, use backer rod to get a 2:1 width-to-depth profile, and switch to a C920 Class 25 or higher sealant.

How long does sealant last compared to caulk?

Outdoors, acrylic latex caulk typically lasts 2–5 years and siliconized acrylic 5–10 years before cracking or shrinking. Polyurethane sealant lasts 10–20 years, hybrid polymer 15–20 years or more, and silicone 20 years or longer. Indoors, where joints barely move and there is no UV, acrylic caulk can last a decade or more. Real-world life depends heavily on sun exposure, joint design and surface preparation, so a premium sealant in a badly prepared joint can still fail early, while lifetime warranties on acrylic caulk reflect ideal conditions rather than a south-facing wall.
