Best Water-Based Polyurethane for Floors

Modern two-component waterborne finishes outlast oil-based polyurethane and never amber. Six tested on white oak and maple for abrasion, chemical resistance and colour stability.

Water-based polyurethane has quietly taken over hardwood floor finishing, and the reason is not environmental — it is that oil-based polyurethane ambers. An oil finish over white oak or maple looks warm and honey-toned the day it goes down and progressively more orange over the following decade. Water-based finishes stay clear, which is why every pale, natural-looking floor you have seen in the last ten years is finished with one.

The old objection to water-based poly was durability, and on modern two-component formulations that objection no longer holds. A commercial-grade waterborne finish outlasts oil-based polyurethane in abrasion testing. What has not changed is that it is harder to apply.

We tested six water-based floor finishes on white oak and maple panels, running Taber abrasion cycles, chemical spot tests, scratch and dent resistance, and colour measurement under UV exposure over eighteen months.

⚡ Quick Answer: Bona Traffic HD is the most durable water-based floor finish available and the right choice for a main living area or anywhere with dogs and children. For a DIY refinish at a lower price, General Finishes High Performance is excellent on furniture and light-traffic floors. Varathane Water-Based Floor Finish is the widely available budget option and is genuinely adequate for a bedroom. Apply all of them with a microfibre pad on a pole, not a brush.

Why Water-Based, and What You Give Up

What You Gain

  • It stays clear. No ambering, ever. On white oak, maple, ash, and any whitewashed or bleached floor, this is the decisive advantage — an oil finish will visibly yellow those woods within a few years.
  • It dries fast. Two to four hours between coats, so three coats can go down in a day rather than three days.
  • Low odour and low VOC. You can occupy the house, which matters enormously in a lived-in home.
  • Modern durability. Two-component commercial formulations outperform oil-based polyurethane in abrasion testing.
  • Water cleanup.

What You Give Up

  • Ease of application. Open time is roughly 60 to 120 seconds. It does not level itself the way oil does, so lap marks and applicator texture are permanent.
  • Warmth. The clarity that prevents ambering also means it does not add the golden depth that oil gives to red oak and pine. Some floors look better with that warmth.
  • Grain raise. Water swells wood fibres, so the first coat raises the grain and needs sanding back.
  • Cost. A commercial two-component finish costs several times what a can of oil-based poly does.
🌡 Oil-Modified Water-Based Polyurethane: A hybrid category worth knowing about — waterborne finishes with oil-modified resins that add a little of the warmth of an oil finish while keeping fast drying and low odour. They amber slightly less than true oil-based poly but more than a pure waterborne. A reasonable middle ground on red oak, and unnecessary on maple or white oak where clarity is the point.

One practical consequence deserves flagging early: waterborne finishes level differently from oil-based ones and are far less forgiving of the wrong applicator. Natural bristle swells and drags in a waterborne, so the applicator is a real decision rather than an afterthought — we work through it in our guide to the best brushes for water-based polyurethane.

The Best Water-Based Floor Finishes

1
TWO-COMPONENT WATERBORNE POLYURETHANE
Bona Traffic HD
⭐ Best Overall — Commercial Grade Durability

Traffic HD is the benchmark, and every professional refinisher we know reaches for it on jobs that have to last. It is a two-component waterborne polyurethane — a crosslinker is mixed in before use — and the crosslinking is what produces a film substantially harder than any single-component finish.

In our Taber abrasion testing it lost less film than any other product by a clear margin, and it was the only finish that showed no visible scuffing after simulated furniture drag. It is rated for commercial use, which in practice means a residential floor finished with it will look good for a decade rather than five years.

Chemical resistance was equally strong: red wine, coffee, vinegar, and a common household cleaner all wiped clean after 24 hours with no mark. On maple, the colour measurement showed effectively zero shift over 18 months of UV exposure.

It cures fast enough to walk on in hours and reaches full hardness in a few days rather than the weeks an oil-based finish takes, which for a family reoccupying a house is a genuine benefit.

The costs are price and process. It is expensive, the two components must be mixed and used within a pot life, and it is generally sold through flooring distributors rather than general retail. It is also unforgiving of application error — this is a finish that rewards a pad, a plan, and a helper.

✓ Pros

  • Most durable water-based finish tested by a clear margin
  • Commercial rated — a decade of residential life
  • Excellent chemical resistance
  • Zero colour shift over 18 months UV
  • Fast cure to full hardness

✗ Cons

  • Two-component mixing with a limited pot life

Bottom line: The right choice for main living areas, hallways, kitchens, and any home with dogs or children. If you are refinishing a whole floor, this is where the money should go.
2
SINGLE-COMPONENT WATERBORNE POLYURETHANE
General Finishes High Performance
💰 Best Value — Furniture and Light-Traffic Floors

High Performance is the finish most woodworkers reach for, and it deserves the reputation. It is a single-component waterborne polyurethane that flows and levels noticeably better than most in its class, which makes it far more forgiving to apply than its durability would suggest.

Abrasion resistance placed it clearly second to Traffic HD and comfortably ahead of the consumer-grade products. On a bedroom, a study, or a low-traffic living room it will hold up for years. In a hallway or a kitchen it will show wear sooner than a two-component finish.

It is genuinely excellent on furniture — tabletops, cabinets, built-ins — where its levelling and clarity are more valuable than commercial abrasion ratings.

Colour stability was very good with no visible ambering over the test period. Chemical resistance was good, with only the household cleaner leaving a faint mark at 24 hours.

Available in flat, satin, semi-gloss, and gloss, and widely stocked by woodworking suppliers and online. Satin is the most forgiving sheen on a floor, since gloss shows every imperfection in the substrate.

✓ Pros

  • Best levelling and flow of anything tested — forgiving to apply
  • Strong durability for a single-component finish
  • Excellent on furniture as well as floors
  • Very good colour stability
  • Four sheens available

✗ Cons

  • Not commercial-grade — will wear sooner in a hallway or kitchen
  • More expensive than big-box consumer finishes
  • Not stocked in general retail
Bottom line: The best all-round choice for a DIY refinish of a bedroom or study, and the finish to use on furniture regardless.
3
SINGLE-COMPONENT WATERBORNE POLYURETHANE
Varathane Water-Based Floor Finish
🏪 Best Budget — Widely Available

Varathane's water-based floor finish is the product most people find first, because it is stocked in every home centre, and it is a genuinely reasonable choice within its limits.

Durability placed it clearly behind the two products above — visible wear appeared in our simulated traffic testing considerably earlier — but it is formulated for floors rather than general use, and on a bedroom or a guest room it will do the job for several years.

It dries fast, often recoatable in two hours, which makes a three-coat day realistic. Colour stability was good with no meaningful ambering.

Where it is weakest is levelling. It sets quickly and holds applicator texture more readily than the General Finishes product, so it demands a pad, a wet edge, and no going back over set finish.

For a rental, a spare room, or a first attempt at refinishing where the budget matters more than the decade, it is a sensible purchase.

✓ Pros

  • Available in every home centre at a low price
  • Formulated specifically for floors
  • Fast recoat — three coats in a day
  • Good colour stability

✗ Cons

  • Noticeably less durable than the two products above
  • Sets fast and holds applicator texture
  • Will need recoating sooner in traffic areas
Bottom line: Fine for bedrooms, guest rooms, and rentals. Not the choice for a hallway, kitchen, or a floor you want to leave alone for a decade.

Choosing a Sheen

Sheen affects perceived durability more than actual durability, and the difference matters on a floor.

Gloss reflects light, which makes every scratch, dust nib, and imperfection in the substrate visible. It also shows footprints and dust. It looks spectacular on a perfectly prepared floor and unforgiving on anything less.

Semi-gloss is a reasonable middle ground and traditional in older homes.

Satin is what most refinishers recommend and what most people should choose. It has enough sheen to look finished and enough diffusion to hide minor scratches and imperfections. Wear shows far more slowly than on gloss.

Matte and flat have become fashionable and read as very natural, close to an unfinished look. They hide scratches best of all, but they show foot traffic patterns and are harder to clean because there is more surface texture holding dirt.

Applying Water-Based Polyurethane to a Floor

Sand and Vacuum Properly

Finish only hides what is under it on a gloss floor; on satin it hides slightly more. Work through the grits, finishing at 100 to 120 for a floor that will take a waterborne finish — going finer can burnish the wood and reduce adhesion. Vacuum thoroughly, then tack with a microfibre cloth.

Take the doors off before you start rather than working around them. A floor finish needs the full width of the doorway to coat cleanly, and refinishing is the natural moment to reconsider the opening itself — if you are weighing a slider against a swinging pair, our barn door vs. French door comparison covers the trade-offs. Rehang once the finish has cured, not merely dried.

Deal With Grain Raise

Water swells wood fibres, so the first coat will raise the grain and leave the floor feeling rough. This is normal and expected. Some finishers deliberately water-pop the floor first — wetting it evenly, letting it dry, and sanding back — which gets the raise out of the way and improves stain penetration. Either way, sand lightly after the first coat.

Use a Pad, Not a Brush

A microfibre applicator pad on a pole is the correct tool for a floor. It holds enough finish to cross the room without reloading, lays a consistent film thickness, and generates fewer bubbles than any alternative. Cut the perimeter in with a synthetic brush first, then pad the field.

Keep a Wet Edge, and Plan Your Exit

Work along the length of the boards, maintain a wet edge across the full run, and plan the sequence so you finish at a doorway. Water-based poly gives you 60 to 120 seconds — once an edge sets, the lap line is permanent and visible under raking light.

Three Coats, Sanded Between

Three coats is standard, four in high-traffic areas. Sand lightly at 220 to 320 grit between coats — enough to knock down raised grain and dust nibs, not enough to cut through. Vacuum and tack after every sanding.

Control the Environment

65 to 75°F and moderate humidity. High humidity extends drying and can cause blushing; heat and low humidity shorten the open time to almost nothing. Shut off HVAC airflow over the floor while coats cure, and keep the room closed to dust.

Respect the Cure

Light foot traffic after 24 hours in socks. Furniture after 48 to 72 hours, lifted rather than dragged, with felt pads fitted. No rugs for two to four weeks — a rug over a curing finish traps solvent and leaves a permanent outline. Full hardness takes one to two weeks even on fast-curing products.

🐕 If You Have Dogs: Buy the two-component finish. Dog claws are the single most demanding thing a residential floor faces, and the difference between a commercial-grade crosslinked finish and a consumer single-component one shows within a year. Keep claws trimmed and put runners on the routes they use, and a Traffic HD floor will still look good in a decade.

The Verdict

If you are refinishing a floor you intend to live on, Bona Traffic HD is where the money should go. It was the most durable finish in our testing by a clear margin, it is what professionals use when the result has to last, and the difference against consumer finishes shows within the first year in a busy house.

General Finishes High Performance is the best value for bedrooms, studies, and low-traffic rooms, and it is the finish to use on furniture regardless of what is on the floors. Its levelling is the best in the test, which makes it the most forgiving choice for a first attempt.

Varathane Water-Based Floor Finish is adequate for a spare room or a rental and it is on the shelf today.

Whichever you choose, the applicator and the technique account for more of the outcome than the product: pad on a pole, cut in the edges first, keep a wet edge, three thin coats sanded between, and no rugs for a month.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best water-based polyurethane for floors?

Bona Traffic HD, by a clear margin. It is a two-component waterborne polyurethane where a crosslinker is mixed in before use, and that crosslinking produces a film substantially harder than any single-component finish. In our Taber abrasion testing it lost less film than anything else tested and was the only product showing no visible scuffing after simulated furniture drag. It is commercial-rated, which for a residential floor means a decade of good appearance rather than five years. It is expensive and sold through flooring distributors rather than general retail. For bedrooms and low-traffic rooms, General Finishes High Performance is the better value; for a rental or spare room, Varathane's floor finish is adequate and on the shelf today.

What is the best applicator for polyurethane on hardwood floors?

A microfibre applicator pad on a pole. Nothing else comes close on a floor: a pad holds enough finish to cross a room without reloading, lays a consistent film thickness without the stroke-to-stroke variation a brush produces, and generates fewer bubbles than any alternative. Its loading capacity is what lets you keep a wet edge across a full board run, which matters because water-based poly gives you only 60 to 120 seconds of open time and a set edge leaves a permanent lap line visible under raking light. Cut the perimeter and any awkward areas in with a synthetic brush first, then pad the field, working along the length of the boards and planning your sequence to finish at a doorway.

Is water-based polyurethane as durable as oil-based?

Modern two-component waterborne finishes are more durable. Bona Traffic HD and comparable crosslinked commercial products outperform oil-based polyurethane in abrasion testing, which is why professional refinishers use them on jobs that have to last. Single-component water-based finishes are a different matter — General Finishes High Performance is comparable to oil-based poly for residential use, and budget consumer waterborne products are less durable. The old reputation that water-based finishes are less tough dates from formulations that are two decades out of date. What has not changed is application difficulty: oil-based poly gives you fifteen minutes of open time and levels itself, where water-based gives you ninety seconds and keeps whatever texture you leave.

Will water-based polyurethane yellow over time?

No, and this is the main reason it has taken over hardwood floor finishing. Water-based finishes stay clear indefinitely — in our 18-month UV exposure testing, Bona Traffic HD showed effectively zero colour shift on maple. Oil-based polyurethane ambers: it looks warm and honey-toned when applied and progressively more orange over the following decade. On white oak, maple, ash, and any whitewashed or bleached floor, that ambering is what people are trying to avoid, and there is no oil formulation that prevents it. The flip side is that on red oak and pine, some people prefer the warmth an oil finish adds, in which case an oil-modified waterborne hybrid is a reasonable middle ground.

Can I use Minwax Polycrylic on floors?

No — it is a furniture and trim finish, not a floor finish, despite being the water-based polyurethane most people have heard of. It is formulated for cabinets, shelving, built-ins, and trim, where abrasion is light, and it will wear through on a floor considerably faster than a product formulated for one. If you want a water-based finish from a home centre for a floor, Varathane's Water-Based Floor Finish is the appropriate product from that shelf. For anything you want to last, buy General Finishes High Performance for light-traffic rooms or Bona Traffic HD for main living areas — both require ordering from a woodworking or flooring supplier rather than a big-box store.

What sheen should I use on a hardwood floor?

Satin, for most people and most floors. It has enough sheen to look finished and enough light diffusion to hide minor scratches, dust nibs, and imperfections in the substrate, so wear shows far more slowly than on higher sheens. Gloss reflects light directly, which makes every scratch and every flaw in the sanding visible, and it shows footprints and dust — spectacular on a perfectly prepared floor, unforgiving on anything less. Semi-gloss is a reasonable middle ground and traditional in older homes. Matte and flat have become fashionable and read as very natural, hiding scratches best of all, but they show foot traffic patterns and are harder to clean because more surface texture holds dirt.

How long before I can put furniture and rugs back on a new floor?

Light foot traffic in socks after 24 hours. Furniture after 48 to 72 hours — lift it rather than dragging it, and fit felt pads before it goes down. Rugs are the one people get wrong: wait two to four weeks. A rug placed over a finish that is still curing traps solvent underneath it and leaves a permanent outline in the film that cannot be removed without recoating. Full hardness takes one to two weeks even on fast-curing two-component products, and longer in cool or humid conditions. During that window, avoid wet cleaning the floor and be careful with anything that could scratch, since the film is at its most vulnerable before it fully crosslinks.

Why does my floor feel rough after the first coat?

Raised grain, and it is normal rather than a defect. Water swells wood fibres, so the first water-based coat lifts the grain and leaves the surface feeling rough — this happens with every waterborne finish and does not indicate a problem with the product or your application. Sand it back lightly with 220 to 320 grit after that coat has fully dried, then vacuum and tack thoroughly before the next one. Subsequent coats raise the grain far less. Some finishers avoid the issue by water-popping the floor before finishing: wetting it evenly with water, letting it dry, and sanding back, which gets the raise out of the way and, as a bonus, improves stain penetration if you are staining.
