Epoxy Flake Flooring: Cost, Pros and Cons (2026 Guide)

A flake floor is four layers, not one: a base coat, a broadcast of vinyl chips, a scrape, and a clear topcoat. How much flake goes down and what goes over it decide how the floor looks, how it grips and how long it lasts.

Epoxy flake flooring — also sold as chip, fleck or broadcast flooring — is a resinous coating with coloured vinyl flakes thrown into the wet base coat, scraped smooth once it cures, then locked in under a clear topcoat. It is the finish most garage coating companies sell, and for good reason: the flakes hide dust, tyre marks and slab blemishes, add grip, and make the floor far more forgiving to live with than a solid colour.

Professionally installed, an epoxy flake floor with a polyaspartic topcoat runs about $3–$7 per square foot, or $1,500–$3,500 for a typical two-car garage, and lasts roughly 10–20 years. A full polyaspartic or polyurea flake system runs $5–$10 per square foot. Doing it yourself with a high-solids epoxy and a polyaspartic topcoat costs $600–$1,400 for 500 square feet before flake and preparation tools — and the two decisions that matter most are how heavily you broadcast and what you put over the top.

⚡ The short answer: A flake floor is a base coat, a broadcast of vinyl chips, a scrape, and a clear topcoat. A full broadcast (flake until the base disappears) uses roughly 0.08–0.15 lb of flake per sq ft, hides the most and grips best. A partial broadcast uses roughly 0.01–0.04 lb per sq ft and costs less but shows the base and is slicker. Pro installation runs $3–$7/sq ft with epoxy and a polyaspartic topcoat; use a UV-stable polyaspartic topcoat anywhere the floor sees sunlight.

What a Flake (Chip) System Is

Every flake floor, from a $200 big-box kit to a $5,000 professional installation, is built in the same four stages. What differs is the chemistry of each layer and how much flake goes into the second one.

1. The Base Coat

A pigmented coat of epoxy (or, on most professional one-day systems, polyurea or polyaspartic) rolled onto a diamond-ground slab. It does two jobs: bonding to the concrete, and acting as the glue that grabs the flakes. Its colour is normally matched to the dominant tone of the flake blend, so any gaps between chips read as part of the pattern rather than as bare patches. The base needs to go on thicker than it would for a solid-colour floor, because a thin film cannot grip the chips.

2. The Broadcast

While the base is still wet, flakes are thrown by hand across the floor. The technique is to toss them upward and outward so they rain down evenly, rather than throwing them at the floor, which produces clumps and bare streaks. The installer wears spiked shoes to walk on the wet coating. The density of the broadcast — a light scatter or a full layer until the base vanishes — is the biggest single design decision in the system.

3. The Scrape

Once the base has cured enough to hold the flakes firmly, the floor is scraped with a long-handled floor scraper. Many flakes land on edge or curl slightly, and those standing edges are sharp. Scraping in several directions knocks them flat or snaps them off, and the loose flake is then vacuumed or blown out. On a full broadcast, a surprising amount of flake comes back up at this stage — that is normal, and it is why full-broadcast floors use so much material.

4. The Clear Topcoat

One or two clear coats seal the flakes in, set the final texture, and take the wear. This layer is where most of the system's durability lives. On a quality floor it is polyaspartic or polyurethane rather than clear epoxy, for reasons covered below.

Partial vs Full Broadcast

"Epoxy flooring with flakes" covers two quite different floors. A partial broadcast — sometimes called a light, random or decorative broadcast — scatters flakes over a solid-colour base so the base remains the dominant colour. A full broadcast, also called broadcast to rejection, keeps throwing flake until the wet base cannot hold any more and disappears completely beneath it.

Choose a full broadcast for a working garage. It is what professionals install for a reason: it hides virtually everything a garage throws at a floor, it grips better, and because the flake layer itself adds body, it tolerates abrasion well. The extra cost on a two-car garage is a few hundred dollars in materials.

Choose a partial broadcast when you actually want the solid colour to show — a clean grey workshop with a few accent specks, say — or when the budget is tight and the floor is lightly used. Understand that you are getting a solid-colour floor with decoration, not a flake floor in the protective sense. Most big-box kits fall into this category, since the flake packet included is enough for a light scatter rather than full coverage.

A middle "medium" broadcast exists, but in practice it combines the main drawbacks of both: you still see the base between chips, and you have spent much of the flake money anyway. Commit to one end or the other.

Flake Sizes and Blends

Vinyl flake is sold by nominal chip size and by colour blend. The size changes the look far more than people expect, and it affects how easily the floor scrapes and topcoats.

Choosing a Blend

Blends are usually three to six colours mixed in fixed proportions. A few practical rules hold across manufacturers:

  • Mid-tone blends hide the most. A grey, tan and black blend with some white disguises dust, tyre marks and road salt residue. Very dark blends show white salt and dust; very light ones show tyre marks and oil.
  • Match the base to the dominant flake colour. On a full broadcast it barely matters; on a partial broadcast the base is most of what you see.
  • Buy all the flake from one batch at once. Blend proportions and dye lots vary between production runs. Keep a few pounds back for repairs.
  • Look at a real sample under your own lighting. Screen images are close to useless for judging flake colour, and garage lighting is usually cool and harsh.

How Much Flake You Need Per Square Foot

Manufacturers publish their own coverage figures, and they vary with flake size, base thickness and how hard the floor is scraped, so treat these as planning ranges and check the product sheet for the flake you buy.

BroadcastTypical lb / sq ft1-car (250 sq ft)2-car (500 sq ft)3-car (750 sq ft)
Light / partial~0.01 – 0.043 – 10 lb5 – 20 lb8 – 30 lb
Full (to rejection)~0.08 – 0.1520 – 40 lb40 – 75 lb60 – 115 lb

Put another way, a full broadcast commonly works out to roughly one pound for every 7–12 square feet, which is why professionals buy flake in 40- to 55-pound boxes. A light broadcast might be a pound per 25–100 square feet, and the small packets included in DIY kits are sized for that kind of scatter.

Two things push consumption up. First, a thick, wet base holds more flake before it rejects. Second, smaller flakes generally take more weight to reach full coverage on a heavy base than people assume, and coverage figures for 1/4" chips do not transfer directly to 1/8" or 1/16". The good news is that much of the excess on a full broadcast is recoverable: flake swept up cleanly before it touches anything wet or dirty can be reused, while flake that comes up during scraping is generally too dusty and broken to reuse.

💡 Order 10–15% extra. Running out halfway across a wet base is the one flake mistake you cannot fix — the base will skin over before more arrives, and the boundary will show forever. Any surplus is your repair stock.

Why the Topcoat Matters

The flakes themselves are decorative vinyl. They are not what protects the floor. The clear topcoat is — it encapsulates the chips, resists abrasion and chemicals, and determines whether the floor still looks good in year eight. Two topcoat chemistries dominate.

Clear Epoxy Topcoat

Cheaper, easy to roll, with a long working time. Its weakness is ultraviolet light: epoxy is aromatic and ambers — yellows — with sun exposure, sometimes noticeably within months near an open garage door or window. It is also softer against abrasion and more prone to dulling in traffic lanes. Under a light flake it can be acceptable in a windowless basement; under a full broadcast in a garage, it is the weak link.

Polyaspartic (or Aliphatic Polyurethane) Topcoat

UV-stable, so it does not amber, and noticeably harder against abrasion, chemicals and hot-tyre pickup. It typically adds roughly $1–$3 per square foot over a bare epoxy floor and is the best value upgrade in the system. The catch for DIY is pot life: some polyaspartics give you only 15–30 minutes once mixed, so you work in small batches with two people. Slower DIY-oriented formulas exist. For a closer look at how the two chemistries compare as complete systems, see epoxy vs polyaspartic garage floors.

How Much Topcoat a Flake Floor Needs

A full broadcast is rough and slightly absorbent, and it needs more clear material than a smooth floor to fully encapsulate the chips. Expect to use noticeably more topcoat than the coverage figure for a smooth surface suggests — often two coats, or one heavy coat followed by a second where the texture is still too coarse. Too little topcoat leaves flake edges exposed, and exposed vinyl wears, stains and holds dirt.

Texture and Slip Resistance

Any glossy resin floor is slippery when wet, and garages are wet: snowmelt, rain off the car, spilled washer fluid. Flake helps, but the final texture is set by the topcoat, not the flake.

  • Topcoat thickness controls texture. One coat over a full broadcast leaves a pronounced orange-peel or fine sandpaper feel. Two coats fill it toward smooth. Smoother is easier to clean and gentler on knees; rougher grips better.
  • Add anti-slip aggregate in the topcoat. Fine aluminium oxide is hard-wearing and aggressive; polymer (polypropylene) grit is softer, easier to mop and kinder to bare feet. It is either mixed into the topcoat or lightly broadcast into it before rolling back.
  • Put grit where water lands. Some owners add aggregate only to the parking bays, apron and steps and keep walkways smoother. That is a reasonable compromise if you clean the floor often.
  • Partial broadcasts need grit more. The bare base between scattered chips behaves like any smooth coating.

Slipperiness is one of the most common complaints about resin floors generally; our page on the disadvantages of epoxy flooring covers it alongside moisture, cure time and removal difficulty.

DIY Steps Overview

A DIY flake floor is very achievable on a sound, dry slab. This is the outline; our step-by-step guide on how to apply garage floor epoxy covers the full process, from moisture testing to mixing and cure.

  1. Test for moisture. Tape a 2 ft plastic square to the bare slab for 24–48 hours. Condensation underneath means you need a moisture-mitigating primer or a different finish.
  2. Empty, degrease and repair. Fill cracks and spalls with an epoxy or polyurea patch. Under a full broadcast, minor patch work disappears.
  3. Grind the floor. Rent a diamond grinder ($80–$150 a day) with a vacuum. Acid etching is cheaper but far less reliable; see acid etching vs grinding for why grinding wins.
  4. Roll the base in sections. Cut in edges, then roll a section a few feet wide at a generous, even thickness. Work toward the door.
  5. Broadcast immediately. One person rolls, the other follows in spiked shoes throwing flake upward so it falls evenly. For a full broadcast, keep going until the surface looks dry and no wet base shows through.
  6. Let it cure. Typically overnight for epoxy; check the label for the scrape window.
  7. Scrape and vacuum. Scrape in at least two directions, then vacuum and blow out thoroughly. Run a gloved hand over the floor; anything sharp needs another pass.
  8. Topcoat. Apply the clear coat (with aggregate if wanted), and a second coat if the texture is too coarse or chips are still exposed.
  9. Cure before use. Foot traffic after about 24 hours; vehicles after five to seven days for an epoxy base, often sooner for polyaspartic. Check the product data sheet.

Cost: DIY vs Professional

Flake is rarely the expensive part of a flake floor. Preparation, resin film thickness and the topcoat dominate. Vinyl flake in bulk boxes commonly costs somewhere around $3–$6 per pound, more in small bags, which puts a full broadcast at very roughly $0.25–$0.90 per square foot in flake alone.

What Moves a Professional Quote

  • Preparation method. Diamond grinding or shot blasting is the reliable standard. A low quote that specifies etching is a different job.
  • Base chemistry. Most one-day garage systems use a polyurea or polyaspartic base instead of epoxy, which is why they sit higher in the range.
  • Slab condition. Crack repair, moisture mitigation ($1–$3 per sq ft) and removing an old coating ($1–$3 per sq ft) all add on.
  • Extras. Stem walls, steps and coved edges are priced separately, often by the linear foot.
  • Size. Small floors cost more per square foot because mobilisation and minimum charges spread over less area.

Pros and Cons of Flake Epoxy Flooring

✓ Pros

  • Hides dust, tyre marks, minor stains and slab imperfections far better than solid colour
  • Better grip than a smooth coating, especially with a full broadcast
  • Flake layer adds body, so abrasion and scratches are less visible
  • Seamless and easy to sweep and squeegee
  • Wide range of colours and sizes
  • Topcoat can be renewed to refresh the floor without starting over

✗ Cons

  • Still fails over a damp or poorly prepared slab, like any bonded coating
  • Rougher texture holds more dirt than a glass-smooth floor
  • Epoxy topcoats amber in sunlight; UV-stable topcoats cost more
  • Full broadcast uses a lot of flake and extra topcoat
  • Hard to remove later; needs grinding
  • Repairs rarely match perfectly once the floor has aged

Cleaning a Flake Floor

Flake floors are low-maintenance, but grit is the enemy. Sand and road grit tracked in on tyres act like sandpaper underfoot and dull the topcoat over time.

  • Dust mop or sweep weekly with a soft broom or microfibre dust mop. A textured floor holds more grit than a smooth one, so do not skip this.
  • Wet clean with a mild, pH-neutral cleaner or a small amount of ammonia diluted in water, then squeegee toward the drain or door.
  • Avoid soap-based cleaners that leave a film. The residue makes the floor slippery and attracts dirt.
  • Avoid strong acids and harsh citrus degreasers on a regular basis; they can dull some topcoats.
  • Wipe up spills promptly, particularly brake fluid, battery acid, and some solvents, which can stain or soften topcoats if left.
  • Use a soft deck brush on texture. A stiff brush is fine for a heavily gritted floor; a scrubbing pad should be non-abrasive.
  • Rinse off road salt in winter. Dried salt leaves a white haze and is abrasive.

Repairing Damage

Most flake-floor damage is one of three things: a gouge from a dropped tool, a worn traffic lane where the topcoat has thinned, or a section that has lost bond.

Gouges and chips. Clean the area, lightly sand around it, fill with a small batch of base coat, broadcast matching flake from your reserve, scrape once cured, and topcoat. The flake pattern itself is forgiving and hides the edge of the repair; the topcoat sheen is what usually gives it away, so feather the clear coat a little wider than the patch.

Worn topcoat. When traffic lanes dull, the fix is not a new floor. Clean thoroughly, abrade the whole surface with a sanding screen to give it tooth, and roll on a fresh clear coat. A recoat like this can extend the life of a flake floor significantly, and it is one of the strongest arguments for the system.

Peeling or lifting sections. Coating that comes up in sheets almost always traces to preparation or moisture rather than wear. Patching over it rarely holds; the area needs to be ground back to sound coating and the cause addressed. Our guide to why epoxy garage floors peel walks through the diagnosis.

Common Mistakes

Flakes not fully embedded. The base was too thin, or the broadcast came too late after the base had begun to skin. Chips sit on top rather than in the coating, pop off during scraping, and leave bald spots. Roll a generous base, work in sections small enough that the broadcaster keeps pace, and broadcast immediately behind the roller.

Sharp chips not scraped. Skip or rush the scrape and the topcoat simply encases the standing edges, leaving a floor that snags socks, cuts knees and holds dirt. Scrape in multiple directions and check by hand before topcoating. This is the step most DIY flake floors visibly fail.

Not enough topcoat. Exposed vinyl edges wear and stain. A full broadcast nearly always needs more clear material than the smooth-surface coverage figure on the can.

Uneven broadcasting. Throwing flake down at the floor creates clumps and thin streaks. Throw it up and let it fall, and keep a wet edge.

Running out of flake. Covered above, and worth repeating: order 10–15% extra from one batch.

Clear epoxy topcoat in a sunny garage. It ambers. If the door is open on sunny days or the floor gets direct light, use a polyaspartic or aliphatic polyurethane.

Skipping preparation. Flake hides surface flaws but does nothing for adhesion. A flaked floor over an unground or damp slab fails exactly as a plain one does.

Driving on it too soon. Hot tyres on undercured resin cause pickup and marks. Wait the full cure time on the label — typically five to seven days for epoxy-based systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does epoxy flake flooring cost?

Professionally installed, an epoxy base with a full flake broadcast and a polyaspartic topcoat typically runs $3–$7 per square foot, or about $1,500–$3,500 for a 500 sq ft two-car garage. A full polyaspartic or polyurea flake system runs $5–$10 per square foot, about $2,500–$5,000. DIY with a high-solids epoxy and polyaspartic topcoat costs $600–$1,400 for 500 sq ft before flake, plus roughly $120–$450 of flake for a full broadcast and $150–$400 of preparation tools. A big-box kit with a light flake scatter runs $150–$400 but lasts only a few years.

What is the difference between partial and full flake broadcast?

A partial broadcast scatters flakes over a solid-colour base, so the base stays the dominant colour and the floor is mostly smooth. It uses roughly 0.01–0.04 lb of flake per square foot. A full broadcast keeps throwing flake until the wet base cannot hold any more and disappears, giving a speckled granite-like look, much better hiding of dirt and slab flaws, and more grip. It uses roughly 0.08–0.15 lb per square foot and needs more topcoat. For a working garage, a full broadcast is usually the better choice.

How many pounds of flake do I need for a 2-car garage?

For a 500 sq ft two-car garage, roughly 40–75 lb for a full broadcast and 5–20 lb for a light, partial broadcast. Actual use depends on flake size, how thick the base coat is and how much comes up during scraping, so check the manufacturer's coverage figure for your product. Order 10–15% extra from a single batch: running out mid-floor leaves a visible boundary, and the surplus becomes your repair stock.

What size epoxy floor flakes are best?

1/4" flakes are the standard for residential garages. They are widely stocked, hide dirt well, lie reasonably flat and scrape easily. 1/8" flakes give a finer, more granite-like look that suits basements and smaller spaces. 1/16" micro flakes read almost as a uniform texture and suit kitchens, retail and steps. Larger chips are dramatic but lie less flat and need more scraping and topcoat. Mid-tone blends of grey, tan and black hide the most.

Is flake epoxy flooring slippery?

Less than a smooth coating, but it can still be slippery when wet. A full broadcast under a single topcoat leaves an orange-peel texture with good grip; a second topcoat smooths it and reduces traction. Partial broadcasts behave much like a smooth floor. For parking bays, aprons and steps, add a fine anti-slip aggregate to the topcoat — aluminium oxide for maximum grip, or polymer grit for a softer, easier-to-clean texture.

Do I need a topcoat over epoxy flakes?

Yes. Without a clear topcoat the vinyl flakes are exposed, so they wear, stain, hold dirt and leave a rough surface. The topcoat encapsulates the chips and takes the abrasion. A polyaspartic or aliphatic polyurethane topcoat is preferred because it is UV-stable and harder-wearing; a clear epoxy topcoat costs less but ambers in sunlight and dulls faster in traffic lanes. Full broadcasts generally need two coats or one heavy coat to fully encapsulate the flakes.

How long does a flake epoxy floor last?

A professionally installed epoxy flake floor with a polyaspartic topcoat typically lasts 10–20 years, and a full polyaspartic or polyurea flake system 15–20 years or more. A big-box kit with a light scatter of flake lasts more like two to four years. Life depends mostly on surface preparation, film thickness and topcoat chemistry. Renewing the topcoat when traffic lanes dull — abrade and roll on a fresh clear coat — can extend the floor's life substantially.

Can I put flakes in a DIY epoxy floor kit?

Yes, and most kits include a small packet, but it is usually only enough for a light decorative scatter, not a full broadcast. For full coverage you will need to buy flake separately in bulk, broadcast it into a base coat applied thicker than usual, scrape once cured, and use enough clear topcoat to encapsulate the chips. If the kit's clear coat is epoxy and the garage gets sun, consider a separate polyaspartic topcoat instead.
