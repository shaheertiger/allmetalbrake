Most people shop for home backup the wrong way round. They pick a battery generator by its headline capacity, carry it home, and only during the first real outage discover that the furnace blower is hardwired, the well pump needs 240 volts, and the sump pump's starting surge trips the inverter the moment the fridge kicks on. The fix is to plan backwards: write down what must keep running, work out when and how hard each load pulls, decide how the battery will reach those loads, and only then choose the box.

This guide is built around that plan. It covers how to write a critical-load list, three worked examples for common households, the safe ways to connect a power station to house circuits (and the one way you must never use), how UPS switchover protects CPAP machines and other medical equipment, what 240 V well pumps really demand, and how to stretch a battery through an outage that lasts days rather than hours. The six picks below are the battery generators we would shortlist for that job, with an emphasis on the 2 to 4 kWh-plus, expandable and 240 V units that can genuinely carry a house. If you want the broader category explained, including the small camping units, start with our main portable power station guide.

⚡ The short answer: For real home backup, plan on a 4 kWh class, expandable LFP system with 120/240 V output wired through a transfer switch, interlock or the maker's smart panel: the EcoFlow Delta Pro 3 for the best all-round system, or the Anker SOLIX F3800 if a 240 V well pump or other big motor is on your list. If you only need to keep a fridge, internet and a CPAP going overnight, a 2 kWh unit such as the Jackery Explorer 2000 v2 plugged in directly is enough. A 1 kWh unit is a bridge for short outages, not a house backup.

Step One: Your Critical-Load List

A critical-load list is a short inventory of everything that must keep working during an outage, with three facts for each item: its running watts, its starting watts if it has a motor, and how many hours a day it actually draws power. Walk through the house with a notepad and be strict. "Must" means safety, health, property protection or food — not convenience.

Most lists fall into four tiers. Tier one is life and health: CPAP or BiPAP machines, oxygen concentrators, refrigerated medication, and in cold climates the heat source. Tier two is property protection: the sump pump in a wet basement, the well pump if you have no city water, and heat to stop pipes freezing. Tier three is food: the refrigerator and any freezer. Tier four is communication and comfort: internet, phones, a few lights, a fan. Size your backup for tiers one to three first, then see what is left for tier four.

The figures are typical ranges; your appliances may differ. The best source is the nameplate sticker on each appliance, or a plug-in energy meter left on the fridge and freezer for a full day. For hardwired equipment, the nameplate on the motor or the furnace's data label gives the amps; multiply by the voltage for an approximate wattage. If you cannot find a figure, an electrician or HVAC technician can measure the starting current with a clamp meter in a few minutes, and that one number can save you from buying the wrong inverter.

Two columns in that table drive the purchase. The hardwired column tells you whether you need a panel connection, which affects which unit you buy and what the installation costs. The starting watts column tells you how much inverter headroom you need: add the running watts of everything that might be on together, then add the single largest starting surge on top. That sum must fit under the power station's continuous and surge ratings.

💡 Stagger the motors. You cannot control when a fridge compressor cycles on, but you can control when you start a microwave, a well pump or a dehumidifier. Avoid running two large motor loads at the same moment and you can often get away with a smaller inverter than the worst-case sum suggests.

Essential-Circuit Worked Examples

The three households below use the runtime rule from our main guide: runtime in hours ≈ (capacity in Wh × 0.85) ÷ average load in watts. The 0.85 covers inverter losses and the reserve the battery management system holds back. For daily energy, we add each load's watts multiplied by its hours, then divide by 0.85 to get the battery capacity actually needed.

Example 1: Summer Storm, Suburban House with a Basement

The risk here is a thunderstorm that knocks out power while rain is still falling, so the sump pump matters as much as the fridge. The daily list: refrigerator 1,500 Wh; sump pump, a 1/3 HP unit at about 800 W running for ten seconds every five minutes during heavy inflow, which works out to roughly 650 Wh across a wet day; internet 20 W around the clock, 480 Wh; CPAP 40 W for eight hours, 320 Wh; LED lights 200 Wh; phone charging 100 Wh. That totals about 3,250 Wh a day, or roughly 3,820 Wh of battery once losses are included.

Output: fridge running 200 W, internet 20 W, CPAP 40 W, lights 60 W, plus the sump pump's 800 W running and a starting surge of 1,300 W or more. Worst case, with the fridge compressor starting at the same instant, you are looking at 2,500 to 3,000 W for a moment. A 4 kWh unit with 4,000 W or more of output handles this for about one full day, and a 2 kWh unit gets through roughly half a day — enough for a typical storm outage, but tight if the power stays off overnight and the rain continues. If the basement floods regularly, also consider a dedicated battery backup sump pump so the pump never depends on the power station at all.

Example 2: Winter Outage, Gas Furnace

A gas furnace still needs electricity: the blower, the igniter, the inducer fan and the control board all stop without it. In a cold snap the blower may run for ten hours a day or more. Using a 400 W blower as a middle-of-the-range figure, that is 4,000 Wh a day on its own. Add the fridge at 1,200 Wh (it works less in a cool house), internet 480 Wh, CPAP 320 Wh, lights 300 Wh in the longer evenings and charging 100 Wh, and the total is about 6,400 Wh, or roughly 7,500 Wh of battery a day.

That is nearly double what a 4 kWh unit holds. In practice it means one of three things: a base unit plus one or more add-on batteries; a plan to recharge from a generator once a day, because winter sun is weak; or turning the thermostat down to the low 60s, which can shorten blower run time substantially. Note that the furnace is hardwired, so this household needs a transfer switch, interlock or smart panel regardless of which battery it buys. Newer furnaces with variable-speed blower motors draw considerably less than older ones, so measure yours rather than assuming.

Example 3: Rural House on a 240 V Well

Here energy is not the hard part; output is. A family rationing water to around 100 gallons a day with a pump moving about 10 gallons a minute runs the pump for perhaps ten minutes a day. At roughly 1,200 W running, that is only about 200 Wh. But the starting surge of a 240 V submersible pump can be several times its running draw, and it arrives without warning whenever the pressure tank drops. Add a fridge at 1,500 Wh, a chest freezer at 900 Wh, internet 480 Wh and lights and charging 300 Wh, and the daily total is around 3,400 Wh, or about 4,000 Wh of battery.

A 4 kWh class unit therefore lasts about a day, but only a model with native 240 V output and plenty of surge capacity can start the pump reliably. That points to the Anker SOLIX F3800 first and the EcoFlow Delta Pro 3 second, connected through a transfer switch that includes the well-pump circuit. Confirm the pump's starting current before buying; it is the number that decides whether this plan works.

Connecting to the House Safely

Plug-in loads are easy: run a heavy-duty extension cord from the power station to the fridge, or put the station right next to it. Because a battery produces no exhaust, it can sit in the kitchen. Hardwired loads — furnaces, many sump pumps, well pumps, and lighting circuits — are different. There are three safe ways to reach them, and one dangerous one.

⚠️ Never backfeed. A double-ended "suicide cord" plugged from a power station into a wall outlet sends power backward into your panel and, unless the main breaker is off, out onto the utility lines, where it can electrocute a line worker. It also bypasses the breaker protection on the circuit you plug into and puts live, exposed prongs on the other end of the cord. It is illegal in most jurisdictions and never worth it.

Manual Transfer Switch

A manual transfer switch is a small subpanel mounted next to your main panel. An electrician moves a handful of circuits — typically six to ten — into it, and each one gets a switch that selects grid or backup. A power inlet box on the outside wall or in the garage accepts a cord from the power station. During an outage you plug in, flip the selected circuits to backup, and those circuits run from the battery while the grid side is physically disconnected. It is simple, foolproof, and forces you to decide in advance which circuits matter — which is exactly the planning this guide recommends.

Panel Interlock Kit

An interlock is a sliding plate fitted to the main panel that makes it mechanically impossible to turn on the main breaker and a backfeed breaker at the same time. With an inlet box wired to that backfeed breaker, the whole panel can be powered from backup once the main is off. It is often the lower-cost option and lets you choose circuits on the fly, but that flexibility is also its risk with a battery: switch on the water heater or the range by mistake and you will drain or trip the station in minutes. Treat the interlock as a whole-panel connection that you manage by turning off every non-essential breaker first.

Manufacturer Smart Panels

EcoFlow and Anker both sell home panels designed for their large units. These sit alongside your main panel, switch selected circuits automatically when the grid fails, and let you monitor and shed loads from an app. They are the most seamless option — the house changes over without anyone going to the garage — and the most expensive, and they tie you to one brand's ecosystem. Whichever connection you choose, it is a job for a licensed electrician with a permit, and your utility may need to be told.

UPS Switchover and Medical Devices

For a CPAP, an oxygen concentrator or a home dialysis setup, the question is not just how long the battery lasts but what happens in the instant the grid drops. Most current power stations offer an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) mode: the device is plugged into the station, the station is plugged into the wall, and wall power passes through until it fails. Manufacturers typically quote a switchover of roughly 10 to 30 milliseconds, fast enough that most CPAP machines, routers and desktop computers keep running without a reboot.

There are caveats worth taking seriously. This pass-through switchover is not the same as a true online UPS, which runs the load from its inverter at all times and has no switchover gap. Some sensitive equipment is less tolerant than a CPAP, so check the device manual or ask the supplier. And a station sitting permanently on pass-through should be one designed for it; the manufacturers that advertise a UPS function intend it for this use.

Energy planning for medical devices is straightforward but often overlooked. A CPAP without its heated humidifier uses roughly 30 to 60 W, so a 1 kWh unit covers two or three nights. Turn the humidifier on and consumption can climb steeply. Stationary oxygen concentrators draw considerably more — often several hundred watts continuously — so a 1 kWh unit may last only a few hours; size those with care and talk to the equipment provider about backup cylinders. Register with your utility's medical-needs or priority restoration program if one exists, and keep a written list of each device's wattage taped near the power station.

💡 Run CPAP from DC if you can. Many CPAP makers sell a 12 V or DC power adapter. Running the machine from the power station's DC output skips the inverter's conversion losses and can add meaningfully to overnight runtime.

240 V Well Pumps

If your water comes from a well, your water is electric. No power means no flushing, no washing and no drinking water once the pressure tank empties. Many deep-well submersible pumps run on 240 V split-phase power, which rules out every 120 V-only power station regardless of capacity. Shallow-well jet pumps and some smaller submersibles are 120 V; the pump's nameplate or your well installer will tell you which you have.

Three numbers matter: the pump's voltage, its running current, and its locked-rotor or starting current. The starting surge is the one that trips inverters, because a battery's inverter cannot momentarily overload the way a large spinning generator can. That is why output, not capacity, is the constraint for well homes, and why the 6,000 W SOLIX F3800 leads our picks for them. The Delta Pro 3's 4,000 W, 240 V output covers many residential pumps as well; check the starting figures against each maker's surge specification before you buy.

Practical strategies help a lot. Ask about a soft-start kit for the pump, which ramps the motor up and can substantially reduce its starting surge. Size the pressure tank generously so the pump cycles less often. During an outage, ration water use and avoid running the pump at the same moment as other motor loads. And make sure the well-pump breaker is one of the circuits wired into your transfer switch — it is surprisingly common for it to be left off.

Multi-Day Outage Strategy

Hurricanes, ice storms and wildfire shutoffs regularly leave homes without power for three days or more. No battery you can reasonably buy stores a week of normal living, so a multi-day plan has three parts: cut consumption, recharge every day, and add storage where it makes sense.

Ration the Big Loads

The refrigerator is the largest flexible load. A closed, full fridge keeps food safe for about four hours without power, and a full freezer for up to two days. Instead of running the fridge continuously, run it for an hour or two, then unplug it for a few hours, and keep the door closed. Depending on the fridge and the kitchen temperature, cycling like this can cut its draw by roughly a third. Move the most perishable food into a cooler, consolidate the freezer, and keep a thermometer in both compartments so you know rather than guess. Do the same with lights and electronics: turn off the AC inverter when nothing needs it, because even idle inverters draw power.

Recharge from Solar

Solar is what turns a battery from a countdown into a cycle. Portable panels typically deliver around 60 to 75% of their rated wattage in real conditions, and a good day gives roughly four to five peak sun hours. Using 65% and 4.5 hours as a middle estimate: 400 W of panels puts back about 1,170 Wh a day, 800 W about 2,340 Wh, and 1,200 W about 3,500 Wh. Pair 1,200 W of panels with a 4 kWh station and a summer-storm household can, on clear days, run its essentials more or less indefinitely. Cloudy days cut that sharply, so treat solar as a strong supplement rather than a guarantee. Our solar generator guide covers panel bundles and input limits in more detail.

Recharge from a Generator

The most robust long-outage setup combines a battery and a fuel generator. Run a small inverter generator outdoors for two or three hours in the afternoon to recharge the power station through its AC charging input, then shut it off and let the battery carry the house silently overnight. You burn a fraction of the gasoline that running the generator around the clock would need, you sleep without engine noise, and the battery handles the brief, peaky loads that generators run inefficiently. Always keep the generator outside, at least 20 feet from doors and windows, and never in a garage, even with the door open.

Add Storage Before You Need It

If your outages routinely run past a day, expandability is the feature to pay for. The Delta Pro 3, SOLIX F3800 and Delta 3 Plus all accept their makers' add-on batteries, which add capacity without buying a second inverter. A fixed unit can still be part of a multi-day plan — a second compact station at the bedside, for example — but it cannot grow. Keep a small portable charger or two for phones as well, so your main battery is not spending its energy on USB charging through the inverter.

🗓️ A three-day plan in one paragraph: Day one, run essentials from a full 4 kWh battery and cycle the fridge. Day two, deploy solar panels at first light and, if skies are gray, run a generator for two to three hours in the afternoon to top up. Day three, repeat, and shed anything not in tiers one to three. Charge every phone and power bank whenever the battery is being recharged, not from the inverter at night.

Winter Outages and Cold Charging

Winter is the hardest season for battery backup, for four reasons. Heating loads are large. Days are short and solar yields fall. Storms that bring down power lines often bring cloud cover too. And lithium batteries do not like the cold.

LFP cells should not be charged below freezing. Charging them under about 32°F (0°C) can permanently damage the cells, and most units' battery management systems block charging in those temperatures to protect them. Discharging in the cold is permitted, but usable capacity drops. The practical rule is simple: keep the power station indoors in winter, in a heated part of the house. A unit stored in an unheated garage may refuse to charge just when you need it, and you will have to bring it inside and let it warm up first. If your transfer-switch inlet is in the garage, store the station inside and roll it out to plug in.

On the load side, attack the furnace blower first. Lower the thermostat into the low 60s, close off unused rooms, and let the sun in on south-facing windows during the day. Keep the house's blower as the only heating load on the battery; an electric space heater drains a 1 kWh battery in well under an hour and should not be part of a battery plan. Protect pipes in exterior walls by opening cabinet doors and, if needed, letting a tap drip — but on a well, remember every drip costs pump energy.

Finally, plan your recharge around the weather. Winter sun is weak and low; tilting portable panels steeply toward the south helps, but expect much less than summer output. In a long winter outage, a generator recharge is usually the backbone of the plan, with solar as a bonus on clear days.

Power Station vs. Standby Generator

A permanently installed standby generator, running on natural gas or propane, is still the benchmark for whole-house backup. It starts automatically, runs for days on piped gas, and can power central air conditioning and electric ranges. A battery system wins on silence, indoor safety, near-zero maintenance and the ability to work with solar every day, not just in emergencies. The right choice depends on how long your outages last and what you need to run.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a portable power station back up my house?

It can back up the parts of your house that matter most, which is usually what people need. A 4 kWh class unit such as the EcoFlow Delta Pro 3 or Anker SOLIX F3800, connected through a transfer switch, interlock or the maker's smart panel, can run a refrigerator, freezer, furnace blower, sump pump, internet, lights and medical devices for roughly a day, longer with solar recharging or add-on batteries. It will not sensibly run central air conditioning, electric heat, an electric water heater or an electric range for long. Write a critical-load list first, then size the battery and choose the connection method around it.

How big a battery do I need for home backup?

Add up the daily energy of everything on your critical-load list, then divide by 0.85 to allow for inverter losses. A fridge, internet, CPAP and lights typically need about 2,500 Wh a day, so roughly 3,000 Wh of battery. Add a sump pump in a storm and you approach 4,000 Wh. Add a gas furnace blower in cold weather and you may need 7,000 Wh or more. For output, add the running watts of everything that can run at once and the largest single starting surge. As a rule of thumb, 1 kWh is an hours-long bridge, 2 kWh covers a night and 4 kWh covers about a day of essentials.

Is a battery generator better than a gas generator for home backup?

Each wins in different situations. A battery generator is silent, produces no carbon monoxide so it can sit indoors, needs almost no maintenance, starts instantly and can recharge from solar. That makes it better for short outages, medical devices, apartments and anyone who does not want to store fuel. A gas generator stores far more energy in a can of fuel and can be refilled indefinitely, so it is better for long outages and heavy loads. For multi-day outages, the strongest setup is both: run a generator outdoors for a few hours to recharge the battery, then let the battery run the house quietly overnight.

Do I need a transfer switch for a power station?

Not for plug-in appliances. A fridge, CPAP, router or lamp can be plugged directly into the power station or into a heavy-duty extension cord from it. You do need a transfer switch, a panel interlock with a power inlet, or the manufacturer's smart panel to run hardwired loads such as a furnace blower, a well pump or lighting circuits. Never use a double-ended cord to backfeed through a wall outlet; it can energize the utility lines and electrocute line workers, and it bypasses your panel's protection. Have a licensed electrician install whichever connection you choose, with a permit where required.

Can a power station run a 240 V well pump?

Only a unit with native 240 V split-phase output and enough surge capacity to start the motor. Many deep-well submersible pumps run on 240 V, which rules out every 120 V-only power station regardless of battery size. Among our picks, the Anker SOLIX F3800 with 6,000 W of output and the EcoFlow Delta Pro 3 with 4,000 W both offer 120/240 V. The deciding number is the pump's starting current, which can be several times its running draw. Get that figure from the nameplate or your well installer, consider a soft-start kit to reduce it, and make sure the pump circuit is wired into your transfer switch.

Can I use a power station as a UPS for a CPAP machine?

Yes, in most cases. Many current power stations have a UPS or pass-through mode: you plug the CPAP into the station and the station into the wall, and when the grid fails the station switches to battery, typically within about 10 to 30 milliseconds by the manufacturers' figures. That is fast enough for most CPAP machines. Check your device's manual or ask the supplier if you are unsure. Without the heated humidifier, a CPAP uses about 30 to 60 W, so a 1 kWh unit lasts two or three nights. Using a DC adapter from the CPAP maker stretches that further by skipping inverter losses.

How do I keep a power station going through a multi-day outage?

Combine rationing, recharging and storage. Cycle the refrigerator on and off rather than running it continuously, keep doors closed, and turn the AC inverter off when nothing needs it. Recharge every day: portable panels deliver roughly 60 to 75% of their rating over four to five peak sun hours, so 800 W of panels returns about 2.3 kWh on a clear day. When skies are gray, run an inverter generator outdoors for a few hours to recharge the station, then shut it off overnight. If long outages are routine, buy an expandable unit and add batteries before you need them.

Can I keep a power station in my garage for winter backup?

It is better kept indoors in a heated space. LFP batteries should not be charged below about 32°F, and most units block charging in those temperatures to protect the cells, so a station stored in a freezing garage may refuse to recharge just when you need it. Discharge still works in the cold, but usable capacity drops. If your transfer-switch inlet is in the garage, store the unit inside the house, and wheel it out to plug in when an outage starts. For long-term storage, keep it at 50 to 80% charge and top it up every few months so it is ready.
