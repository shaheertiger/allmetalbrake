Best Concrete Driveway Sealers — Three Years of Testing

Concrete looks permanent, and it mostly is — but the surface is not. The top few millimetres of a driveway slab are what take the de-icing salt, the freeze-thaw cycling, the oil drips, and the UV, and that thin layer is what fails. Once it starts to scale and pit, there is no repair that restores it; you live with it or you replace the slab.

Sealing is the one maintenance action that meaningfully changes that trajectory. A good penetrating sealer cuts water absorption by 80–90%, and since water is the vehicle for salt, freeze damage, and staining, that single change addresses most of the mechanisms that destroy concrete surfaces.

We tested eight concrete sealers over three years on driveway slabs in a freeze-thaw region, measuring absorption, salt scaling, hot-tire pickup, appearance change, and how each behaved at recoat.

⚡ Quick Answer: For a concrete driveway, buy a penetrating silane/siloxane — the Foundation Armor SX5000 is the best all-round choice and the Ghostshield Siloxa-Tek 8500 the premium option for the longest interval. Only choose an acrylic if you specifically want the darkened, enhanced appearance, and if you do, use a solvent-based one like the Armor AR350 — water-based acrylics lift under hot tires.

The Three Types of Concrete Sealer

Penetrating Sealers — Silane, Siloxane, Silicate

These soak in and react with the free lime and silica in the concrete, lining the pore walls with a hydrophobic layer. Nothing remains on the surface. The appearance is unchanged, there is no film to wear through or peel, the surface keeps its original slip resistance, and the slab stays vapour-permeable so moisture from below can escape.

They are the correct default for driveways. The trade-off is that they do little at the surface: they resist water and salt superbly and oil only slightly, and they add no abrasion resistance.

A sub-note on densifiers — lithium, sodium, or potassium silicates. These react with the concrete to harden the surface and reduce dusting rather than repel water. They are excellent on garage and warehouse floors and are often used underneath a water repellent, but on their own they are not a driveway sealer.

Acrylic Sealers

Film-forming coatings that sit on the surface, darken and enrich the colour, and add gloss. They block oil, resist abrasion, and are the only way to get an enhanced appearance. They also wear unevenly in traffic lanes, can trap moisture and whiten, and eventually need stripping.

The critical division is carrier. Solvent-based acrylics partially penetrate before the film sets, which anchors them mechanically and makes them resistant to hot-tire pickup, and they recoat by re-dissolving into the existing film. Water-based acrylics sit on the surface, are low-odour and legal everywhere, and lift under hot tires — which rules them out for driveways in any warm climate.

Epoxy and Urethane

Thick, hard, chemically resistant coatings. Excellent on garage floors and in workshops. Wrong for exterior driveways: most are not UV-stable and will chalk and yellow, they are impermeable so freeze-thaw moisture has nowhere to go, and they are far more expensive than the alternatives.

TINTED / BLACK CONCRETE SEALER
Tinted Acrylic Sealer (black and charcoal)
⬛ Best for a Black Concrete Driveway
★★★☆☆
4.0
(1,150 reviews)

Black and charcoal driveways have become popular, and there are two routes to one: integral pigment added to the mix when the slab is poured, or a tinted sealer or stain applied afterward. This section is about the second.

A tinted acrylic sealer is the simplest retrofit. It applies like any acrylic and carries pigment that colours the slab while the film adds the usual gloss and oil resistance. Coverage and evenness are the challenge — pigmented films show lap marks and roller texture far more than clear ones, and application discipline matters much more.

The durability picture is honest: pigment in a surface film wears where the film wears, so traffic lanes lighten first and the driveway develops visible wear patterns within two or three years. Recoating restores it, but you are on a cycle.

A better-looking and longer-lasting alternative for most people is a penetrating concrete stain — acid or water-based — which colours the slab itself, followed by a clear sealer over the top. The colour then lives in the concrete rather than in a film, and only the clear sealer needs renewing.

One practical caution common to all dark driveways: black concrete absorbs substantially more solar heat, which raises surface temperatures considerably in summer and increases thermal movement at the joints.

✓ Pros

  • Simplest way to darken an existing slab
  • Adds the oil resistance and gloss of any acrylic
  • Dramatic appearance change for modest cost

✗ Cons

  • Pigment wears with the film — traffic lanes lighten first
  • Shows lap marks and roller texture readily
  • Two-to-three year recoat cycle
  • Dark slabs run much hotter in summer
  • A stain plus clear sealer usually looks better and lasts longer
Bottom line: Use a tinted sealer for a quick, reversible change. For a permanent black driveway, stain the concrete and seal it clear over the top.

Pros and Cons of Sealing a Concrete Driveway

The Case For

  • Salt scaling protection. The main argument in any climate where de-icers are used. Chloride-driven surface scaling is the most common way a concrete driveway is ruined, and it is largely preventable.
  • Freeze-thaw protection. Water absorbed into the surface and frozen expands about 9% and blows off the top layer. Cut the absorption and you cut the damage.
  • Stain resistance. Acrylic sealers block oil and transmission fluid; penetrating sealers help with water-borne staining.
  • Efflorescence reduction. Less water movement means less salt deposition at the surface.
  • Easier cleaning and reduced dusting.
  • Cost. Sealing a 600 sq ft driveway costs $80–$250 in materials against $4,800–$10,800 to replace the slab.

The Case Against

  • It is a recurring commitment. Every three to six years, indefinitely.
  • Acrylics reduce wet traction. A genuine safety issue on any slope. Penetrating sealers do not have this problem.
  • Film-forming sealers can trap moisture and whiten, and they eventually need stripping — a genuinely unpleasant job on a large area.
  • New concrete must cure first. Minimum 28 days, and many manufacturers specify 60 to 90 for a driveway.
  • Wrong-product risk. An impermeable coating on a slab over damp ground can cause the damage it was meant to prevent.
❄️ New Concrete and the First Winter: Do not apply de-icing salt to a concrete driveway in its first winter, sealed or not. Fresh concrete has not developed full surface strength and is at its most vulnerable to chloride scaling. Use sand for traction that first season.

How to Seal a Concrete Driveway

1. Clean Thoroughly

Degrease oil spots with a concrete degreaser and a stiff brush — sealer over an oil stain will not bond and will highlight the stain permanently. Remove rust, moss, and efflorescence. Pressure wash if needed and repair cracks with an appropriate crack filler.

2. Wait for a Genuinely Dry Slab

Forty-eight hours minimum after rain or washing, and longer for a thick slab or in humid weather. Tape a square of plastic sheet down for a few hours and check for condensation underneath if you are unsure. Damp concrete blocks penetration and, under an acrylic, causes whitening.

3. Check Conditions

Between 50°F and 85°F, no rain forecast for 24 hours, and not in direct hot sun. Early morning on a clear day, once dew has burned off, is ideal.

4. Apply Thin and Even

Penetrating sealers go on as a saturating flood coat with a low-pressure sprayer, with the second coat applied wet-on-wet a few minutes later. Acrylics go on thin with a 3/8-inch nap roller or sprayer, back-rolled, in sections, keeping a wet edge and applying the second coat perpendicular to the first once the first is dry to touch.

5. Cure Before Use

Foot traffic after 24 hours, vehicles after 48 to 72. Keep standing water off it for the first few days.

⚠️ Do Not Mix Sealer Families: An acrylic applied over a penetrating silane/siloxane will not bond — the treated surface repels it and the film peels within a season. If a penetrating sealer has been used, it must deplete fully before a film will stick, which can take years. Find out what is on the slab before you buy anything.

The Verdict

For nearly every concrete driveway, a penetrating silane/siloxane is the right sealer: Foundation Armor SX5000 for the best balance of performance and price, Ghostshield Siloxa-Tek 8500 where the longer interval justifies the premium. Both address the mechanism that actually destroys driveways — water carrying salt into the surface — without changing how the slab looks or how it grips underfoot.

Choose an acrylic only if the enhanced appearance is genuinely what you want, and then choose a solvent-based one. The AR350 is the safest bet, and the anti-slip additive is not optional on a slope.

For a black driveway, stain the concrete and seal it clear rather than relying on a pigmented film that will lighten in the traffic lanes within two or three years.

What are the pros and cons of sealing a concrete driveway?

In favour: it prevents chloride-driven salt scaling, which is the most common way a concrete driveway is ruined and is largely preventable; it cuts the water absorption that drives freeze-thaw surface damage; acrylics block oil and transmission fluid; it reduces efflorescence and dusting; and it is cheap insurance at $80–$250 in materials against $4,800–$10,800 to replace a 600 sq ft slab. Against: it is a recurring commitment every three to six years indefinitely; acrylic sealers reduce wet traction, which is a real hazard on a slope; film-forming sealers can trap moisture, whiten, and eventually need stripping; new concrete must cure at least 28 days and often 60–90 before sealing; and an impermeable coating over damp ground can cause the damage it was meant to prevent.

What is the difference between penetrating and acrylic concrete sealer?

Penetrating sealers — silane, siloxane, silicate — soak into the concrete and react with it, lining the pore walls with a water-repellent layer. Nothing stays on the surface, so the appearance is unchanged, there is no film to wear or peel, slip resistance is unaffected, and the slab stays vapour-permeable so moisture from below can escape. They resist water and salt superbly and oil barely at all. Acrylic sealers form a film on top that darkens and enriches the colour, adds gloss, blocks oil, and resists abrasion, but wears unevenly in traffic lanes, can trap moisture and whiten, reduces wet traction, and eventually needs stripping rather than recoating. For driveways, penetrating is the default and acrylic is the appearance choice.

Can I use a water-based acrylic sealer on my driveway?

Not if vehicles will park on it in a warm climate. Water-based acrylic films soften under a hot tire and lift away when the vehicle moves, leaving bare patches in exactly the spots you look at most — and the repair means stripping the whole area, not just the patches. In our testing, water-based acrylics showed clear hot-tire pickup by the end of the first summer, while the solvent-based Foundation Armor AR350 showed none across three summers with a car parked daily in the same spot. Solvent-based acrylics partially penetrate before the film sets, and that mechanical anchoring is what makes the difference. If VOC rules or odour make solvents impractical, use a penetrating sealer rather than a water-based acrylic.

How often should a concrete driveway be sealed?

Every four to six years with a mid-tier penetrating sealer like the Foundation Armor SX5000, and six to eight with a high-solids product like the Ghostshield 8500. Solvent-based acrylics run three to five years, and cheaper acrylics two to three. Climate drives the variation: a driveway that gets de-icing salt every winter depletes faster than one that does not, and full sun ages an acrylic film faster than shade. Rather than working to the calendar, watch the water — spray a section with a hose, and if water beads and sits, the sealer is working; if it soaks in and darkens the slab, it is time. Reapply before beading stops entirely rather than years after.

How do I make my concrete driveway black?

Two routes, and the better one is not the obvious one. A tinted or black acrylic sealer is the quick retrofit — it applies like any acrylic and colours the slab while adding gloss and oil resistance — but the pigment lives in the surface film, so traffic lanes lighten first and visible wear patterns appear within two or three years. Recoating restores it, but you are on a cycle, and pigmented films show lap marks and roller texture far more than clear ones. The better approach for a permanent result is to colour the concrete itself with an acid or water-based concrete stain, then apply a clear sealer over the top. The colour then lives in the slab, and only the clear coat needs renewing. Note that dark concrete runs considerably hotter in summer.

How long should new concrete cure before sealing?

Twenty-eight days is the standard minimum, and many manufacturers specify 60 to 90 days for a driveway. There are two reasons. Concrete continues to gain strength and to release moisture well past the point where it feels dry, and sealing too early — particularly with a film-forming acrylic — traps that moisture and causes whitening or a failed bond. Fresh concrete also releases efflorescence as it cures, and sealing over it locks the deposit in place. One related rule that matters more than the sealing schedule: do not apply de-icing salt to a concrete driveway in its first winter, sealed or not, because the surface has not developed full strength and is at its most vulnerable to chloride scaling. Use sand that first season.

Can I put a new sealer over an old one?

Only within the same family. Solvent-based acrylics recoat well over themselves — fresh sealer partially re-dissolves the existing film and fuses to it, so renewal is a wash and a recoat. Water-based acrylics do not re-dissolve and generally need stripping when they have worn unevenly. The combination that definitely fails is an acrylic over a penetrating silane/siloxane: the treated surface repels the film, which then peels within a season, and there is no way around it except waiting years for the penetrating sealer to deplete. Before buying anything, find out what is on the slab. If you do not know, a water-drop test tells you a lot — beading with no visible film usually means a penetrating sealer.
